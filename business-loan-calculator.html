Business loan costs: what the APR doesn't tell you

Business loan pricing is less transparent than consumer lending. A lender might quote you a "factor rate" of 1.3 on a $100,000 loan — which sounds like 30% total cost, and it is: you repay $130,000. But if the term is 18 months with daily payments, the effective APR is closer to 40–50%. Factor rates, origination fees, and repayment frequency all obscure the true cost in ways that consumer lending regulations don't allow.

The main loan types and what they actually cost

SBA 7(a) loans

The gold standard for small business borrowing. Rates are currently prime + 2.25–4.75% depending on loan size and maturity (roughly 9.75–13.25% as of mid-2026). Terms up to 25 years for real estate, 10 years for equipment, 7 years for working capital. The SBA guarantees 75–85% of the loan, which is why banks are willing to lend to businesses they'd otherwise reject. The downside: paperwork. Expect 30–90 days from application to funding, mountains of documentation, and a personal guarantee.

Conventional bank loans

If you have 2+ years of strong financials, collateral, and a relationship with a bank, conventional business loans offer rates similar to SBA loans without the SBA paperwork. But approval rates for small businesses at big banks are around 14%. Community banks and credit unions are significantly more approachable, with approval rates above 40%.

Online lenders (Kabbage, OnDeck, Bluevine, etc.)

Fast funding (1–3 days), minimal paperwork, but expensive. APRs typically range from 15–80% when you convert their quoted terms to true annual rates. These make sense only for short-term cash flow gaps where the revenue generated by having the capital exceeds the financing cost. Using a 40% APR online loan for a long-term equipment purchase is a bad trade.

Comparing $100,000 borrowed three ways

SBA 7(a), 10-year term, 11%: Monthly payment: $1,377. Total repaid: $165,240. Total interest: $65,240.
Bank term loan, 5-year, 10%: Monthly payment: $2,125. Total repaid: $127,480. Total interest: $27,480.
Online lender, 18-month, factor rate 1.3: Daily payment: $240. Total repaid: $130,000. Total cost: $30,000 — but the effective APR is ~42% because of the compressed timeline and daily payments.

What lenders evaluate

Business lenders look at different things than consumer lenders:

  • Revenue and cash flow: Can the business service the debt from operating income? Most lenders want a debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) above 1.25× — meaning net operating income is at least 125% of the annual debt payment.
  • Time in business: Under 2 years and you're limited to startups lenders (SBA microloans, CDFIs) or online lenders. Most banks want 3+ years.
  • Personal credit: Yes, they check your personal credit too. Below 680 limits your options. Below 600 limits you to expensive online options.
  • Collateral: SBA loans require all available collateral. Banks want specific assets pledged. Online lenders may take a UCC lien on all business assets.
  • Use of funds: "Working capital" is the hardest to get approved for. Equipment, real estate, and business acquisition have collateral backing and specific ROI stories — lenders prefer them.

The personal guarantee

Almost every small business loan — including SBA loans — requires a personal guarantee from any owner with 20%+ ownership. This means if the business can't repay, you personally owe the balance. Your house, savings, and personal assets are on the line. This is the trade-off for getting a business loan without an established corporate credit history.

Common questions

Can I get a business loan with no revenue yet?
Pre-revenue businesses can access SBA microloans (up to $50,000), grants, angel investment, or personal loans used for business purposes. Traditional lenders won't touch a business with no revenue. If you're pre-revenue, your personal credit, savings, and the strength of your business plan are what matter. SCORE.org offers free mentoring and can help you prepare loan applications.
SBA loans vs. line of credit — which is better?
Different tools for different needs. A term loan (SBA or otherwise) is for a specific purchase or investment: equipment, buildout, acquisition. A line of credit is for managing cash flow fluctuations — you draw when you need it and pay interest only on what you use. Most established businesses benefit from having both: a term loan for growth investments and a line of credit for seasonal cash gaps.
How do I improve my chances of approval?
Clean up your personal credit (above 700 helps significantly), show 2+ years of profitable tax returns, prepare a clear use-of-funds plan, offer collateral if available, and apply at a community bank or credit union that lends to your industry. Having an existing banking relationship matters — a bank where you keep your business checking account is more likely to lend to you than one that's never seen your cash flow.
