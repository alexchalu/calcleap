IRC §2039 Annuity and Pension Plan Inclusion in 2026: The §2039(a) Survivor-Annuity Rule, §2039(b) Proportion-of-Purchase-Price Attribution, §2039(c) ERISA Grandfather, and the §72/§691 Income-Tax Coordination

§2039 is the section that reaches every survivor benefit that flows out of a retirement plan, a commercial annuity, or a private annuity contract — every 401(k) balance, every IRA rollover, every defined-benefit pension survivor annuity, every joint-and-survivor commercial annuity, every intra-family private annuity with a term-certain feature. The inclusion is not the payment stream itself but the actuarial present value at date of death, valued under §7520 and Table B/K/S, then attributed under §2039(b)'s proportion-of-purchase-price rule that deems all employer contributions to be the decedent-employee's contributions. This is the 2026 walkthrough — including the §691 income-in-respect-of-a-decedent trap that transforms a §2039 inclusion into a double-tax exposure that reaches 40% estate tax and 37% ordinary income tax on the same dollar of survivor benefit.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 16, 2026 24 min read How we research

Every estate planner working an estate that includes an IRA, a 401(k), a defined-benefit pension, or a commercial annuity has to answer the same question: how much of the future payment stream comes back into the gross estate at date of death, and at what value?[1] The answer routes through IRC §2039 — the annuity-and-pension-plan inclusion rule — and the answer for practically every modern retirement-account balance is the full date-of-death fair market value, plus the actuarial present value of any survivor-annuity feature.[2]

§2039 is structurally different from the four Chapter 11 retained-interest pillars — §§2035, 2036, 2038, and 2042 — which we walked in the four-piece §2035/§2036/§2042/§2038 sequence closed on 2026-09-15.[3] The retained-interest pillars turn on what powers the decedent held over transferred property at death. §2039 turns on a narrower and more mechanical question: was the decedent receiving (or entitled to receive) a periodic payment stream that continues in some form after death to a designated survivor? If yes, the actuarial present value of the surviving payment stream is included in the gross estate — regardless of retained powers, regardless of when the underlying contract was purchased, regardless of whether the decedent had already begun receiving payments.[4]

This guide walks §2039 from the statute forward: the operative mechanics of §2039(a) (the general survivor-annuity inclusion rule), the §2039(b) proportion-of-purchase-price attribution formula (with the crucial rule that employer contributions to a qualified plan are deemed contributions of the employee), the DEFRA §525 repeal of the pre-1985 §2039(c) qualified-plan exclusion and the vestigial grandfather, the §7520 actuarial valuation mechanics using Table B/K/S factors, the overlap with §§2036/2038/2033/2042, the §691 income-in-respect-of-a-decedent coordination and the §691(c) partial offset, the §2056 marital-deduction analysis for joint-and-survivor spousal annuities under §2056(b)(7)(C), the private-annuity analysis with Rev. Rul. 69-74 valuation, and three worked case studies. Every dollar figure is the 2026 figure under OBBBA §70106 (Public Law 119-21, July 2025) and Rev. Proc. 2025-32.[5] When you're ready to size the numbers, our estate-tax calculator, retirement calculator, and compound-interest calculator handle the arithmetic side.

The 1954 origin — closing the pre-1954 annuity gap

The current §2039 dates from the Internal Revenue Code of 1954. Before 1954 there was no dedicated statutory rule for annuity inclusion in the gross estate. Survivor-annuity benefits were reached, if at all, through §811(c) of the 1939 Code (the predecessor to modern §2036) if the arrangement was structured as a transfer with a retained life interest, or through §811(a) (the predecessor to modern §2033) if the annuity contract itself was owned by the decedent at death.[6] The pre-1954 approach produced systematic under-inclusion of employer-provided pension survivor annuities — the employee had never transferred anything in the classical §2036 sense (the employer's contributions were the source of the corpus), and the employee did not own the annuity contract at death in the classical §2033 sense (the plan trustee owned the contract or the employer had promised the benefit contractually without a discrete contract).[7] The classical response — Congress's response — was to draft a dedicated inclusion rule that reached the economic reality of the arrangement without requiring either a §2036 transfer or a §2033 ownership finding.[1]

The 1954 Code enacted §2039 as a new dedicated section reaching "the value of an annuity or other payment receivable by any beneficiary by reason of surviving the decedent under any form of contract or agreement" other than insurance on the decedent's life, provided the decedent had a right to receive a payment during life for a period measured with reference to death.[1] The 1954 draft included the §2039(b) proportion-of-purchase-price attribution formula and a temporary §2039(c) partial exclusion for qualified pension survivor benefits — the exclusion that DEFRA §525 later repealed for post-1984 decedents.[8] The rule has been substantively unchanged since 1954 in its (a) and (b) subsections; the (c) subsection was repealed prospectively in 1984 and again narrowed by the Tax Reform Act of 1986 §1852.[8]

§2039(a) — the general survivor-annuity inclusion rule

The current text of §2039(a) provides that the value of the gross estate shall include the value of an annuity or other payment receivable by any beneficiary by reason of surviving the decedent under any form of contract or agreement entered into after March 3, 1931 (other than as insurance under policies on the life of the decedent), if, under such contract or agreement, an annuity or other payment was payable to the decedent, or the decedent possessed the right to receive such annuity or payment, either alone or in conjunction with another for the decedent's life or for any period not ascertainable without reference to the decedent's death or for any period which does not in fact end before the decedent's death.[1]

Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(a) implements the statute and adds the operational nuance that §2039 applies to the value of any payment receivable "under any form of contract or agreement" including arrangements between an employer and an employee (whether or not covering multiple employees).[9] The regulation makes explicit that a lump-sum death benefit paid to a designated beneficiary under an employer plan is reached by §2039 if the plan gave the employee any lifetime rights to receive payment — even if the specific lump-sum death benefit was itself contingent on the employee dying while employed. The Estate of Bahen v. United States, 305 F.2d 827 (Ct. Cl. 1962), Estate of Wadewitz v. Commissioner, 39 T.C. 925 (1963), and Estate of Fusz v. Commissioner, 46 T.C. 214 (1966) trilogy defines the outer edges of §2039's coverage — reaching deferred compensation and employer-provided death benefits where the decedent had a right to lifetime payments even if the specific death-benefit feature was contingent.[10]

What counts as "any form of contract or agreement" — the Bahen-Fusz-Wadewitz trilogy

Bahen involved a Bethlehem Steel deferred-compensation plan under which the decedent had a contractual right to receive $50,000 per year for life beginning at retirement, and his beneficiary had a right to receive continued payments for a period certain if the decedent died before retirement or during the payment period. The Court of Claims held that the survivor payments were included in the decedent's gross estate under §2039 even though the decedent had died before actually retiring and had never received a payment during life — the right to lifetime payments under the contract was enough to trigger §2039.[10] Wadewitz reached the same result for a Western Publishing deferred-compensation arrangement providing a death benefit and a competing retirement benefit under a single contract; the Tax Court held that the retirement benefit and the death benefit were sufficiently linked that they qualified as a single "contract or agreement" under §2039(a), and both benefits were pulled into the gross estate.[11]

Fusz reached the opposite result on facts where the death benefit was structured under a separate contract with no lifetime payment right, distinguishing Bahen and Wadewitz on the ground that §2039 requires a lifetime payment right under the same contract or agreement that produces the survivor benefit.[12] The Fusz distinction has been narrowed by subsequent cases and by Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(b)(1)(ii), which treats multiple related agreements between the same parties as a single agreement for §2039 purposes if the arrangement as a whole gave the decedent lifetime payment rights.[9] The doctrinal upshot: modern §2039 reaches essentially any employer-provided deferred-compensation or pension arrangement in which the employee had any lifetime payment right, regardless of how the death benefit is documented within the arrangement.

§2039(b) — the proportion-of-purchase-price attribution formula

§2039(b) governs how much of the survivor-annuity value is included in the gross estate when the annuity was funded partially by contributions of the decedent and partially by contributions of another party (typically an employer, but potentially a spouse in a jointly-purchased commercial annuity).[1] The statutory rule provides that only that portion of the value of the survivor annuity is included in the gross estate as is proportionate to the part of the purchase price of the annuity contributed by the decedent.[1]

Included Value = Full Actuarial Present Value × (Decedent's Contributions ÷ Total Contributions)

The crucial statutory deeming rule — the rule that transforms §2039(b) from a taxpayer-friendly attribution into a full-inclusion rule for practically every modern employer plan — is the second sentence of §2039(b): any contribution made by the employer or former employer of the decedent to the purchase price of the annuity or other payment shall be considered to be contributed by the decedent if made by reason of the decedent's employment.[1] The rule attributes 100% of the employer contribution to the employee for §2039(b) purposes, producing 100% inclusion of the survivor-annuity actuarial present value in a wholly-employer-funded pension plan.[13]

Key rule — employer contributions are deemed employee contributions

§2039(b) treats every dollar the employer contributed to a qualified retirement plan on the employee's behalf as if the employee had contributed it. The employee's own after-tax contributions, pre-tax elective deferrals under §401(k), and the employer's matching and non-matching contributions all count 100% toward the decedent-employee's contribution ratio. Except for a purely third-party-funded arrangement (a windfall bequest or a truly gratuitous survivor annuity from a stranger), the §2039(b) ratio is almost always 100% and the survivor-annuity value comes into the gross estate in full.

The 1971 Rev. Rul. 71-317 attribution examples

Rev. Rul. 71-317, 1971-2 C.B. 328, walked the §2039(b) attribution formula across four common fact patterns.[14] Example 1: a commercial single-life annuity purchased by the decedent alone for $100,000 with the decedent's after-tax funds — 100% attribution to the decedent, but no survivor benefit exists so no §2039 inclusion (the payment stream terminates at the decedent's death). Example 2: a joint-and-survivor commercial annuity purchased by the decedent alone for $150,000, payable to the decedent for life and thereafter to the decedent's spouse for the spouse's life — 100% attribution to the decedent under §2039(b), full actuarial present value of the surviving spouse's lifetime payment stream included under §2039(a). Example 3: a joint-and-survivor commercial annuity purchased jointly by the decedent and the decedent's spouse from community-property funds — 50% attribution to the decedent under community-property law, 50% actuarial present value included under §2039(a). Example 4: an employer-provided joint-and-survivor pension annuity fully funded by the employer over 30 years of the decedent's employment — 100% attribution to the decedent under §2039(b)'s employer-contribution deeming rule, full actuarial present value included under §2039(a).[14]

Joint-purchase edge cases

The classical joint-purchase case is one where two parties (typically spouses, or a parent and a child) purchase a joint-and-survivor commercial annuity together. Where the parties are spouses in a community-property state, the standard analysis under Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(c) treats each spouse as having contributed one-half regardless of which spouse wrote the check.[9] Where the parties are in a common-law state, the analysis turns on which spouse actually paid the premium; a spouse who paid the entire premium is deemed to have contributed 100% under §2039(b), producing 100% attribution and full inclusion of the surviving spouse's lifetime payment stream (typically offset by the §2056 marital deduction). Where the parties are unrelated (parent and adult child, for example) and the child paid the premium partially, the child's contribution reduces the parent's attribution correspondingly. The proportionate-consideration rule can produce partial inclusion in unrelated-party arrangements but rarely does so in intra-family arrangements because the intra-family payer is typically the party being inclusion-tested.[15]

§2039(c) — the DEFRA §525 repeal and the vestigial pre-1985 grandfather

Before 1985 §2039(c) provided a full estate-tax exclusion for the value of any annuity receivable by a beneficiary (other than the decedent's executor) under a qualified pension, profit-sharing, or stock-bonus plan described in §401(a), a §403(a) annuity plan, or a §403(b) tax-sheltered annuity, to the extent attributable to employer contributions.[16] Combined with the §2039(b) employer-contribution deeming rule, the pre-1985 §2039(c) exclusion produced a complete estate-tax exclusion for the entire actuarial present value of any employer-funded qualified-plan survivor annuity — a benefit that made the qualified retirement plan the most estate-tax-favored asset class in the U.S. transfer-tax system.[16]

Congress narrowed the exclusion in the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) §311(a) by capping the excluded amount at $100,000 for decedents dying after December 31, 1982, and repealed the exclusion prospectively in the Deficit Reduction Act of 1984 (DEFRA) §525 for decedents dying after December 31, 1984.[17] The Tax Reform Act of 1986 §1852 tightened the DEFRA grandfather clause and closed several transitional exceptions. The vestigial grandfather that remains in 2026: the pre-DEFRA §2039(c) exclusion still applies if the participant had separated from service before January 1, 1985, and had irrevocably elected the form of benefit before July 18, 1984, and if the participant's benefit was in pay status (or the participant was entitled to begin payments) at the time of death.[8]

In 2026 the grandfather is essentially obsolete. A participant who separated from service before 1985 and irrevocably elected a benefit form before July 18, 1984, would today be at least 62 years post-separation, meaning the participant would have to be at least 85 or 90 years old to have any grandfather-eligible benefit still running.[18] Estate planners should treat §2039(c) as historical unless the estate involves an extraordinarily long-lived pre-1985 pension arrangement. All modern qualified-plan survivor annuities are fully includible under §2039(a) plus §2039(b)'s employer-contribution attribution rule with no offsetting exclusion.

Actuarial valuation under §7520 and Table B/K/S

The §2039 inclusion amount is the actuarial present value at date of death of the survivor's right to receive the annuity payments, valued under the §7520 mechanics using the IRS's published actuarial tables.[19] §7520 was added by the Technical and Miscellaneous Revenue Act of 1988 §5031 to consolidate the valuation of annuities, life estates, remainders, and terms of years across the transfer-tax and income-tax provisions of the Code.[19] The §7520 rate — 120% of the applicable federal midterm rate rounded to the nearest two-tenths of one percent, published monthly by the IRS in a Revenue Ruling (currently under Rev. Rul. 2026-17, 2026-37 IRB, for September 2026) — controls the discount rate applied to the future payment stream.[19]

The §7520 valuation trade-off

Higher §7520 rates produce lower present values (future payments are discounted more heavily). Lower §7520 rates produce higher present values. A joint-and-survivor annuity paying $50,000 per year to a 65-year-old survivor for life has a present value roughly 20% higher at a 3.0% §7520 rate than at a 5.0% rate — the difference in gross estate inclusion is dollar-for-dollar. Practitioners often choose the month of death for the §7520 rate election under Treas. Reg. §20.7520-2 by comparing the month of death and the two preceding months (the election allows the executor to pick the most favorable of the three months for valuation of a §2039 annuity).

Table S, Table B, and Table K/R factors

Treas. Reg. §20.2031-7 (referencing Treas. Reg. §20.7520-1) provides the actuarial tables used for §2039 valuation.[20] Table S provides single-life annuity factors — the present value of $1 per year payable annually at the end of each year of the annuitant's life. Table B provides term-certain annuity factors — the present value of $1 per year payable annually at the end of each year for a fixed number of years. Table K/R provides joint-and-last-survivor factors — the present value of $1 per year payable until the last of two lives ends.[20] The tables are updated periodically to reflect current mortality experience; the current tables were promulgated in 2022 under Treasury Decision 9974 based on the 2010CM mortality table published by the Society of Actuaries.[21]

The month-of-death election under Treas. Reg. §20.7520-2

Treas. Reg. §20.7520-2(a)(2) provides that if a decedent dies during any month, the executor may elect to value a §2039 annuity, life estate, or remainder using the §7520 rate for the month of death, or for either of the two months preceding the month of death — whichever produces the lowest gross estate value.[20] The election is made on the first Form 706 filed by the executor and is irrevocable once made. In a rising-rate environment the executor typically elects the highest available §7520 rate to minimize inclusion; in a falling-rate environment the executor picks the lowest rate to maximize the §2056 marital-deduction offset if the survivor is a spouse.[20] The election cannot be split by asset class — the same §7520 rate applies to every §2039 annuity, every §2036 retained-life-estate, every §2032A special-use valuation, and every other §7520-valued item in the estate.

IRAs, 401(k)s, and other retirement-plan account balances

The most common §2039 fact pattern in a modern estate is not a defined-benefit pension survivor annuity — it is an IRA or 401(k) balance passing to a designated beneficiary at death. The technical section under which the inclusion occurs is contested — some practitioners treat IRAs as §2039 annuity contracts (arguing that the IRA is a "contract or agreement" under which the decedent had a lifetime right to receive payments during retirement), and other practitioners treat IRAs as §2033 owned property (arguing that the decedent owned the underlying account at death).[22] The practical result is the same: the full date-of-death fair market value of the IRA account, computed as the sum of all account balances including any accrued but unpaid earnings, is included in the gross estate.[22]

Rev. Rul. 84-105, 1984-2 C.B. 197, held that an IRA balance passing to a designated beneficiary at the account owner's death is includible in the decedent's gross estate — leaving open whether §2039 or §2033 is the correct inclusion vehicle but confirming the full inclusion.[23] The IRS has generally treated IRAs as §2039 annuity contracts for post-death income-tax purposes (see the discussion of IRD in the next section) and as §2033 owned property for gross-estate-valuation purposes (see the discussion of valuation immediately following).[23]

IRA valuation — the "cash-out" approach

Because IRAs are valued at fair market value under §2033 rather than at actuarial present value under §2039, the IRA valuation is simply the sum of the account balances at date of death (or, at the executor's election under §2032, six months after death for the alternate-valuation election). This is the "cash-out" approach — the IRA is valued as if the beneficiary immediately liquidated the account.[24] The alternative — valuing the IRA as an actuarial present value of the beneficiary's expected withdrawals over the 10-year window imposed by SECURE Act §401(a)(9)(H) — has been argued unsuccessfully by taxpayers seeking a reduced valuation; the IRS and the courts have consistently rejected the argument on the ground that the beneficiary's ability to immediately cash out at date of death defines the fair market value.[24]

The SECURE Act §401(a)(9)(H) 10-year distribution rule

The SECURE Act of 2019 §401 replaced the pre-2020 stretch-IRA rules with the modern 10-year distribution rule under §401(a)(9)(H).[25] A non-eligible-designated-beneficiary (non-EDB) must fully distribute the inherited IRA by December 31 of the tenth year following the account owner's year of death. Eligible designated beneficiaries (EDBs — surviving spouses, minor children of the account owner, disabled or chronically ill beneficiaries, and beneficiaries not more than 10 years younger than the account owner) retain a life-expectancy stretch under §401(a)(9)(B).[25] The 10-year rule does not affect the §2039/§2033 gross-estate inclusion — the IRA is included at full date-of-death value regardless of the beneficiary's distribution timeline. But the 10-year rule dramatically increases the §691 income-tax pressure on the inherited-IRA payment stream, because the beneficiary must recognize the full IRD balance as ordinary income over 10 years rather than over the beneficiary's remaining life expectancy.

§691 income-in-respect-of-a-decedent coordination

Survivor-annuity payments are the paradigmatic §691 income-in-respect-of-a-decedent (IRD) property. §691(a) provides that the amount of any item of gross income in respect of a decedent that would have been included in the decedent's income tax return but for the decedent's death shall, when received, be included in the gross income of the recipient.[26] The classical IRD items are (1) accrued but uncollected wages, (2) accrued but unpaid interest and dividends, (3) installment-sale income, (4) royalty payments, (5) survivor-annuity payments under §72, and (6) inherited-IRA and inherited-401(k) distributions.[26]

The IRD double-tax trap

A survivor-annuity payment stream is included in the decedent's gross estate at its actuarial present value under §2039 and taxed at up to 40% under §2001. Each subsequent annuity payment is then included in the surviving beneficiary's ordinary income under §72 and taxed at up to 37% under §1. The §691(c) partial deduction offsets only the estate-tax portion of the double tax; the timing and rate differential remain. State estate tax and state income tax overlay both federal exposures. In the aggregate, a §2039-included IRD item can face effective combined federal-plus-state tax rates in the mid-60% range for a large estate in a high-tax state.

§691(c) — the partial deduction for federal estate tax attributable to IRD

§691(c) provides a partial offset to the IRD double-tax exposure. The recipient of an IRD item may deduct an amount equal to the federal estate tax attributable to inclusion of the IRD item in the decedent's gross estate, spreading the offset across the periodic income recognition.[26] The deduction is available as a miscellaneous itemized deduction not subject to the 2% floor and is available in each year the recipient recognizes IRD income.[26] The mechanical calculation: (1) compute the total federal estate tax on the aggregate taxable estate; (2) compute what the federal estate tax would have been if all IRD items had been excluded from the gross estate; (3) the difference is the federal estate tax attributable to the IRD items in the aggregate; (4) each IRD recipient's proportionate share equals the recipient's IRD item value divided by the aggregate IRD item value, times the aggregate attributable estate tax; (5) each year's §691(c) deduction equals the recipient's proportionate share divided by the aggregate IRD amount, times the current year's IRD receipt.[27]

The absence of a §1014 basis step-up on IRD items

§1014(a) generally provides that the basis of property in the hands of a person acquiring the property from a decedent shall be its fair market value at the date of the decedent's death (or the §2032 alternate-valuation-date value if elected).[28] §1014(c), however, expressly excludes IRD items from the basis-step-up rule: "This section shall not apply to property which constitutes a right to receive an item of income in respect of a decedent under section 691."[28] The IRD carve-out is the single most important exception to the general §1014 step-up-at-death rule. An inherited IRA, an inherited pension survivor annuity, and an inherited private-annuity payment stream all carry the decedent's original cost basis into the beneficiary's hands — meaning the pre-tax character of the underlying deferred income is preserved and taxed as ordinary income to the beneficiary on receipt.[28]

§2056 marital-deduction analysis for joint-and-survivor spousal annuities

A joint-and-survivor commercial annuity or pension survivor annuity payable to the decedent's surviving spouse creates a §2056 marital-deduction analysis. §2056(a) provides an unlimited marital deduction for the value of any interest in property that passes from the decedent to the surviving spouse, but §2056(b) excludes "terminable interests" — interests that will terminate on the occurrence of an event or the lapse of a period — from the marital deduction if any interest in the property may pass to any person other than the surviving spouse.[29]

§2056(b)(7)(C) provides a specific rule for qualifying-income-interest-for-life annuities that resolves the terminable-interest problem for spousal survivor annuities. A joint-and-survivor annuity where only the decedent and the surviving spouse have the right to receive any payments during the surviving spouse's lifetime is automatically treated as qualified terminable interest property (QTIP) under §2056(b)(7)(C) unless the executor irrevocably elects otherwise on the Form 706 estate tax return.[30] The automatic QTIP treatment means:

  1. The full actuarial present value of the surviving spouse's payment stream is included in the decedent's gross estate under §2039(a).
  2. The same value is deductible from the taxable estate under §2056 as a marital-deduction QTIP interest.
  3. The surviving spouse recognizes each payment as ordinary income under §72 as it is received during the spouse's remaining lifetime.
  4. At the surviving spouse's death, §2044 pulls the then-remaining actuarial present value of the annuity payment stream back into the surviving spouse's gross estate as though the spouse had owned it directly.

The automatic QTIP treatment under §2056(b)(7)(C) avoids the classical terminable-interest disqualification while preserving the second-death inclusion under §2044. The executor's ability to opt out — by making an affirmative election on Form 706 — is exercised occasionally to avoid over-consuming the surviving spouse's §2010(c) exclusion in a case where the executor prefers to consume the deceased spouse's exclusion at the first death instead.[30]

Private annuities and intra-family transfers

The classical private annuity is the intra-family transaction where a parent transfers appreciated property (a family business interest, a real-estate parcel, a stock portfolio) to an adult child in exchange for the child's unsecured promise to pay the parent an annuity for life. The private annuity is a §2039 arrangement to the extent of any surviving beneficiary's right to continued payments after the parent's death.[31]

A pure single-life private annuity — payable to the parent for life with no continuation to any survivor — produces zero §2039 inclusion (the payment stream terminates at the parent's death and no survivor benefit exists). The underlying transferred property escapes the parent's gross estate under §2033 because the parent no longer owns it at death; the appreciation between the transfer date and the death date is captured only in the parent's basis carried into the child's hands under §1015 (with adjustments for the private-annuity-payment recharacterization).[32]

A joint-and-survivor private annuity — payable to the parent for life and thereafter to the parent's surviving spouse for the spouse's life — produces §2039 inclusion of the actuarial present value of the surviving spouse's payment stream, typically offset by the §2056 marital deduction under §2056(b)(7)(C) as discussed above. A term-certain private annuity — payable for a fixed number of years regardless of survival, with the balance of the term-certain period continuing to a designated beneficiary if the parent dies before the term expires — produces §2039 inclusion of the actuarial present value of the remaining term-certain payment stream, valued under Table B and discounted at the §7520 rate.[31]

The 2006 proposed regulations and their withdrawal

The IRS in 2006 proposed regulations (REG-141901-05, 71 Fed. Reg. 61441) that would have treated private annuities as taxable exchanges under §1001 — eliminating the classical §72 income-tax deferral that made the private annuity attractive as a wealth-transfer technique.[33] The proposed regulations were withdrawn in 2009 in response to industry criticism and are no longer under active consideration. The withdrawal restored the pre-2006 regime under which the classical private annuity retains its §72 deferral for the parent-transferor's payment stream, but signaled continuing IRS skepticism about the technique. In practice private annuities have become substantially less common since 2006 because the IRS's Rev. Proc. 2007-19, 2007-7 I.R.B. 515, announced the IRS's refusal to issue letter rulings on the classical technique — leaving practitioners without the assurance of pre-transaction confirmation.[33]

Overlap with §2036, §2038, §2033, and §2042

§2039 does not operate in isolation. A single retirement-plan or annuity arrangement can trigger multiple Chapter 11 sections, and the overlap analysis is critical to correct gross-estate valuation.[3]

Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(a) provides the coordinating rule: where §2039 and another Chapter 11 section both reach the same property, the section producing the larger inclusion controls, but the sections do not cumulate.[9] In practice this means that for an IRA the analysis produces the same result under §2033 or §2039 (full date-of-death fair market value), for a private annuity with a retained life estate the §2036 analysis usually controls (full transferred-property value), and for a commercial annuity with a retained power to change beneficiary the §2038 analysis usually controls (full property-subject-to-power value).

Three worked case studies

Case study 1 — $8M IRA rolled from a §401(k), 65-year-old surviving spouse

Decedent dies in 2026 with an $8,000,000 traditional IRA that was rolled from a §401(k) plan in 2015. Sole designated beneficiary is the decedent's 65-year-old surviving spouse. The IRA is included in the gross estate at the full $8,000,000 date-of-death fair market value under §2039 (as a survivor-annuity contract) or §2033 (as owned property) — the result is the same. The §2056 marital deduction offsets the $8,000,000 inclusion in full because the surviving spouse is a U.S. citizen and the IRA passes outright to the surviving spouse (rather than in trust). No cash federal estate tax at the first death. The surviving spouse elects to treat the IRA as the spouse's own under §408(d)(3)(C), stretching the required minimum distributions over the surviving spouse's own life expectancy. The IRD attribute is preserved — no §1014 basis step-up — and the surviving spouse will recognize each distribution as ordinary income under §72 and §408(d)(1) at up to 37% federal ordinary income tax rate. At the surviving spouse's death, the remaining balance is again included in the surviving spouse's gross estate under §2039/§2033 and offset by the surviving spouse's own §2010(c) exclusion of $15,000,000 (plus any DSUE portability from the first spouse's death).

Case study 2 — $250,000/year defined-benefit pension joint-and-survivor annuity, 60-year-old surviving spouse

Decedent dies in 2026 at age 62 with a fully-funded defined-benefit pension providing a joint-and-100%-survivor annuity of $250,000 per year, payable monthly. Surviving spouse is 60 years old. Under Table K/R and the September 2026 §7520 rate (assume 5.0% for illustration), the present value of a $250,000/year lifetime annuity payable to a 60-year-old is roughly $3,750,000 (life-annuity factor of approximately 15.0 for a 60-year-old at a 5.0% §7520 rate, times $250,000/year).[20] The $3,750,000 actuarial present value is included in the decedent's gross estate under §2039(a) with 100% attribution under §2039(b) (employer contributions deemed employee contributions). The §2056(b)(7)(C) automatic QTIP treatment offsets the $3,750,000 inclusion with a §2056 marital deduction; no cash federal estate tax at the first death. Each annual $250,000 payment to the surviving spouse is ordinary income to the spouse under §72 at up to 37% federal ordinary income tax rate. On the surviving spouse's later death, §2044 pulls the then-remaining actuarial present value of the annuity payment stream back into the surviving spouse's gross estate at the spouse's death-date actuarial present value.

Case study 3 — private annuity funded by $2M business-interest transfer, single-life plus 10-year term certain

Decedent transfers a $2,000,000 minority interest in a family LLC to an adult child in exchange for a private annuity paying the decedent $150,000 per year for the decedent's life, with a 10-year term-certain feature (payments continue to a designated beneficiary if the decedent dies before receiving 10 years' worth of payments). Decedent dies in year 4 of the annuity, having received 4 years × $150,000 = $600,000 of payments during life. The remaining 6 years of the term-certain feature (6 × $150,000 = $900,000 of future payments to the designated beneficiary) are the §2039 survivor-benefit inclusion item. Actuarial present value of the 6-year term-certain payment stream at a 5.0% §7520 rate (Table B factor of approximately 5.08 for a 6-year term): $150,000 × 5.08 = $762,000, included in the decedent's gross estate under §2039. The underlying transferred $2M LLC interest is not in the decedent's gross estate (the transfer was a completed §2033 transfer for full consideration under the private-annuity mechanics), but any residual retained life interest under §2036 would be separately analyzed. The designated survivor-beneficiary recognizes each of the remaining 6 annual $150,000 payments as ordinary income under §72 to the extent of the pre-death deferred income component.

Practitioner action checklist

Before filing Form 706 for any decedent with a retirement plan, commercial annuity, or private annuity arrangement, run this eight-item checklist:

  1. Inventory every annuity-like arrangement. Identify every IRA, 401(k), 403(b), defined-benefit pension, deferred-compensation plan, commercial annuity, and private annuity in which the decedent had any lifetime payment right. Include arrangements where the decedent had a right to future payments even if payments had not begun at death.
  2. Identify each survivor beneficiary and payment structure. For each arrangement, determine (a) who receives any post-death payments, (b) the form of payment (lump-sum, term-certain, single-life, joint-and-survivor), and (c) whether the decedent's spouse is the sole survivor (which typically produces a §2056(b)(7)(C) automatic QTIP result).
  3. Compute the §2039(b) attribution ratio. Determine the decedent's contribution ratio for each arrangement. For qualified plans and employer-provided arrangements, apply the §2039(b) employer-contribution deeming rule (100% attribution). For jointly-purchased commercial annuities in community-property states, apply the 50/50 attribution. For jointly-purchased annuities in common-law states, determine which party paid each premium.
  4. Choose the §7520 rate under Treas. Reg. §20.7520-2. For each §2039 annuity, compare the §7520 rate for the month of death and the two preceding months. Elect the rate that produces the lowest gross-estate inclusion (or the highest §2056 marital deduction if the spouse is the beneficiary). The election is irrevocable and applies to every §7520-valued item in the estate.
  5. Compute the actuarial present value under Table B/K/S. Apply the appropriate table (Table B for term-certain, Table S for single-life, Table K/R for joint-and-last-survivor) to compute the actuarial present value of each survivor benefit. Confirm the mortality table used (currently 2010CM under T.D. 9974).
  6. Coordinate with §§2036, 2038, 2033, and 2042. For each survivor-benefit inclusion, run the overlap analysis. Where multiple sections reach the same property, apply the section producing the larger inclusion under Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(a). Do not cumulate.
  7. Compute the §691(c) partial deduction. For each IRD item, compute the federal estate tax attributable to the item. The §691(c) deduction is available to each subsequent recipient of income under the arrangement and reduces the effective double-tax exposure.
  8. Plan the §2056(b)(7)(C) QTIP election (or opt-out) for spousal survivor annuities. By default, a spousal-only joint-and-survivor annuity is automatic QTIP. The executor may elect to opt out on Form 706 if consumption of the deceased spouse's §2010(c) exemption is preferred at the first death — a decision that turns on the relative sizes of the two spouses' estates and the DSUE portability posture.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2039 do in 2026?

IRC §2039 is the annuity-and-pension-plan inclusion rule in Chapter 11. It pulls into the decedent's gross estate the value of any annuity or other payment receivable by a beneficiary by reason of surviving the decedent, if the annuity or other payment was payable to the decedent (or the decedent possessed the right to receive it, alone or in conjunction with another, for life or for any period not ascertainable without reference to death or for any period that did not in fact end before death), under any form of contract or agreement other than as insurance on the decedent's life. §2039 reaches every survivor-annuity payment stream — commercial annuities, employer-provided defined-benefit pension survivor annuities, defined-contribution profit-sharing plan survivor balances, IRA and 401(k) balances payable to designated beneficiaries at death, and private annuity contracts. The inclusion amount is the actuarial present value at date of death computed under §7520 using Table B/K/S factors for the survivor's age.

How does §2039(b) proportion-of-purchase-price attribution work?

§2039(b) provides that only that portion of the survivor annuity is included that is proportionate to the part of the purchase price contributed by the decedent. The crucial deeming rule: employer contributions to a qualified retirement plan under §§401(a), 403(a), 403(b), and 408 are deemed contributed by the employee. For a §401(k) with 60% employee deferral and 40% employer match, 100% is attributed to the decedent-employee. For a community-property commercial annuity, 50% attributes to each spouse. For a wholly-employer-funded pension, 100% attributes to the employee. The formula: Included Amount = Full Actuarial Present Value × (Decedent's Contributions ÷ Total Contributions).

Is an IRA balance included in the decedent's gross estate under §2039?

Yes — practically every IRA balance is included at full date-of-death fair market value. Whether the technical inclusion is under §2039 (as an annuity contract for a period ascertainable only by reference to death) or under §2033 (as property owned at death) does not change the result. Traditional, Roth, SEP, SIMPLE, and inherited IRAs are all reached. The IRA becomes IRD under §691 — the beneficiary pays ordinary income tax on distributions and gets no §1014 basis step-up.

What is the §2039(c) pre-1985 ERISA grandfather and does it matter in 2026?

§2039(c) — as it stood before DEFRA §525 repealed the exclusion — provided a full estate-tax exclusion for qualified-plan survivor annuities to the extent attributable to employer contributions. DEFRA §525 repealed the exclusion for post-1984 decedents subject to a narrow grandfather for participants who separated from service before January 1, 1985, and irrevocably elected the benefit form before July 18, 1984. In 2026 the grandfather is essentially obsolete — such participants would be 85 or 90 years old. All modern qualified-plan survivor annuities are fully includible under §2039(a) plus §2039(b)'s employer-contribution attribution rule.

How does §2039 interact with §2036 and §2038 — the overlapping retained-interest sections?

Where §2039 and another Chapter 11 section both reach the same property, the section producing the larger inclusion controls under Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(a), but the sections do not cumulate. Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(a) treats §2039 as the exclusive inclusion vehicle for pure survivor-annuity arrangements. The Bahen (305 F.2d 827), Fusz (46 T.C. 214), and Wadewitz (39 T.C. 925) trilogy defines the outer edges of §2039's coverage.

What is the actuarial present value formula for a §2039 survivor annuity?

Present Value = Annual Payment × Life-Annuity Factor for the Survivor's Age × Section 7520 Rate Adjustment. The §7520 rate — 120% of the applicable federal midterm rate rounded to the nearest two-tenths of one percent, published monthly by the IRS — controls the discount rate. Higher §7520 rates produce lower present values; lower §7520 rates produce higher present values. Table S (single life), Table B (term certain), and Table K/R (joint-and-last-survivor) give the appropriate factors. Treas. Reg. §20.2039-1(c) provides the specific attribution mechanics. Valuation is at date of death regardless of whether the survivor has begun receiving payments.

How is a private annuity between family members treated under §2039?

A single-life private annuity terminating at the parent's death produces zero §2039 inclusion (no survivor benefit exists). A joint-and-survivor private annuity, or one with a term-certain feature extending beyond the parent's death, produces §2039 inclusion of the actuarial present value of the surviving payment stream. Rev. Rul. 69-74 and Rev. Proc. 2007-19 govern the valuation. The IRS's 2006 proposed regulations that would have treated private annuities as taxable exchanges were withdrawn in 2009 but signaled continuing IRS skepticism about the technique.

Does the §2056 marital deduction apply to a §2039-included joint-and-survivor spousal annuity?

Yes, subject to the terminable-interest rule under §2056(b). §2056(b)(7)(C) provides a specific rule for qualifying-income-interest-for-life annuities: a joint-and-survivor annuity where only the decedent and the surviving spouse have the right to receive any payments during the surviving spouse's lifetime is automatically treated as QTIP unless the executor irrevocably elects otherwise on Form 706. The survivor-annuity value is included under §2039, offset by the §2056 marital deduction, and then included at the surviving spouse's death under §2044 at the spouse's date-of-death actuarial present value.

How does §2039 coordinate with §691 income-in-respect-of-a-decedent?

Annuity and pension survivor benefits are paradigmatic §691 IRD property. §691(a) requires the recipient to include IRD in gross income when received. §691(c) provides a partial deduction equal to the federal estate tax attributable to inclusion of the IRD item — spreading the offset across the periodic income recognition. §1014(c) expressly excludes IRD items from the general §1014 basis-step-up rule. The result is the classical IRD double-tax exposure — up to 40% federal estate tax under §2001 plus up to 37% federal ordinary income tax under §1, partially offset by the §691(c) deduction.

How does §2039 interact with the $15 million §2010(c) basic exclusion amount under the OBBBA?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 2025) §70106 permanently set the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 for decedents dying after December 31, 2025, indexed for inflation. §2039 operates independently: the actuarial present value of any survivor annuity is included in the gross estate, then the $15M unified credit offsets the tentative tax. For estates near or above the threshold, a large IRA ($8M+) or a large joint-and-survivor pension ($5M+ actuarial present value) can transform a below-threshold estate into a taxable estate. The §2010(c)(4) DSUE portability preserves the deceased-spouse-unused-exemption; the §691(c) deduction offsets the ordinary-income double-tax exposure on subsequent annuity payments.

Conclusion — §2039 as the fifth Chapter 11 inclusion pillar

§2039 opens the fifth Chapter 11 inclusion-rule pillar beyond the four-pillar §§2035/2036/2038/2042 retained-interest foundation. The retained-interest pillars govern property that a decedent transferred during life while retaining control or economic benefit; §2039 governs periodic-payment arrangements — annuities and pensions — that were structured from the outset to continue in some form after the decedent's death. The two frameworks work together to reach the two economic realities of modern wealth: transferred property with retained strings, and contractually-committed payment streams with built-in survivor features.

For estates below the $15M §2010(c) threshold, §2039 inclusions produce increases in the taxable-estate line but no cash tax. For estates above the threshold, a large IRA or pension survivor annuity can be the single largest §2039 inclusion item, and the §691 IRD trap on subsequent annuity payments can be the single largest ordinary-income exposure the surviving beneficiary ever encounters. Careful planning — the §2056(b)(7)(C) automatic QTIP election, the §7520-rate month election under Treas. Reg. §20.7520-2, and the §691(c) partial-deduction claim on the surviving beneficiary's Form 1040 — reduces the aggregate exposure but does not eliminate it. The estate planner's job at every death is to inventory the survivor-benefit arrangements, run the §2039(b) attribution, choose the valuation month, and coordinate the §2056 marital-deduction and §691 IRD analyses across the decedent's return, the surviving spouse's return, and the individual beneficiaries' returns for years to come.
