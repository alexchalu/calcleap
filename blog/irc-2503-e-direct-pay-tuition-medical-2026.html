IRC §2503(e) Direct-Pay Tuition and Medical Exclusion in 2026: The Unlimited Gift-Tax Exclusion That Stacks on §2503(b), the §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) Institution Test, the §213(d) Medical-Care Boundary, and the Multi-Generational Playbook for Grandparents

The three-piece §529 arc that closed yesterday — §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging Monday, §529(c)(3)(E) SECURE 2.0 Roth-rollover Wednesday, §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change Thursday — walked the §529 mechanic end to end for practitioners. Each of those pieces flagged the same corner case at the edges of §529's operating room: what happens when §529 is exhausted, unavailable, or simply the wrong tool. For pre-K tuition (which §529 does not reach even after TCJA 2017 §11032). For international schools that lack Title-IV eligibility (which §529(e)(3) does not typically cover). For medical expenses that cross generations (which §529 does not cover at all). For high-net-worth donors whose §529 planning already caps out at the §529(c)(5)(A) $95,000-per-donor-per-beneficiary five-year front-load and who still want to move more capital toward a grandchild's tuition burden without touching the $15,000,000 OBBBA lifetime exemption. The answer to all four cases lies one section back in Chapter 12: IRC §2503(e), the unlimited direct-pay tuition and medical exclusion, added by the Deficit Reduction Act of 1984 §441(b)(1)(B) and unchanged in its core mechanic for four decades. This piece walks it in the same depth the §529 arc walked its own subject. CalcLeap Editorial Published August 29, 2026 24 min read How we research

The Deficit Reduction Act of 1984 added §2503(e) to the Internal Revenue Code as the "Exclusion for certain transfers for educational expenses or medical expenses."[1] The addition responded to a decades-old grievance from high-net-worth donors: the §2503(b) annual exclusion, which was $10,000 per donee in 1984, imposed a per-year ceiling on family support that had no relationship to the actual cost of a grandchild's private-school tuition or a family member's hospital bill. Congress determined that direct payment of another person's tuition or medical care — obligations the recipient would otherwise bear — was economically distinct from a cash gift subject to §2503(b), and enacted a separate unlimited exclusion for such direct payments. The mechanic has been stable ever since. It applies to any donor. It reaches any individual — no family-relationship test. It has no dollar cap. It does not consume the donor's lifetime unified gift/estate/GST exemption (currently $15,000,000 for 2026 under OBBBA Pub. L. 119-21). It does not require a Form 709 filing.[2]

Yet §2503(e) is the most underused tool in the practitioner's gift-tax kit. Most families implementing multi-generational planning default to §2503(b) $19,000-per-donee annual-exclusion gifts and §529(c)(5)(A) five-year-averaged §529 contributions. Both channels have real limits: the §2503(b) $19,000 caps the marginal transfer, and the §529(c)(5)(A) $95,000 per beneficiary is a five-year budget consumed by front-loading. §2503(e) has no such limit. A grandparent can pay $80,000 of a grandchild's private-school tuition, $75,000 of a granddaughter's medical school tuition, and $120,000 of a great-grandchild's hospital bill in the same tax year without any gift-tax consequence, without any Form 709 filing, and without any consumption of the $15,000,000 OBBBA lifetime exemption. That same grandparent, using §2503(b) alone, would burn 14.5 years of $19,000 annual-exclusion capacity or consume ~$256,000 of OBBBA exemption to deliver the same $275,000 of support. §2503(e) is the load-bearing tool for high-value tuition and medical support to descendants. This piece walks the mechanic in detail.

The §2503(e) statutory framework and legislative history

IRC §2503(e) contains only two paragraphs. The full statutory text as amended through 2026:[3]

IRC §2503(e) — Exclusion for certain transfers for educational expenses or medical expenses

(1) In general. Any qualified transfer shall not be treated as a transfer of property by gift for purposes of this chapter.

(2) Qualified transfer. For purposes of this subsection, the term "qualified transfer" means any amount paid on behalf of an individual — (A) as tuition to an educational organization described in section 170(b)(1)(A)(ii) for the education or training of such individual, or (B) to any person who provides medical care (as defined in section 213(d)) with respect to such individual as payment for such medical care.

The paragraph is deceptively brief. Every word carries operational weight. "On behalf of an individual" locates the beneficiary — the person whose tuition or care is paid. "As tuition" narrows the tuition exclusion to tuition proper (not fees, room and board, books, or supplies). "To an educational organization described in section 170(b)(1)(A)(ii)" incorporates a full statutory test by reference — the §170 charitable-deduction machinery for what counts as an educational organization. "To any person who provides medical care" — "any person," so both physicians and hospitals and insurers, and "provides medical care," which sends the reader to §213(d) for what qualifies. "For such medical care" locks the payment to the medical event, so post-event reimbursements do not qualify. Two sentences, and the exclusion is fully defined.

The 1984 legislative history documents Congress's dual purpose. First, to align the gift-tax treatment of direct educational and medical support with the economic reality that such payments discharge the recipient's own obligation — different in kind from a cash gift that the recipient could redirect. Second, to remove a distortion in the pre-1984 §2503(b) landscape, where a grandparent's direct payment of a grandchild's tuition was technically a gift subject to §2503(b), while the same payment by the grandchild's parent was not (support obligations satisfied under §262 support law were not gifts under Reg. §25.2503-3(b)). The 1984 change equalized the treatment across the family — anyone paying tuition or medical care directly on behalf of an individual gets the unlimited exclusion.[4]

The mechanic has been amended only twice since 1984, and both amendments were technical rather than substantive. The 1986 Tax Reform Act §1898(e)(1) clarified that §2503(e) applies to §529-predecessor state prepaid tuition plans as well as to direct institutional payments — a clarification that became moot in 1996 when §529 was federally established and moved to its own gift-tax regime under §529(c)(5). The 2003 Jobs and Growth Tax Relief Reconciliation Act cross-referenced §2503(e) into the §529(c)(5) rules to make explicit that §529 contributions and §2503(e) direct payments are separate channels. Since 2003, the statutory text has been stable.[5]

Treasury issued Reg. §25.2503-6 in 1985 (T.D. 8028) implementing §2503(e). The regulation is the operative interpretive authority. It defines "qualified transfer" as "any amount paid on behalf of a donee directly to a qualifying educational organization as tuition for such donee" or "any amount paid on behalf of a donee directly to any person who provides medical care (as defined in section 213(d)) with respect to such donee as payment for such medical care." The word "directly" in the regulation — nowhere present in the statute — is the source of the strict direct-payment rule that has shaped four decades of §2503(e) practice.[6]

The §2503(e)(2)(A) tuition test and the §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) institution requirement

§2503(e)(2)(A) has three moving parts: (1) an amount, paid (2) as tuition, (3) to an educational organization described in §170(b)(1)(A)(ii). Each part has its own boundary conditions.

What counts as "tuition"

"Tuition" is not defined in §2503(e) itself. Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(2) supplies the operational definition: tuition means the amount charged by a qualifying institution for the education or training of the donee, but does not include books, supplies, dormitory fees, board, or other similar expenses. The regulation follows the ordinary-language meaning of tuition: the fee charged by an educational institution for instruction. The narrowness is important. A single check written to a college for a full billed amount — say $80,000 covering $52,000 of tuition, $16,000 of room and board, $8,000 of meal plan, and $4,000 of activity fees — qualifies for §2503(e) treatment only as to the $52,000 tuition portion. The remaining $28,000 is a standard gift subject to §2503(b).[7]

Request an itemized invoice

Practitioners advising a §2503(e) tuition payment should always request an itemized invoice from the educational institution's bursar. The invoice should break out tuition separately from room and board, meal plan, activity fees, technology fees, and health-service fees. Only the tuition line qualifies for §2503(e). The rest may qualify for §2503(b) annual-exclusion treatment (up to $19,000/year to that donee for 2026), for §529 qualified distributions within the §529(e)(3) definition of qualified higher education expenses, or — if neither is available — for taxable-gift treatment consuming OBBBA lifetime exemption. The itemization is contemporaneous documentation that the donor's records will need in the event of examination.

The §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) institution definition

§170(b)(1)(A)(ii) is a definitional cross-reference from the charitable-deduction machinery. It defines an educational organization as one which "normally maintains a regular faculty and curriculum and normally has a regularly enrolled body of pupils or students in attendance at the place where its educational activities are regularly carried on."[8] Four elements:

  • Regular faculty. The institution has identifiable teaching staff engaged in instruction. Ad hoc lecture arrangements, guest-speaker series, and gig-based tutor rosters do not satisfy this.
  • Regular curriculum. A defined program of study with sequenced coursework or an organized instructional plan. Test-preparation "cram" programs generally lack this — they teach test-taking rather than a curriculum. Structured tutoring programs with defined learning objectives may satisfy it.
  • Regularly enrolled body of students. A defined student body with enrollment records, not a walk-in service or an audit-only arrangement.
  • Regular place of instruction. A physical or virtual campus where instruction happens. Fully online institutions do satisfy this if the online delivery is the institution's regular mode of instruction; sporadic online-course marketplaces without an institutional structure do not.

The international-school reach

§2503(e)(2)(A) has no U.S.-accreditation requirement. A payment to a Swiss boarding school, a British university, or an Australian graduate program qualifies for the exclusion provided the institution satisfies the §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) formal-instruction test. IRS PLR 200602002 confirmed the international-school reach: a grandparent's direct payment to a foreign boarding school for a grandchild's tuition qualified for §2503(e) treatment, notwithstanding the school's lack of U.S. federal accreditation or Title-IV eligibility.[9] This reach makes §2503(e) uniquely valuable for families with children attending non-U.S. institutions. §529 qualified higher education expenses under §529(e)(3) are more restrictive — many state 529 plans require the institution to be Title-IV-eligible for §529 distributions to qualify for tax-free treatment, and non-Title-IV foreign schools frequently do not qualify. §2503(e) has no such limitation.

The pre-K, K-12 grad-school, and foreign-school combinations

Three fact patterns most commonly exhaust the §529 mechanic and drop the practitioner into §2503(e). First, pre-K tuition: §529 does not permit qualified distributions for pre-K, regardless of TCJA 2017 §11032 or SECURE 2.0 §126. Only §2503(e) reaches pre-K tuition. Second, private K-12 tuition above $10,000/year: §529(c)(7) permits up to $10,000/year of §529 distributions for K-12 tuition, but a $75,000/year private-school bill exceeds that. §2503(e) covers the entire tuition amount. Third, graduate and professional school tuition: §529 covers this, but a grandchild's medical school at $90,000/year over four years ($360,000 total) may exceed the available §529 balance. §2503(e) supplements without limit. Fourth, foreign-school tuition: §529 may not qualify a non-Title-IV foreign school, but §2503(e) does under the §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) test.

The §2503(e)(2)(B) medical test and the §213(d) medical-care definition

§2503(e)(2)(B) permits a direct payment "to any person who provides medical care (as defined in section 213(d)) with respect to such individual as payment for such medical care." The parenthetical cross-references §213(d), the medical-expense-deduction definition. §213(d)(1) defines medical care to include four categories, and each category is available for §2503(e) purposes:[10]

The insurance-premium exception has practitioner value

Category (D) — direct payment of health-insurance premiums — is the most commonly overlooked §2503(e) fact pattern. A grandparent paying a grandchild's ACA-marketplace premium directly to the insurance carrier qualifies for §2503(e) treatment, regardless of the premium amount. The same reach covers long-term-care-insurance premiums paid on behalf of a parent, Medicare Part B and D premiums paid on behalf of an elderly parent, and Medigap supplemental-insurance premiums. The practitioner value: a single Medicare-supplement premium can run $3,000-$8,000/year, and Medicare-Part-D catastrophic-coverage-tier drug premiums for a chronically-ill parent can add another $6,000-$15,000/year. Absorbing these through §2503(e) preserves the annual-exclusion $19,000 for other family support and avoids consuming lifetime exemption on what is often a decades-long support commitment.[11]

The §213(d)(9) cosmetic-surgery exclusion

§213(d)(9) excludes "cosmetic surgery or other similar procedures" from medical care unless the procedure is necessary to ameliorate a deformity arising from or directly related to a congenital abnormality, a personal injury resulting from an accident or trauma, or a disfiguring disease.[12] The exclusion feeds through to §2503(e) — a direct payment for elective cosmetic surgery does NOT qualify for the §2503(e) exclusion, even if paid directly to the surgeon or hospital. A payment for reconstructive surgery following a burn or a mastectomy DOES qualify. The line between the two runs through medical necessity; documentation from the treating physician establishing medical necessity is the practitioner's protection in a §213(d)(9) boundary case.

Insurance-reimbursed medical care

Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(3) provides that a §2503(e) medical payment is qualified only to the extent the amount is not reimbursed by the donee's insurance. If a grandchild's hospital bill is $50,000, insurance pays $40,000, and the grandparent pays the remaining $10,000 balance directly to the hospital, only the $10,000 net payment qualifies for §2503(e). A payment of the full $50,000 by the grandparent, with a subsequent $40,000 insurance reimbursement flowing to the grandchild, converts $40,000 of the payment into a taxable gift by the grandparent (subject to §2503(b) annual exclusion, with any excess consuming OBBBA lifetime exemption).[13]

Coordinate with the insurance flow

Practitioners advising a §2503(e) medical payment should confirm the current insurance status and expected reimbursement before issuing the direct payment. Wait for the insurance adjudication where possible, so the direct payment covers only the net patient-responsibility portion. Where advance payment is required (deposits, non-participating providers, pre-authorization deposits), obtain a written agreement that any subsequent insurance reimbursement will flow to the payer (grandparent) rather than to the patient, so the mechanic remains a direct payment for medical care rather than a reimbursement of a taxable gift.

The direct-payment rule and the reimbursement trap

Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(2) requires payment "directly to a qualifying educational organization" and Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(3) requires medical payment "directly to any person who provides medical care." The word "directly" is not in the statute — it is a regulatory implementation of the §2503(e) mechanic. But it has been rigorously enforced in Treasury's audit practice since 1985.[14]

Three flavors of the direct-payment rule matter in practice:

  1. Payee identity. The check, wire, or ACH must be payable to the educational institution or medical provider — not to the student, the patient, a parent, a guardian, or a family trust. The payee-identity rule is strict. A check payable to "John Smith (or the University of Michigan)" does not qualify; the payee must be the institution alone.
  2. Payment timing. The payment must be made contemporaneously with the tuition or medical-care obligation. A payment made months before the enrollment or the medical service — a "deposit" for future tuition or care — is fine, provided the payment is applied to actual tuition or care within a reasonable time. A payment made months after the fact, characterized as reimbursement of the student's or patient's own earlier payment, does NOT qualify — it is a reimbursement, not a direct payment.
  3. No intermediation. A payment routed through the recipient (grandparent gives cash to student, student pays the college) does not qualify for §2503(e) treatment even if the paper trail is contemporaneous. The direct-payment rule requires that the transfer of funds bypass the recipient entirely — grandparent → institution — with no intermediate custody, endorsement, or reimbursement.

The reimbursement trap in practice

The most common §2503(e) failure mode in practice is the reimbursement scenario. A grandchild pays her medical school tuition of $70,000 out of a student loan or personal savings on the September enrollment deadline. Two weeks later, the grandparent transfers $70,000 to the grandchild "for tuition." Grandparent believes this qualifies for §2503(e) because the underlying purpose was tuition. It does not. The direct-payment rule requires that the grandparent's $70,000 go from the grandparent's account to the medical school's bursar — not to the grandchild. As executed, the $70,000 is a §2503(b) gift with a $19,000 annual-exclusion portion and a $51,000 taxable-gift portion that consumes OBBBA lifetime exemption.[15]

The workaround: prospective payment. If the grandparent expects to support the grandchild's tuition, the grandparent should establish a standing wire to the institution's bursar office, timed to arrive by the tuition due date. If time pressure forces immediate action, the grandparent should call the institution's bursar and pay by phone or online payment portal — nearly all U.S. institutions accept third-party tuition payments directly. A grandparent who has already made the reimbursement should NOT try to unwind it; the $70,000 gift stands and the practitioner should ensure the appropriate Form 709 filing to report the §2503(b) treatment of the first $19,000 and the taxable-gift treatment of the remaining $51,000.

The plan-administrator direct-billing workaround

For colleges and graduate schools that support "third-party sponsorship" arrangements — where a sponsor is billed directly for a student's tuition — the arrangement is a clean §2503(e) fit. The grandparent registers as an authorized payer on the student's account, the institution bills the grandparent directly for the tuition portion of the semester's charges, and the grandparent remits payment to the institution. No intermediation through the student. Nearly every accredited undergraduate and graduate institution supports third-party billing; contact the bursar's office to enroll.

Interaction with §2503(b), §529, HSAs, and MSAs

§2503(e) is a standalone exclusion — it does not consume, and is not consumed by, the §2503(b) annual exclusion, §529 contributions, HSA contributions, or MSA contributions. The four channels are independent and can be layered.

§2503(e) plus §2503(b) same-year layering

A donor may combine §2503(e) direct payments with §2503(b) annual-exclusion gifts to the same donee in the same tax year without limit on the §2503(e) portion.[16] Example: grandparent pays $80,000 directly to the grandchild's private high school for tuition (§2503(e), no limit, no Form 709), and separately gifts $19,000 in cash to the grandchild for personal use (§2503(b), full annual exclusion). Total 2026 transfer to the grandchild: $99,000. Exemption consumed: $0. Form 709 filing required: none. That same transfer via §2503(b) alone would consume ~$80,000 of OBBBA lifetime exemption and require a Form 709.

§2503(e) plus §529(c)(5)(A) five-year-averaged front-load

A donor may combine a §529(c)(5)(A) front-loaded contribution with §2503(e) direct-pay tuition to the same beneficiary in the same year. The two channels do not compete for annual-exclusion capacity — §529(c)(5)(A) consumes the §2503(b) $19,000-per-year budget (via the five-year averaging election), while §2503(e) is exempt from §2503(b) entirely. Example: grandparent pays $80,000 directly to a grandchild's medical school in 2026 (§2503(e)) and simultaneously contributes $95,000 to a §529 for the same grandchild with a §529(c)(5)(A) election (consuming the $19,000 §2503(b) exclusion for each of 2026 through 2030). Total 2026 transfer: $175,000. No taxable gift; no consumption of OBBBA lifetime exemption; only a Form 709 filing to memorialize the §529(c)(5)(A) election.[17]

§2503(e) vs HSA/MSA family contributions

A donor cannot use §2503(e) to fund another individual's HSA (Health Savings Account) or MSA (Medical Savings Account). HSA and MSA contributions are governed by §223 and §220 respectively; a third-party contribution to another individual's HSA is a §2503(b) gift subject to the $19,000 annual exclusion, not a §2503(e) qualified medical transfer. The distinction turns on the payee: HSA/MSA contributions go into an account controlled by the beneficiary, not to a medical provider. §2503(e) requires payment directly to the medical provider.[18] A grandparent supporting a grandchild's medical costs should pay the medical providers directly under §2503(e), not fund the grandchild's HSA — the direct-pay approach preserves annual-exclusion capacity and lifetime exemption.

Multi-generational planning and the OBBBA-preservation math

The OBBBA Pub. L. 119-21 signed July 4, 2025 set the permanent unified gift/estate/GST exemption at $15,000,000 per individual for 2026 (indexed for inflation), replacing the TCJA sunset. For a high-net-worth family, the multi-generational planning question is which channels move capital to descendants without consuming that $15,000,000 exemption. §2503(e) is uniquely powerful because it does not consume the exemption at all — every dollar routed through §2503(e) preserves an equal dollar of lifetime exemption for other transfers (taxable gifts of investment property, generation-skipping trust funding, testamentary bequests).[19]

The exemption-preservation calculation

Consider a grandparent supporting a grandchild through eight years of private school ($60,000/year), four years of undergraduate ($80,000/year), and three years of graduate school ($90,000/year). Total tuition support: $1,070,000. Two implementation paths:

Path A — §2503(b) annual-exclusion only: The grandparent gifts $19,000/year to the grandchild for 15 years, totaling $285,000 covered by annual exclusion. The remaining $785,000 of tuition support must be delivered as taxable gifts consuming $785,000 of OBBBA lifetime exemption. Post-support exemption remaining: $15,000,000 − $785,000 = $14,215,000. Form 709 required each year the payment exceeds $19,000.

Path B — §2503(e) direct-pay only: The grandparent pays $1,070,000 in tuition directly to the schools over 15 years. Annual exclusion used: $0. OBBBA exemption consumed: $0. Post-support exemption remaining: $15,000,000. Form 709 filings: none. The $19,000/year §2503(b) budget is fully preserved for other gifts to the grandchild — cash gifts, §529 contributions, gifts of appreciated property — totaling another $285,000 available across the same 15 years without gift-tax consequence.

Path B preserves $785,000 more OBBBA lifetime exemption than Path A, delivers the same $1,070,000 of tuition support, and eliminates 12+ Form 709 filings across the 15-year period. This is the load-bearing arithmetic of §2503(e) planning.

Multi-generation §2503(e) chains

§2503(e) has no beneficiary-relationship test — the recipient of tuition or medical support need not be a family member of the donor. This permits chained direct-pay support across multiple generations without any GST-tax analog to the §529(c)(5)(B) two-generations-below trigger. A great-grandparent may pay a great-grandchild's private-school tuition directly under §2503(e); the transfer is exemption-neutral for gift-tax purposes AND is not a Chapter 13 direct skip. The reason: §2612(c) defines "direct skip" as "a transfer subject to a tax imposed by Chapter 11 or Chapter 12 of an interest in property to a skip person." A §2503(e) qualified transfer is by statutory definition NOT subject to Chapter 12 gift-tax — §2503(e)(1) provides that "any qualified transfer shall not be treated as a transfer of property by gift." No gift-tax event, no direct-skip trigger, no GST consumption.[20]

§2503(e) is GST-exemption-neutral

This is the most-underappreciated feature of §2503(e) for high-net-worth multi-generational planning. Every other transfer channel — §2503(b) annual exclusion, §529 contribution, §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change to a two-or-more-generations-below family member, direct trust distributions — has either a gift-tax consequence or a GST-tax consequence when the transferee is a skip person. §2503(e) has neither. A great-grandparent's direct payment of a great-grandchild's medical school tuition, no matter how large, does not require GST-exemption allocation, does not consume the $15,000,000 OBBBA GST exemption, and does not appear on any Form 706 or Form 709. It is the only skip-person-neutral gift-tax exclusion in the Code.

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Elena (private-school grandparent, K-12 through undergraduate)

Elena, age 66, has a granddaughter Sofia (age 6) entering first grade at a private day school in Boston with $58,000/year tuition. Elena wants to support Sofia's full K-12 and undergraduate education. Sofia's parents earn well but do not have the cash flow to absorb the full $58,000/year on top of two other children's tuition. Elena's estate is $28,000,000; she is considering the interaction between §2503(e), §529, and her OBBBA lifetime exemption.

Elena's plan: Establish a §529 for Sofia in 2026 with a §529(c)(5)(A) five-year-averaged $95,000 front-load — consuming five years of §2503(b) annual-exclusion capacity through 2030 for §2503(b) purposes. Separately, pay Sofia's private-school tuition directly to the school each year under §2503(e). For K-12 years (Sofia age 6-17, 12 years), Elena pays $58,000/year directly to the school (assume 3% annual increases; total ~$820,000). For undergraduate years (Sofia age 18-21, 4 years at $85,000/year rising to $95,000/year at 3% inflation; total ~$380,000), Elena pays the college directly.

Total lifetime §2503(e) support: ~$1,200,000. OBBBA exemption consumed: $0. Form 709 filings: one in 2026 for the §529(c)(5)(A) election, none for the §2503(e) direct payments. Elena's post-support estate is smaller by ~$1,200,000 (removed from her taxable estate at no exemption cost) and her OBBBA $15,000,000 exemption remains fully available for other lifetime or testamentary transfers. The §529 balance ($95,000 growing at 6.5% for 12 years) becomes ~$202,000 by Sofia's college freshman year, available for §529(c)(3)(A) qualified distributions to cover room and board, books, technology fees, and any residual tuition after §2503(e) direct payments. After Sofia graduates, any §529 residual is available for §529(c)(3)(E) $35,000 Roth-rollover or a §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) change to a younger sibling.

The §529+§2503(e) layering is the archetypal high-net-worth setup: §529 for room-and-board and future flexibility (5-year budget), §2503(e) for the tuition line (unlimited, exemption-neutral).

Case 2: Marcus (international-medical grandparent, non-U.S. care)

Marcus, age 71, has a grandson David (age 24) who is completing an internship in Switzerland when he is diagnosed with a rare autoimmune condition requiring specialized treatment at a Zurich hospital. The Swiss hospital estimates $340,000 for the initial treatment course, with an additional $80,000/year expected for maintenance care over the following four years. David is enrolled in a marketplace ACA plan with limited international coverage; U.S. insurance will reimburse only $60,000 of the initial $340,000. Marcus wants to cover the uninsured $280,000 initial cost and the $80,000/year maintenance.

Marcus's plan: Pay the Swiss hospital directly under §2503(e)(2)(B). §213(d) medical care includes all treatment of disease regardless of the treatment location — no U.S.-provider requirement. Marcus wires $280,000 directly to the hospital's bank account (specifying the payment as tuition for David's treatment account). For the following four years, Marcus wires $80,000/year directly to the hospital or to Marcus's separately-established payment relationship with David's Swiss medical-care coordinator.

Total §2503(e) medical support: $280,000 + ($80,000 × 4) = $600,000 across five years. OBBBA exemption consumed: $0. Form 709 filings: none. Marcus's §2503(b) $19,000 annual exclusion remains fully available each year for other gifts to David (personal-support cash, gifts of investment property, contributions to David's Roth IRA if David has earned income). The Zurich hospital's foreign location does not affect the §2503(e) reach — §213(d) has no U.S.-only limitation and §2503(e)(2)(B) has no jurisdictional limit. The insurance-reimbursement coordination requires care: Marcus's payment covers only the uninsured portion; the $60,000 of insurance reimbursement flows to David and offsets David's insurance premiums or is retained as a cash reserve, not returned to Marcus.

Key mechanic: Marcus does NOT wire the money to David and let David pay the hospital — that would be a reimbursement gift subject to §2503(b) with the excess consuming OBBBA exemption. The direct-wire-to-hospital execution is the difference between exemption-neutral and $580,000-consuming.

Case 3: Diana (multi-generational chain with medical school and long-term care)

Diana, age 78, has three living generations of descendants: her son Robert (age 52), her granddaughter Priya (age 24), and her great-granddaughter Anaya (age 3). In 2026 Diana is coordinating three simultaneous support commitments: (a) Priya's medical school tuition at Columbia ($92,000/year, entering M1 in fall 2026, four years total ~$400,000 with 3% inflation), (b) Anaya's Montessori pre-K tuition at a Manhattan school ($42,000/year, three years total ~$132,000), and (c) Robert's mother-in-law's (Marion, age 88, Diana's contemporary) long-term care at a Manhattan skilled-nursing facility ($185,000/year, indeterminate horizon; Marion has three-year life expectancy per treating physician).

Diana's plan:

  • Priya's medical school: §2503(e)(2)(A) direct payment to Columbia's bursar for tuition portion each semester. Room and board (Priya lives in a Columbia-affiliated apartment for $28,000/year) is NOT §2503(e)-eligible; Diana funds this via a separate §2503(b) $19,000 annual-exclusion gift to Priya each year, with Priya covering the remaining $9,000 from part-time work.
  • Anaya's pre-K: §2503(e)(2)(A) direct payment to the Montessori school for tuition. Pre-K is not §529-eligible in any state as of 2026; §2503(e) is the only available channel. Diana pays $42,000/year directly for three years.
  • Marion's long-term care: §2503(e)(2)(B) direct payment to the skilled-nursing facility. §213(d)(1)(C) covers qualified long-term care services under §7702B(c). Diana pays $185,000/year directly to the facility. The recipient (Marion) is NOT a member of Diana's family — Marion is Robert's mother-in-law, i.e., no §529(e)(2)-style test applies. §2503(e) has no family test. Full exclusion.

Total 2026 §2503(e) support: $92,000 (Priya tuition) + $42,000 (Anaya pre-K) + $185,000 (Marion long-term care) = $319,000. Plus $19,000 §2503(b) to Priya for room and board. Total 2026 transfer: $338,000. OBBBA exemption consumed: $0. Form 709 filings: none. Diana's $15,000,000 exemption is fully preserved.

Multi-year cumulative through 2029: ~$400,000 (Priya medical school total) + ~$132,000 (Anaya three years pre-K) + ~$555,000 (Marion three years long-term care) = ~$1,087,000 of §2503(e) support. Plus ~$76,000 of §2503(b) annual-exclusion gifts across four donees. Total 2026-2029 transfer: ~$1,163,000. OBBBA exemption consumed: $0.

The three-channel simultaneous mechanic: Case 3 illustrates how §2503(e) supports simultaneous multi-generational planning across grandchild (medical school), great-grandchild (pre-K), and non-family (long-term care) recipients without exemption consumption. Every dollar of the $1,087,000 support preserves an equal dollar of Diana's OBBBA lifetime exemption for other planning (a testamentary bequest to a dynasty trust, a lifetime gift of appreciated stock to Robert, a GST-exemption-allocated transfer to a great-grandchild trust for Anaya).

State-tax considerations

Fewer than 10 states impose a state gift tax as of 2026 (Connecticut is the primary example; most states repealed their gift taxes decades ago). For those states with a gift tax, the §2503(e) exclusion is generally respected — state gift-tax exclusions typically incorporate federal §2503 by reference. Connecticut's gift tax, for example, incorporates §2503(e) verbatim under Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-643(3), so a direct-pay tuition or medical payment escapes both federal and Connecticut gift-tax treatment.[21]

State income-tax deductions for educational savings vehicles are distinct from the gift-tax analysis. A §2503(e) direct payment to a college does NOT qualify for a state's §529 tax deduction — the deduction requires a contribution to a state-plan §529 trust, not a direct payment to the institution. If the donor's state offers a §529 deduction (e.g., New York's $10,000/year for married-filing-jointly filers), the practitioner should ensure the §529 contribution channel is used for that state-tax benefit and layer §2503(e) direct pay for the excess. In such multi-state layering, the §529 contribution captures the state deduction while §2503(e) direct payment provides unlimited additional support.[22]

Six recurring §2503(e) mistakes

  1. Writing the check to the student instead of the institution. The single most common failure mode. A check payable to the student — even if the student immediately endorses it over to the college — does not qualify for §2503(e). The direct-payment rule under Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(2) is strict and formal. Always write the check to the institution.
  2. Reimbursing after the student pays. A student pays tuition from a student loan or personal savings; the grandparent reimburses the student. This is a §2503(b) gift, not a §2503(e) qualified transfer. If the grandparent knows tuition support will be provided, arrange prospective direct payment before the tuition due date.
  3. Treating room and board, books, or fees as §2503(e). §2503(e)(2)(A) covers tuition only. Room and board, books, supplies, technology fees, activity fees, and meal plans are NOT §2503(e) qualified. Only the tuition line on the itemized invoice qualifies; the rest is a separate §2503(b) gift or a §529 qualified distribution.
  4. Overlooking the insurance-reimbursement offset in medical payments. A §2503(e) medical payment qualifies only to the extent not reimbursed by insurance under Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(3). Wait for insurance adjudication where possible; pay only the net patient-responsibility portion; document the coordination.
  5. Assuming §2503(e) covers §529 contributions or HSA/MSA contributions. §2503(e) requires payment directly to a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) institution or a §213(d) medical provider. A §529 contribution goes to a state trust, an HSA contribution goes to the beneficiary's account. Neither qualifies. Layer §2503(e) alongside §529 or HSA channels, do not substitute.
  6. Failing to document the §2503(e) qualified-transfer treatment. Even though no Form 709 is required for §2503(e), the donor should retain (a) the institution's invoice showing the tuition line separately from other charges, (b) the cancelled check, wire confirmation, or ACH remittance advice showing the payment to the institution, (c) for medical care, the insurance-adjudication documents showing the net patient-responsibility amount, and (d) a contemporaneous note explaining the §2503(e) qualification. In an examination, this documentation establishes the qualified-transfer treatment retrospectively.

Eight-item §2503(e) pre-payment checklist

  1. Confirm the institution or provider qualifies. For tuition: does the institution satisfy §170(b)(1)(A)(ii)'s regular-faculty-and-curriculum test? For medical: is the provider furnishing §213(d) medical care? Ask the institution or provider to confirm in writing where the answer is not obvious (test-preparation, structured tutoring, elective cosmetic surgery, some MOOC arrangements).
  2. Request an itemized invoice. For tuition, insist on separation of tuition from room and board, books, fees, and other non-tuition charges. Pay only the tuition line under §2503(e); handle the rest separately.
  3. Confirm the payment routing. Wire, ACH, or check must be payable to the institution or provider — not to the student, patient, or intermediary. Third-party authorized-payer arrangements are the cleanest execution for repeated payments.
  4. Verify insurance reimbursement status (medical only). Wait for insurance adjudication so the direct payment covers only the net patient-responsibility portion. Where advance payment is unavoidable, document the coordination and ensure any subsequent insurance reimbursement flows to the payer.
  5. Confirm no reimbursement mechanic. If the recipient (student or patient) has already paid the institution or provider, a subsequent transfer to the recipient is a §2503(b) gift, not a §2503(e) qualified transfer. Do not attempt to convert reimbursements to §2503(e) after the fact.
  6. Layer §2503(e) with §2503(b) annual-exclusion and §529 as appropriate. The four channels (§2503(e), §2503(b), §529, HSA/MSA) are independent and can be combined. Use §2503(e) for direct tuition and medical; use §2503(b) for the remaining $19,000 per donee; use §529 for future-year budgeting and state-tax deduction; use HSA/MSA where the beneficiary is contribution-eligible.
  7. Document contemporaneously. Retain the institution's invoice, the payment record (cancelled check, wire confirmation, ACH remittance), for medical care the insurance-adjudication documents, and a brief note explaining the §2503(e) qualification. No Form 709 is required, but examination documentation should be complete.
  8. Coordinate multi-generational planning across §2503(e), §529, and dynasty-trust vehicles. §2503(e) preserves lifetime and GST exemption for other transfers. In multi-generational planning, use §2503(e) first for the tuition and medical line; use annual-exclusion and §529 for other current-year budget; reserve OBBBA lifetime exemption for larger discrete transfers (dynasty trust funding, gifts of appreciated property, testamentary bequests). The exemption-preservation math tilts heavily toward maximizing §2503(e) direct-pay when tuition and medical are on the family agenda.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2503(e) permit?

An unlimited gift-tax exclusion for two types of qualified transfers: (1) tuition paid directly to a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational organization for the education of any individual, and (2) medical expenses paid directly to a §213(d) medical-care provider for the medical care of any individual. The exclusion is uncapped, does not consume the donor's OBBBA lifetime exemption, requires no Form 709 filing, and applies without regard to family relationship.

Is the §2503(e) exclusion in addition to the §2503(b) annual exclusion?

Yes. §2503(e) is fully additive to §2503(b). A donor may pay $80,000 in tuition directly under §2503(e) and separately gift $19,000 under §2503(b) to the same donee in the same year — total $99,000, zero exemption consumed, no Form 709. Treasury Reg. §25.2503-6(a) confirms the additive treatment.

What is a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational organization?

An organization that "normally maintains a regular faculty and curriculum and normally has a regularly enrolled body of pupils or students in attendance at the place where its educational activities are regularly carried on." The test is deliberately broad — public and private K-12 schools, undergraduate and graduate colleges (including foreign non-Title-IV institutions), professional schools, technical/trade schools with formal curricula, and accredited online institutions all qualify. MOOC platforms, test-preparation centers, and informal tutoring do not qualify.

Does §2503(e) cover pre-kindergarten tuition?

Yes, provided the pre-K program satisfies the §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) formal-instruction test. Accredited Montessori, Waldorf, religious, and secular pre-K programs with a regular curriculum, faculty, and enrolled students qualify. §2503(e) is uniquely valuable for pre-K because §529 does not permit qualified distributions for pre-K expenses, even after TCJA 2017 §11032 or SECURE 2.0 §126.

What counts as medical care under §213(d) for §2503(e) purposes?

All four §213(d) categories: (A) diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, or affecting the structure/function of the body; (B) transportation essential to medical care; (C) qualified long-term care services under §7702B(c); and (D) insurance covering (A) or (C) care. This reaches physician fees, hospital charges, prescription drugs, mental-health treatment, dental/vision, long-term care, and health-insurance and long-term-care-insurance premiums. Cosmetic surgery under §213(d)(9) is excluded unless medically necessary.

Can a §2503(e) payment cover room and board or books?

No. §2503(e)(2)(A) is limited to tuition. Room and board, books, supplies, and technology fees are NOT §2503(e) qualified even if paid directly to the institution. Request an itemized invoice separating tuition from other charges; pay only the tuition portion under §2503(e); handle the rest separately via §2503(b) annual exclusion or §529 qualified distribution.

Does §2503(e) cover contributions to a §529 plan?

No. §529 contributions go to a state-sponsored trust and are governed by §529(c)(2)(A) and §529(c)(5)(A), not by §2503(e). §529 contributions consume the §2503(b) $19,000 annual exclusion (or use §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging up to $95,000). Layer §2503(e) direct-pay tuition alongside §529 contributions rather than substituting.

What happens if the check is written to the student instead of the institution?

The §2503(e) exclusion is lost. Reg. §25.2503-6(b)(2) requires payment directly to the institution or medical provider. A check payable to the student — even one endorsed over to the college — becomes a §2503(b) gift with any excess over $19,000 consuming OBBBA lifetime exemption. Always write directly to the institution's bursar; nearly every college supports third-party direct billing.

Does the recipient have to be a family member?

No. §2503(e) has no family-relationship requirement. A donor may pay tuition or medical expenses directly on behalf of any individual — grandchild, friend's child, scholarship recipient, employee, or stranger — and the exclusion applies. This distinguishes §2503(e) from §529(e)(2), which requires a member-of-family test for tax-free beneficiary changes.

How does §2503(e) interact with the OBBBA lifetime exemption?

§2503(e) transfers are exemption-neutral — they do not consume any portion of the $15,000,000 OBBBA lifetime gift/estate exemption (2026) and appear on no Form 709. For high-net-worth donors, this makes §2503(e) the most efficient tool for moving capital toward descendants through tuition and medical support without eroding the exemption available for larger discrete transfers (dynasty trust funding, gifts of appreciated property, testamentary bequests).

Applies to Connecticut donors and to gifts of Connecticut real property regardless of donor residence. cga.ct.gov/current/pub/chap_228c.htm
  22. Internal Revenue Code §529(c)(2)(A) — Contribution treatment — governs the gift-tax treatment of §529 contributions, which are separate from §2503(e) qualified transfers. State §529 income-tax deductions apply only to contributions to a state §529 plan, not to §2503(e) direct payments. law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/529#c_2_A

The §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) institution test and §213(d) medical-care definition have subtle boundary conditions (test-preparation, cosmetic surgery, elective procedures, foreign schools, non-Title-IV institutions); consult primary sources or IRS private letter rulings before treating a payment as §2503(e)-qualifying in a boundary case. Multi-generational planning combining §2503(e), §2503(b), §529, and dynasty-trust vehicles should be coordinated across the family's estate-planning framework; the exemption-preservation math is powerful but requires attention to Form 709 filing coordination for the §529(c)(5)(A) election, insurance-reimbursement documentation for the §2503(e) medical-care payments, and state-law variation (Connecticut, in particular, has state-level gift-tax exposure for large non-§2503(e) transfers). Consult a state-licensed tax advisor or CPA experienced in Chapter 12 gift-tax planning before executing any material §2503(e) qualified transfer, and confirm the institution's or provider's third-party-payer documentation requirements in advance.
