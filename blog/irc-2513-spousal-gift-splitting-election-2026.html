IRC §2513 Spousal Gift-Splitting Election in 2026: The 1948 Revenue Act Mechanic, the Form 709 Signature Requirement, the $38,000 Doubled Annual Exclusion, the $190,000 §529(c)(5)(A) Married-Couple Front-Load, and the §2652(a)(2) GST Coordination

Yesterday's IRC §2503(e) direct-pay tuition and medical piece walked the one gift-tax channel that is fully exemption-neutral by statutory design — a channel that, by not being a gift at all for Chapter 12 purposes, has no need for any spousal-splitting mechanic. Every other married-couple gift-tax planning move does. IRC §2513, enacted by §371 of the Revenue Act of 1948 to level the treatment between common-law-property and community-property jurisdictions, is the load-bearing coordination mechanic across the entire annual-exclusion, §529, and GST landscape for married couples: elect it on Form 709, and every gift by one spouse to a third party for that calendar year is treated as made half by each spouse. The doubling reaches four places at once — the §2503(b) annual exclusion goes from $19,000 to $38,000 per donee for 2026, the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year-averaged front-load goes from $95,000 to $190,000 per §529 beneficiary, the §2010(c) lifetime-exemption footprint effectively doubles (each spouse's separate $15,000,000 OBBBA exemption is drawn on for half the transfer), and — via the §2652(a)(2) cross-reference — the GST-exemption footprint of a direct skip is halved on each spouse's side. This piece walks the mechanic in the same depth the §2503(e) direct-pay piece walked its own subject. Coverage: the §2513(a) eligibility test with the both-spouses-U.S.-citizen-or-resident bar and the no-remarriage rule; the §2513(b) Form 709 signature-and-timing mechanic with the April 15 revocation window; the §2503(b) doubling arithmetic; the §529(c)(5)(A) doubled front-load with the five-year budget interaction; the §2652(a)(2) GST split; the §2523(i) noncitizen-spouse coordination; three worked case studies covering the $190,000 grandparent front-load, the divorce-mid-year timing trap, and the death-of-spouse execution; and an eight-item pre-election checklist.

The Revenue Act of 1948, Pub. L. 80-471, §371(a), added §1000(f) of the 1939 Internal Revenue Code — the direct predecessor of what became §2513 in the 1954 recodification — to solve a two-decade grievance among common-law-state taxpayers.[1] Before 1948, community-property-state spouses had an automatic gift-tax advantage that common-law-state spouses lacked: a $20,000 gift made by a husband in California from community-property funds was, by state law, deemed half owned by his wife, and each spouse was treated as the donor of $10,000, so both halves qualified for the (then-$3,000) §1003(b) annual exclusion. The same $20,000 gift by a husband in Illinois from separate-property funds was a full $20,000 gift from him alone — only one $3,000 annual exclusion applied. The disparity had grown politically untenable after two decades of §1003 practice, and Congress corrected it by enacting a federal election that lets any married couple treat any gift by one spouse to a third party as made half by each. The 1948 change equalized common-law and community-property gift-tax treatment across the country.[2]

The mechanic has been stable in its core structure ever since — §2513 was re-numbered but not substantively amended in the 1954 recodification, and the only material subsequent amendments have been the 1976 addition of §2513(b) formalizing the Form 709 signature requirement and the 1986 addition of §2652(a)(2) coordinating the split-gift election with the newly-enacted Chapter 13 GST tax.[3] The 2026 practitioner working under §2513 works with substantially the same statute enacted in 1948, applied against the 2026 $19,000 §2503(b) annual exclusion (per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32) and the $15,000,000 OBBBA-permanent lifetime unified gift/estate/GST exemption (Pub. L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025).[4] The doubling arithmetic is the same regardless of the base numbers: elect §2513, and the couple's per-donee annual exclusion is $38,000 (2 × $19,000), the couple's §529(c)(5)(A) five-year front-load ceiling per beneficiary is $190,000 (2 × $95,000), and each spouse's $15,000,000 OBBBA exemption is drawn on for half of any larger transfer.

The §2513 statutory framework and 1948 legislative history

IRC §2513 contains two subsections. The full statutory text as amended through 2026:[5]

IRC §2513 — Gifts by husband or wife to third party

(a)(1) Considered as made one-half by each. A gift made by one spouse to any person other than his spouse shall, for the purposes of this chapter, be considered as made one-half by him and one-half by his spouse, but only if at the time of the gift each spouse is a citizen or resident of the United States. This paragraph shall not apply with respect to a gift by a spouse of an interest in property if he creates in his spouse a general power of appointment, as defined in section 2514(c), over such interest.

(a)(2) Consent of both spouses. Paragraph (1) shall apply only if both spouses have signified (under the regulations provided for in subsection (b)) their consent to the application of paragraph (1) in the case of all such gifts made during the calendar year by either while married to the other.

(b) Manner and time of signifying consent. (1) Consent may be signified at any time after the close of the calendar year in which the gift was made, subject to the following limitations — (A) the consent may not be signified after the 15th day of April following the close of such year, unless before such 15th day no return has been filed for such year by either spouse, in which case the consent may not be signified after a return for such year is filed by either spouse; (B) the consent may not be signified after a notice of deficiency with respect to the tax for such year has been sent to either spouse in accordance with section 6212(a). (2) Revocation of consent. Consent may be revoked at any time on or before the 15th day of April following the close of the calendar year in which the gift was made.

Every clause carries operational weight. "A gift made by one spouse to any person other than his spouse" locates the scope — third-party gifts only, not interspousal transfers (which are covered by the unlimited §2523 marital deduction for citizen spouses and the §2523(i) $185,000 annual exclusion for noncitizen-spouse gifts). "Considered as made one-half by him and one-half by his spouse" is the reallocation itself — a legal fiction that treats the gift as jointly-sourced for gift-tax and (via §2652(a)(2)) GST-tax purposes, even though the actual dollars came from one spouse's separate account. "At the time of the gift each spouse is a citizen or resident of the United States" is the U.S.-nexus test — either spouse a nonresident noncitizen at the gift date disqualifies. "In the case of all such gifts made during the calendar year" is the all-or-nothing rule — §2513 is not gift-by-gift; a couple that elects for one gift elects for every gift either spouse made to third parties during the year.[6]

The 1948 legislative history makes the equalization purpose explicit. Both House Report No. 1274 and Senate Report No. 1013 accompanying the Revenue Act of 1948 described §371 as "elimination of tax disparity between residents of common-law and community-property jurisdictions with respect to gifts to third parties." Congress paired the §371 gift-splitting election with the §303 income-splitting election (which became the joint-return mechanic still central to §6013 filing today) and the §361 estate-splitting election (which became the §2056 marital deduction). Together, the three 1948 elections eliminated the federal-tax penalty for common-law-state residence that had persisted since the 1930s enactment of the modern income, gift, and estate taxes.[7]

Treasury issued Reg. §25.2513-1 in 1958 (T.D. 6334) implementing the recodified §2513, with subsequent minor amendments in 1975 (T.D. 7378) and 1988 (T.D. 8187) reflecting the §2513(b) codification and the §2652(a)(2) GST coordination. Reg. §25.2513-1(a) confirms the all-or-nothing rule: "if the spouses consent, the gift-splitting provisions apply to all gifts made by each spouse to third parties during the calendar year." Reg. §25.2513-1(b)(1)-(3) enumerates the eligibility conditions (citizens or residents throughout the year, married at the time of gift, not remarried before year-end). Reg. §25.2513-2 governs the Form 709 signature mechanics. Reg. §25.2513-3 governs the revocation window. Reg. §25.2513-4 addresses the coordination with §2514 general-power-of-appointment transfers.[8]

The both-spouses-U.S.-nexus bar

§2513(a)(1)'s U.S. citizen or resident requirement applies to both spouses, at the time of each gift AND (per Reg. §25.2513-1(b)(1)) throughout the entire calendar year in which the gift is made. This is stricter than the §2523(i) noncitizen-spouse marital-deduction rule, which merely limits the deduction available for interspousal transfers to a noncitizen spouse rather than disqualifying gifts to third parties. §2513 does not have a partial election — either both spouses satisfy the U.S. nexus test for the whole year or no election is available at all.[9]

Practical implications: A U.S.-citizen spouse married to a U.S.-resident-alien green-card holder who surrenders the green card on July 15, 2026 to relocate abroad cannot use §2513 for any 2026 gift, including gifts made while both were fully U.S.-tax residents in January through June. A U.S.-citizen expatriating spouse under §877A cannot use §2513 for the year of expatriation. A U.S.-citizen spouse married to a nonresident-alien spouse can never use §2513 for gifts to third parties (though the §2523(i) $185,000/year exclusion for gifts TO the noncitizen spouse remains available). Mixed-status international families frequently discover the §2513 bar only when the practitioner requests spouse consent on Form 709 — audit early.

The no-remarriage rule under §2513(a)(2)

§2513(a)(2) contains the "no-remarriage" rule that most first-time §2513 filers miss: gift-splitting applies only for gifts made "while married to the other" and only if neither spouse "remarries before the end of the calendar year." A couple that divorces on June 30, 2026 may elect §2513 for gifts made through June 30, 2026 — but only if neither ex-spouse remarries a different person during the remainder of 2026. If the divorcing husband remarries in October 2026, no §2513 election is available for ANY gift made in 2026 by either party, even the gifts made in April while the marriage was intact. The no-remarriage rule is a poison pill that reaches backward.[10]

Time the divorce-year gift execution carefully

Couples contemplating divorce during a year in which they intend to make gift-split transfers should: (1) execute the intended gift-split transfers before the divorce decree is entered; (2) confirm in writing between the ex-spouses that neither will remarry a different person before December 31 of the divorce year; (3) if a subsequent-year remarriage is planned, defer the wedding until at least January 1 of the year following the divorce; (4) if a same-year remarriage is unavoidable, treat the year as a sole-donor gift year and plan the transfers within one spouse's own §2503(b) annual exclusion and lifetime exemption. The gift-split arithmetic is powerful, but the no-remarriage rule is unforgiving of practitioner inattention.

The §2513(b) consent mechanics: Form 709, signatures, timing, revocation

Reg. §25.2513-2 governs the execution of §2513 consent. The consent must be evidenced by both spouses' signatures on Form 709, United States Gift (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return, for the calendar year in which the gifts were made. If only one spouse made gifts to third parties during the year (below the annual-exclusion threshold does NOT excuse filing when §2513 is elected), only that spouse files Form 709; the non-donor spouse signs the "Consent of Spouse" block on Part 1, Line 18 of the donor's Form 709. If both spouses made gifts during the year, each files a separate Form 709 but both filings must reference the same §2513 election, and each spouse must sign the consent block on the other spouse's Form 709.[11]

The April 15 filing and revocation deadline

§2513(b)(1)(A) sets the consent deadline: no later than April 15 of the year following the gift year. §6075(b)(1) sets the Form 709 filing deadline: also April 15 of the year following the gift year. A §6081 six-month extension is available: an extension of time to file the donor's individual income-tax return under §6081 (Form 4868) automatically extends the Form 709 filing deadline by the same six months; alternatively, a stand-alone Form 8892 extends only the Form 709 deadline. Either extension carries the §2513 election deadline with it — the election need not be made by April 15 if a valid extension is in effect, provided both spouses sign by the extended deadline.[12]

The revocation window under §2513(b)(2) is narrower than the filing window. Consent may be revoked only through April 15 of the year following the gift year — the extension of time to file does NOT extend the revocation window. A couple that elects §2513 on a Form 709 filed February 2, 2027 may revoke through April 15, 2027 by filing an amended Form 709 with "Election Revoked Under §2513(b)(2)" noted at the top. After April 15, 2027, the election stands and cannot be revoked even if both spouses later regret it. A couple that files an extended Form 709 on October 15, 2027 has locked in the election as of the original April 15, 2027 deadline — six months of §2513 finality accrues before the extended return is even filed.[13]

The extension asymmetry is a planning trap

The §2513(b) extension asymmetry — filing extended to October 15, revocation locked at April 15 — creates a hard practitioner discipline. Do NOT elect §2513 on a return you might want to revoke unless both spouses have fully considered the election by April 15. A common failure: couple files for a Form 4868 extension to work out §529 contribution details, executes the §2513 election in September on the extended Form 709, and only in the following spring discovers that a §2652(a)(2) GST allocation was suboptimal — the election is irrevocable, the GST allocation stands. If revocation may be desired, file a stand-alone Form 8892 for the extension and revisit before April 15.

The deficiency-notice bar under §2513(b)(1)(B)

§2513(b)(1)(B) provides that consent cannot be given after a §6212(a) notice of deficiency has been sent to either spouse with respect to gift tax for the year in question. This bar is rarely triggered because deficiencies for a gift year typically issue years after the gift year (after the §6501(a) three-year assessment statute begins running on the filed Form 709), by which time the April 15 consent deadline has long passed. But the bar is operative in one narrow scenario: where a Form 709 was filed by only one spouse without §2513 election, the IRS issued a deficiency notice challenging the reported gift value, and the couple later attempts a first-time §2513 election to reduce each spouse's exposure. The election is barred.

The §2503(b) annual-exclusion doubling: from $19,000 to $38,000 per donee

The most-used application of §2513 is the doubling of the §2503(b) present-interest annual exclusion. For 2026, §2503(b)(1) as indexed by §2503(b)(2) provides a $19,000 per-donee annual exclusion (Rev. Proc. 2025-32). When the couple elects §2513, each donee receives an effective $38,000 exclusion — $19,000 attributed to the donor spouse under §2513(a)(1) and $19,000 attributed to the non-donor spouse under the same paragraph. The doubling is automatic once §2513 is elected; no separate donee-by-donee allocation is required.[14]

The arithmetic scales linearly with donee count and repeats every year. A married couple maintaining a 16-donee gift program under §2513 through age 90 who began at age 60 removes $18,240,000 of principal from their combined taxable estates ($608,000/year × 30 years) — more than a full $15,000,000 OBBBA exemption of estate value, at zero exemption cost. Add in the appreciation on those transfers held outside the couple's estates, and the multi-decade §2513 program moves 25-40% of the family's transfer-tax exposure to the next generation with no lifetime-exemption or GST-exemption cost. This is the load-bearing arithmetic behind the American estate-planning practice of the annual gift-exclusion program.[15]

Present-interest requirement carries through

§2513's doubling applies only to gifts that qualify for the §2503(b) annual exclusion in the first place — meaning gifts of a present interest. A gift of a future interest (e.g., a remainder interest, a discretionary trust interest, a §2503(b) fails to qualify because the beneficiary has no immediate right to enjoy the property) does NOT qualify for annual-exclusion treatment even with §2513 elected. §2513 does not convert a future-interest gift into a present-interest gift; it merely doubles the exclusion available for gifts that already qualify. Practitioners drafting Crummey trust powers, §2503(c) minor's trusts, and §529 accounts should confirm the present-interest character of the transfer before assuming §2513 will double the exclusion.[16]

The §529(c)(5)(A) doubled front-load: $190,000 per beneficiary

§529(c)(5)(A) permits a §529 contributor to elect five-year averaging on a lump-sum contribution up to five times the §2503(b) annual exclusion — $95,000 per beneficiary for 2026 (5 × $19,000). When the couple elects §2513 in the same year, the doubled annual exclusion of $38,000 flows through to the §529(c)(5)(A) formula, and the effective ceiling doubles to $190,000 per beneficiary (5 × $38,000).[17] This is the single largest tax-favored one-time transfer to a single beneficiary available under the Code short of direct-pay tuition under §2503(e) and a lifetime-exemption allocation.

The doubled front-load's five-year budget occupies both spouses' §2503(b) capacity

A $190,000 §529(c)(5)(A) front-load made in 2026 with §2513 elected consumes each spouse's $19,000 §2503(b) annual exclusion for the beneficiary across each of 2026, 2027, 2028, 2029, and 2030. Neither spouse can make any additional §2503(b)-relying gift to that same beneficiary during the five-year period without excess consuming lifetime exemption. This budget-lock is the most-underappreciated §529(c)(5)(A) planning constraint. Practitioners should audit the couple's other gift-tax obligations to the same beneficiary — Roth-IRA-funding gifts, wedding gifts, down-payment gifts, or holiday gifts — before executing the doubled front-load, and either time those separately-purposed gifts before the front-load year or plan for them to consume lifetime exemption during the five-year window.

The doubled-front-load Form 709 election mechanics

Making a §529(c)(5)(A) front-load with §2513 gift-splitting requires two coordinated elections on the same Form 709:

  1. The §2513 election. Both spouses sign the "Consent of Spouse" block on Part 1, Line 18 of the contributing spouse's Form 709. If both spouses made third-party gifts during the year (e.g., the non-donor spouse made separate $10,000 annual-exclusion cash gifts to nieces and nephews), both spouses file separate Form 709s each with the other spouse's consent signature.
  2. The §529(c)(5)(A) election. The contributing spouse checks the box on Schedule A, Part 2, Column H of Form 709 electing five-year averaging on the §529 contribution and reports 1/5 of the $190,000 contribution ($38,000) as a 2026 gift, with the remaining $152,000 spread across 2027-2030. Reg. §1.529-5(b)(2)(iv) confirms that the §529(c)(5)(A) election is made per contribution and per contributor — the contributing spouse elects once for the full contribution, and the §2513 election automatically causes the doubled amount to flow through.

The Form 709 mechanics are procedurally straightforward but pitfall-prone. Common failure modes include: filing the Form 709 with the §2513 election but forgetting the §529(c)(5)(A) box (result: the $190,000 contribution is treated as a $190,000 present-interest gift in 2026, consuming ~$114,000 of each spouse's lifetime exemption after subtracting each spouse's 2026 $19,000 annual exclusion allocation to that beneficiary); filing the §529(c)(5)(A) box but omitting the spouse consent (result: $190,000 gift treated as sole-donor, consuming ~$76,000 of donor spouse's lifetime exemption after the sole $19,000 annual exclusion); and making the doubled front-load in a year where the other spouse also made an annual-exclusion gift to the same beneficiary (result: excess of $19,000 per year per spouse consumes lifetime exemption).

The §2652(a)(2) GST coordination: split gifts for direct-skip transfers

§2652(a)(2), added by the Tax Reform Act of 1986, coordinates §2513 with Chapter 13 generation-skipping transfer tax: where a §2513 gift-splitting election is in effect for gift-tax purposes, the transfer is treated as made one-half by each spouse for GST-tax purposes as well. This doubles the effective GST-exemption footprint of a direct-skip transfer to a grandchild or more remote descendant.[18]

Example: a grandparent makes a $760,000 direct-skip gift to a grandchild in 2026 (all four parents of the grandchild are living, and the grandchild is a §2613(a) skip person because she is two generations below her grandparent). Absent §2513, the grandparent's own $15,000,000 OBBBA GST exemption is consumed by $760,000 to shelter the transfer from the 40% GST tax on generation-skipping transfers. With §2513 elected, each spouse's GST exemption is drawn on for only $380,000 — freeing $380,000 of the non-donor spouse's exemption for future transfers.

The §2652(a)(2) coordination is automatic once §2513 is elected — no separate GST election is required, and the GST split is not revocable independently of the underlying §2513 election. This is a helpful simplification for the practitioner: file Form 709 with §2513 election, execute Schedule C GST allocation on the doubled basis, and the coordination is complete. The complication arises where the couple wants to elect §2513 for gift-tax purposes (to double the annual exclusion for a program of small annual-exclusion gifts to nieces and nephews) but does NOT want the §2652(a)(2) split for a large direct-skip transfer in the same year — a wish the statute does not accommodate. §2513 is all-or-nothing across all gifts in the year, and the §2652(a)(2) GST coordination follows §2513 for every direct-skip gift.[19]

The taxable-termination and taxable-distribution coordination

§2652(a)(2)'s coordination extends only to gifts that are direct skips (§2612(c) transfers) — it does not automatically follow §2513 into taxable terminations (§2612(a)) or taxable distributions (§2612(b)) from a trust to which the couple contributed together. Where a couple contributed jointly (under §2513) to a grandchild's dynasty trust in 2026, and 20 years later the trust distributes $500,000 to a great-grandchild (a taxable distribution because the great-grandchild is a skip person), the GST-exemption allocation to the trust in 2026 by each spouse determines what portion of the $500,000 distribution is subject to GST tax. If each spouse allocated $1,000,000 of GST exemption to their respective $1,000,000 halves of the 2026 contribution, both halves are fully exempt and the 2046 distribution incurs no GST. If either spouse failed to allocate GST exemption, that spouse's half of the trust corpus carries an inclusion ratio > 0 and the 2046 distribution is partially taxable.[20]

Why §2513 has no application to §2503(e) direct-pay transfers

Yesterday's §2503(e) direct-pay piece walked the mechanics of the unlimited gift-tax exclusion for tuition paid directly to a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational institution and medical expenses paid directly to a §213(d) medical provider. §2503(e)(1) provides that any qualified transfer "shall not be treated as a transfer of property by gift" for Chapter 12 purposes. Because §2513 operates on gifts subject to Chapter 12 gift-tax, and §2503(e) qualified transfers are by definition NOT gifts under Chapter 12, §2513 has no application to §2503(e) direct payments.[21]

The practitioner consequence is a simplification: a grandparent paying $80,000 of a grandchild's medical school tuition directly to the university under §2503(e)(2)(A) needs no §2513 election, no Form 709 filing, and no spouse consent. The $80,000 is not a gift, does not consume the $19,000 §2503(b) annual exclusion, does not consume the $15,000,000 OBBBA lifetime exemption, and does not require §2652(a)(2) GST coordination even if the recipient is a skip person. The doubling arithmetic that §2513 provides for §2503(b) annual-exclusion gifts and §529 contributions is unnecessary because the §2503(e) transfer is already unlimited.

The interaction matters in mixed years, however. A grandparent making both a §2503(e) direct payment of $80,000 to the grandchild's medical school AND a §529(c)(5)(A) $190,000 five-year-averaged front-load in the same 2026 year with §2513 elected splits only the §529 contribution — the §2503(e) payment stands outside the split-gift regime. On the Form 709, the §529 contribution appears on Schedule A with both the §2513 consent and the §529(c)(5)(A) election; the §2503(e) direct-pay transfer does not appear at all.

§2523(i) noncitizen-spouse coordination

§2523(i) limits the marital deduction for gifts BETWEEN spouses to $185,000 per year (2026, indexed) where the donee spouse is a noncitizen — in lieu of the unlimited §2523 marital deduction available for gifts to a citizen spouse. §2523(i) governs interspousal transfers only; it does not directly address §2513's third-party gift-splitting rules. But because §2513(a)(1) requires that both spouses be U.S. citizens or residents at the time of the gift and throughout the year, a couple with one nonresident-noncitizen spouse cannot use §2513 for third-party gifts regardless of the §2523(i) status of interspousal transfers.[22]

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Elena and David (doubled §529(c)(5)(A) front-load for two grandchildren)

Elena (age 68) and David (age 71) are U.S. citizens with a $22,000,000 combined estate. They have two grandchildren — Sofia (age 6) and Lucas (age 3) — and want to make maximum §529 contributions for both in 2026 to jump-start college-funding for the family. Elena's separate brokerage account will fund the contributions.

Elena and David's plan: Elena contributes $190,000 to Sofia's §529 in January 2026 and $190,000 to Lucas's §529 in January 2026 — $380,000 total from Elena's separate funds. The couple elects §2513 gift-splitting on Elena's Form 709 for the 2026 gift year and elects §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging for both contributions. David signs the "Consent of Spouse" block on Elena's Form 709.

Gift-tax treatment:

  • Each $190,000 §529 contribution is split under §2513: $95,000 attributed to Elena, $95,000 attributed to David.
  • Each attributed $95,000 is spread across 2026-2030 under §529(c)(5)(A): $19,000/year to each grandchild.
  • 2026 gift-tax result per spouse per beneficiary: $19,000 annual-exclusion consumed (Elena's $19,000 to Sofia + Elena's $19,000 to Lucas + David's $19,000 to Sofia + David's $19,000 to Lucas), totaling $76,000 across both spouses' 2026 annual-exclusion capacity.
  • 2027-2030 gift-tax result per spouse per beneficiary (each year): same $76,000 annual-exclusion consumption budget, with no additional §2503(b)-relying gifts to Sofia or Lucas possible from either spouse during those years without excess consuming lifetime exemption.
  • Lifetime exemption consumed: $0. GST-exemption consumed: $0.

Comparison to sole-donor scenario: Absent §2513, Elena's $190,000 to each grandchild would be split by §529(c)(5)(A) into $38,000/year over 2026-2030, consuming her sole $19,000/year annual exclusion for each grandchild plus $19,000/year of lifetime exemption for each grandchild ($95,000 of lifetime exemption per grandchild, $190,000 total across both grandchildren, over five years). The §2513 election preserves $190,000 of Elena's lifetime exemption and creates matching lifetime-exemption capacity on David's side.

Case 2: Marcus and Priya (mid-year divorce timing trap)

Marcus (age 45) and Priya (age 43) are dissolving their marriage; their divorce decree is scheduled for entry August 15, 2026. They have three children: Aiden (17), Chloe (14), and Kai (11). Both parents want to contribute to a §529 for each child in the last year they can gift-split. Marcus has separate funds available. The couple's attorneys advise them to consider whether §2513 remains viable given the divorce timing.

Marcus and Priya's original plan: Marcus contributes $190,000 to each child's §529 in July 2026 (before the August 15 divorce decree). Total contribution: $570,000. §2513 election on Marcus's Form 709 for 2026 with §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging elected for all three contributions.

The complication: In October 2026, Marcus becomes engaged to a new partner and marries her on December 20, 2026. Under §2513(a)(2), Marcus's remarriage during the same calendar year as the July gifts disqualifies the §2513 election for ALL 2026 gifts, including the July gifts made while Marcus was married to Priya.

Revised outcome without §2513: Marcus's $570,000 total contributions are sole-donor. Under §529(c)(5)(A), each $190,000 is spread across 2026-2030 as $38,000/year — exceeding Marcus's $19,000 annual exclusion for each child by $19,000/year, or $19,000 × 3 children × 5 years = $285,000 of Marcus's lifetime exemption consumed. Form 709 must be filed by April 15, 2027 reporting the sole-donor treatment; Marcus's OBBBA exemption goes from $15,000,000 to $14,715,000 post-2026-transfers.

The lesson: Had Marcus deferred the wedding to January 2, 2027, the §2513 election would have remained available for the July 2026 gifts, saving $285,000 of Marcus's lifetime exemption. The three-week wedding-date choice cost $285,000 of exemption. Practitioners advising divorcing clients contemplating same-year remarriage must run the §2513 preservation calculation before either party sets a wedding date; deferring even by a few weeks into the following year is often the cheapest planning move available.

Case 3: Diana and Robert (posthumous election after donor spouse's death)

Diana (age 76) and Robert (age 78) made $304,000 of annual-exclusion gifts to eight grandchildren in February 2026 ($38,000 to each, split via a pre-executed §2513 plan). Diana funded all $304,000 from her separate account. Robert dies unexpectedly on November 3, 2026 before Diana files the 2026 Form 709. Diana's attorney needs to file the Form 709 with §2513 election by April 15, 2027.

The mechanics: Under Reg. §25.2513-2(c), the executor of Robert's estate may execute the "Consent of Spouse" block on Diana's Form 709 posthumously. Robert's will names Diana as executrix; Diana signs the Consent of Spouse block in her capacity as executrix of Robert's estate. The election is effective; the $304,000 is split $152,000 to each spouse; each spouse consumed $19,000 of annual exclusion for each of the eight grandchildren ($152,000 per spouse), fully absorbed by annual-exclusion capacity with no lifetime-exemption consumption.

Coordination with Robert's Form 706 estate return: Robert's Form 706 estate return (due 9 months after death — August 3, 2027, extendable to February 3, 2028 via Form 4768) reports Robert's date-of-death estate. The 2026 gifts attributed to Robert under §2513 are included in Robert's cumulative-transfer calculation on Form 706 Schedule G at the $152,000 attributed amount, not the $0 out-of-pocket amount, because the §2513 election legally reallocates the transfer for gift-tax and estate-tax purposes. Diana's DSUE (deceased spousal unused exemption) portability computation under §2010(c)(4) reflects Robert's exemption at $15,000,000 minus $152,000 = $14,848,000, which Diana may elect to port under §2010(c)(5)(A) by making the portability election on Robert's Form 706.

The lesson: Death of either spouse during the gift year does not by itself disqualify the §2513 election, provided the surviving spouse (or the deceased spouse's executor) makes the necessary consent by the April 15 deadline. Practitioners handling estates with unfiled Form 709s should audit the decedent's gift-year obligations early and preserve the §2513 election where the decedent's estate benefits from portability or the surviving spouse's estate benefits from exemption preservation. The posthumous election is often the most valuable single Form 709 line item in an estate.

Six recurring §2513 mistakes

  1. Missing the non-donor spouse's signature on Form 709. The single most common §2513 failure. Only donor spouse files Form 709; non-donor spouse never signs the "Consent of Spouse" block. The election is void; gifts revert to sole-donor treatment. Always confirm both signatures before mailing or e-filing.
  2. Not filing Form 709 at all when only §2513-doubled annual-exclusion gifts were made. A couple thinks: "each gift was under $19,000 to each donee, so no Form 709 is required." True for sole-donor treatment, but §2513 election REQUIRES a Form 709 filing to make the election, even if all gifts are within the doubled $38,000 annual exclusion. Without the Form 709 filing, no election has been made, and any single-spouse gift over $19,000 to any donee is a taxable gift consuming that spouse's lifetime exemption.
  3. Ignoring the no-remarriage rule. Couple divorces mid-year, one spouse remarries a new partner before December 31, no §2513 election is available for any 2026 gift. Time the wedding into the following calendar year, or plan the divorce-year gifts on a sole-donor basis.
  4. Ignoring the noncitizen-spouse bar. Mixed-status international couples frequently discover the §2513 bar only at Form 709 preparation. Audit citizenship and residency status of both spouses for the entire gift year before assuming §2513 availability.
  5. Missing the April 15 revocation window. Couple files Form 709 on February 2, 2027 with §2513 election, discovers in June 2027 that a §2652(a)(2) GST allocation was suboptimal. The election is irrevocable after April 15, 2027; the sub-optimal allocation stands. Do not file §2513 elections early unless the plan is fully vetted.
  6. Assuming §2513 splits §2503(e) direct-pay transfers. A couple with a large §2503(e) direct-pay tuition payment plus a §529(c)(5)(A) front-load in the same year sometimes assumes §2513 applies to both. §2513 has no application to §2503(e) transfers because they are not gifts for Chapter 12 purposes. Only the §529 contribution and any other §2503(b) gifts are split; the §2503(e) direct-pay stays outside the split-gift regime and does not appear on Form 709 at all.

Eight-item §2513 pre-election checklist

  1. Confirm both spouses' U.S. citizen or resident status for the entire gift year. §2513(a)(1) and Reg. §25.2513-1(b)(1) require both spouses to be U.S. persons at the time of each gift AND throughout the year. Any surrender of green card, expatriation, or failure of substantial-presence test at any point disqualifies the election.
  2. Confirm no remarriage of either spouse during the gift year. §2513(a)(2) disqualifies the entire year if either spouse remarries a different person before December 31. In divorce-year gift planning, defer any same-year remarriage until January 1 of the following year.
  3. Enumerate ALL gifts by BOTH spouses to third parties during the year. §2513 is all-or-nothing across every gift both spouses made to third parties during the year. Do not elect §2513 for the "large gift" while ignoring the "small gifts" — the election either applies to every gift or to none.
  4. Determine whether both spouses will file separate Form 709s. If both spouses made gifts to third parties during the year, each files a separate Form 709 and each must sign the other's consent block. If only one spouse made gifts, only that spouse files, and the non-donor spouse signs the Consent of Spouse block on that single Form 709.
  5. Coordinate §529(c)(5)(A) elections with the §2513 election. Any §529 contribution over $19,000 per beneficiary intended to use five-year averaging with the §2513 doubling requires the §529(c)(5)(A) election box on Schedule A, Part 2, Column H of Form 709. Missing either box costs lifetime exemption.
  6. Audit §2503(b) capacity to same beneficiaries during a §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging period. A doubled §190,000 front-load consumes each spouse's $19,000 annual-exclusion capacity for that beneficiary for 2026-2030. Other planned gifts to that beneficiary (Roth-IRA-funding gifts, wedding gifts, down-payment gifts) during the five-year window will consume lifetime exemption unless timed outside the averaging period.
  7. Coordinate §2652(a)(2) GST allocation for direct-skip transfers. If any 2026 gift is a §2612(c) direct skip (e.g., contribution to a grandchild's §529 or a dynasty trust), each spouse's Form 709 Schedule C allocates GST exemption on the doubled basis. Confirm both spouses' available GST-exemption balances and desired allocations before filing.
  8. File Form 709 by April 15 of the year following the gift year. §2513(b)(1)(A) sets the election deadline at April 15; a §6081/Form 4868 or stand-alone Form 8892 extension carries the election deadline forward six months. But revocation window under §2513(b)(2) is locked at April 15 regardless of extension. Do not file §2513 elections early unless the plan is fully vetted; do not defer to the extended October 15 without confirming both spouses are settled.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2513 permit?

A married couple may elect to treat any gift made by one spouse to a third party as made half by each spouse for gift-tax and GST-tax purposes. The election is made on Form 709 with both spouses' signatures, applies to ALL third-party gifts made by either spouse during the calendar year, doubles the §2503(b) annual exclusion from $19,000 to $38,000 per donee, doubles the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year-averaged front-load from $95,000 to $190,000 per beneficiary, and via §2652(a)(2) doubles the GST-exemption footprint of a direct skip.

Do both spouses have to sign Form 709?

Yes. §2513(b)(2) and Reg. §25.2513-2(a) require both spouses' signatures. Failure to obtain both signatures voids the election and reverts the gifts to sole-donor treatment. If both spouses made gifts during the year, each files a separate Form 709 and each must sign the other spouse's Form 709 in the Consent of Spouse block.

Can §2513 double the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging front-load?

Yes — the ceiling doubles from $95,000 to $190,000 per beneficiary for 2026. Both spouses must consent on Form 709, both spouses must be U.S. citizens or residents throughout the year, and neither spouse can make additional §2503(b)-relying gifts to the same beneficiary during the five-year averaging period without excess consuming lifetime exemption.

Does §2513 apply to generation-skipping transfers?

Yes — via §2652(a)(2). Where a §2513 election is in effect, the gift is treated as made half by each spouse for GST-tax purposes as well. This doubles the effective GST-exemption footprint of a direct-skip transfer: a $760,000 gift to a grandchild consumes only $380,000 of each spouse's $15,000,000 OBBBA GST exemption rather than $760,000 of one spouse's exemption. The coordination is automatic once §2513 is elected.

Does §2513 apply to §2503(e) direct-pay tuition and medical payments?

No. §2503(e) qualified transfers are not gifts for Chapter 12 purposes, so there is nothing to split under §2513. A grandparent paying $80,000 of tuition directly to a grandchild's medical school needs no §2513 election, no Form 709 filing, and no spouse's signature. §2513 becomes relevant only for gifts that ARE gifts for Chapter 12 purposes — §2503(b) annual-exclusion gifts, §529 contributions, and larger transfers consuming OBBBA lifetime exemption.

Does §2513 work if one spouse is a nonresident noncitizen?

No. §2513(a)(1)(B) requires both spouses to be U.S. citizens or U.S. residents at the time of each gift AND throughout the entire calendar year. If either spouse is a nonresident noncitizen at any point during the year — including via mid-year green-card surrender or §877A expatriation — no §2513 election is available for any gift made in that year.

When must the §2513 election be filed and can it be revoked?

Form 709 must be filed by April 15 of the year following the gift year (§6075(b)(1)). A §6081 six-month extension via Form 4868 or Form 8892 extends the filing deadline to October 15. But the revocation window under §2513(b)(2) is locked at April 15 regardless of extension — after April 15 of the year following the gift year, the election is irrevocable. Do not file §2513 elections early unless the plan is fully vetted.

What happens if the couple divorces mid-year?

§2513(a)(2) permits gift-splitting only for gifts made while both spouses were married to each other and only if neither spouse remarries a different person before December 31. A couple divorcing on June 30, 2026 may elect §2513 for gifts made through June 30 — but only if neither ex-spouse remarries in 2026. Same-year remarriage disqualifies the entire year, including gifts made before the divorce.

What happens if the donor spouse dies before filing Form 709?

The executor of the deceased spouse's estate may make the §2513 election posthumously with the surviving spouse's consent (or vice versa). Reg. §25.2513-2(c) permits either the deceased spouse's executor or the surviving spouse to execute the required consent. The April 15 deadline still applies, but Form 4768 (Form 706 estate return extension) typically carries the associated Form 709 filing deadline as well. Estate practitioners should audit the decedent's prior-year Form 709 obligations early.

Is §2513 the same as the §2523 marital deduction?

No — the two provisions address different transactions. §2523 provides an unlimited marital deduction for gifts BETWEEN spouses. §2513 provides an election to split gifts by one spouse to a THIRD party, treating them as made half by each. §2523 is automatic (no election required for citizen spouses); §2513 requires an affirmative election with both spouses' signatures on Form 709. The two provisions coordinate cleanly and can be used in the same year.
