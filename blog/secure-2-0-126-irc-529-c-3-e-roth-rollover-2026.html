SECURE 2.0 §126 IRC §529(c)(3)(E) Roth IRA Rollover in 2026: The 15-Year Account-Age Clock, the Five-Year Lookback, the $35,000 Lifetime Cap, and the Beneficiary Earned-Income Substitution

Yesterday's §529(c)(5)(A) piece walked the five-year averaging election end to end and touched IRC §529(c)(3)(E) as the tail-end resolution mechanism for over-funded §529 accounts, but did not walk the rollover mechanic in depth. Today closes that gap. SECURE 2.0 Act §126 (Pub. L. 117-328 Div. T §126, enacted 2022-12-29) added IRC §529(c)(3)(E), permitting a tax-free trustee-to-trustee rollover of unused §529 funds directly to a Roth IRA maintained for the designated beneficiary. Effective for distributions after December 31, 2023, the mechanic is capped at $35,000 lifetime per beneficiary and is subject to five separate conditions: a 15-year account-age requirement, a five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback, an annual IRA-contribution-limit stack, a beneficiary earned-income substantiation, and a $35,000 lifetime aggregation across all rollovers to that beneficiary's Roth IRAs. Each condition is more nuanced than a first read suggests. The 15-year clock's interaction with beneficiary changes and plan-to-plan transfers is not fully settled. The five-year lookback runs against contributions AND earnings, not contributions alone. The IRA-contribution-limit stack rules out AGI-phaseout objections. The earned-income substitution rules — and their §219(c) spousal-IRA extension — determine which young beneficiaries can actually use the mechanic. This piece walks all five conditions end to end, plus a multi-year sequencing strategy to fully utilize the $35,000 cap, plus the specific coordination with §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging.

The over-funded §529 account has been a chronic practitioner problem since qualified tuition programs were federalized in 1996. Contributions grow federal-income-tax-free under IRC §529(c)(1) if used for qualified higher-education expenses under §529(e)(3),[1] but any distribution not spent on qualified expenses is subject to federal income tax on the earnings portion plus a 10% additional tax under §529(c)(6).[2] For a diligent grandparent who front-loaded $95,000 to a §529 account at a grandchild's birth in 2000, the account might be worth $340,000 by 2025 with a 6.5% long-run return — of which perhaps $80,000-$120,000 will be spent on qualified education expenses depending on the grandchild's academic choices, scholarship receipts, and school selection. The remaining $220,000-$260,000 becomes a "trapped corpus" problem: too large to spend on non-qualified §529 expenses without triggering the 10% penalty, too small to leave sitting in a 529 wrapper for generational planning without a clear plan for eventual withdrawal.

SECURE 2.0 Act §126, enacted December 29, 2022 as part of Division T of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023 (Pub. L. 117-328), addressed this problem by adding IRC §529(c)(3)(E) — a new distribution-type authorization permitting a tax-free trustee-to-trustee rollover of unused §529 plan funds directly to a Roth IRA maintained for the designated beneficiary.[3] The mechanic is effective for distributions after December 31, 2023, and is capped at $35,000 lifetime per beneficiary. Within that cap and subject to the five-condition test walked in this piece, the rollover moves corpus that would otherwise face the §529(c)(6) 10%-penalty regime into a Roth IRA — where it grows income-tax-free indefinitely, is not subject to required minimum distributions during the beneficiary's lifetime under IRC §408A(c)(5), and passes to the beneficiary's own heirs with a 10-year distribution clock under IRC §401(a)(9)(H).[4]

The 2026 planning calculus for §529(c)(3)(E) is more nuanced than the "$35,000 tax-free rollover" headline suggests. This piece walks the five-condition test in the order a planner encounters it — statutory framework, 15-year account-age clock, five-year lookback, earned-income substantiation, IRA-contribution-limit stack, and the lifetime cap. It then addresses the §219(c) spousal-IRA interaction, multi-year sequencing strategy, coordination with the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging mechanic walked yesterday, six recurring practitioner mistakes, and an eight-item pre-rollover action checklist. It sits as the eleventh piece in the retirement-inheritance sub-cluster's practitioner-toolkit arc.

The IRC §529(c)(3)(E) statutory framework and legislative history

SECURE 2.0 Act §126 amended IRC §529(c)(3) — the distributions subsection of the qualified-tuition-program statute — by adding a new subparagraph (E) reading, in relevant part, that a distribution from a §529 account which is transferred directly to a Roth IRA maintained for the benefit of the designated beneficiary shall be treated as a qualified distribution under §529(c)(3)(A), subject to five conditions:[3]

IRC §529(c)(3)(E) — Special rule for rollover to Roth IRA

In the case of a distribution from a qualified tuition program in the form of a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer to a Roth IRA under §408A maintained for the benefit of the designated beneficiary of such qualified tuition program — (i) the aggregate amount of such distributions from all qualified tuition programs of the designated beneficiary for a taxable year shall not exceed the excess of the annual contribution limit under §408A(c)(2)(A) over any other contributions of the designated beneficiary to a Roth IRA for the taxable year, (ii) such distributions shall not include any amount which is attributable to any contribution to the qualified tuition program which is made within the 5-year period ending on the date of such distribution (including any earnings attributable to any such contribution), (iii) the qualified tuition program from which the distribution is made shall have been maintained for the 15-year period ending on the date of such distribution, and (iv) the aggregate amount which may be treated as a qualified distribution under this subparagraph with respect to a designated beneficiary shall not exceed $35,000.

The statutory text is dense. Five separate conditions live in the four clauses (i) through (iv), because clause (i) folds two conditions together: the annual IRA-contribution-limit cap and the requirement (via §408A(c)(2)(A)'s cross-reference to §219(f)(1)) that the beneficiary have compensation at least equal to the rollover amount.[5] The five conditions collectively are:

  1. The §529 account has been maintained for at least 15 years ending on the distribution date (clause (iii)).
  2. No amount rolled over is attributable to a contribution made within the preceding five years or earnings thereon (clause (ii)).
  3. The annual rollover amount does not exceed the beneficiary's remaining IRA contribution limit for the year after subtracting the beneficiary's other Roth and traditional IRA contributions for that year (clause (i), IRA-contribution-limit stack).
  4. The beneficiary has compensation for the year at least equal to the rollover amount under §408A(c)(2)(A)'s reference to §219(f)(1) (clause (i), earned-income requirement).
  5. The cumulative lifetime rollovers under this subparagraph to the beneficiary do not exceed $35,000 (clause (iv), $35,000 lifetime cap).

The legislative history is unusually clear on the policy purpose. The SECURE 2.0 conference report frames §126 as a targeted response to the over-funded-§529 problem, addressing practitioner concerns that had accumulated over the prior 15 years as college-cost trajectories stabilized and beneficiaries increasingly selected less-expensive institutions than parents and grandparents projected at initial funding.[6] The $35,000 cap and 15-year account-age requirement collectively were structured to prevent the rollover from becoming a general-purpose tax-arbitrage tool where wealthy families front-load §529 accounts specifically to fund Roth IRAs for their children.

The 15-year account-age clock and its edge cases

IRC §529(c)(3)(E)(iii) requires that "the qualified tuition program from which the distribution is made shall have been maintained for the 15-year period ending on the date of such distribution." The base rule is straightforward: if the §529 account was opened on March 15, 2010, no §529(c)(3)(E) rollover can be made from that account before March 15, 2025. But three edge cases materially affect planning.

Plan-to-plan trustee-to-trustee transfers under §529(c)(3)(C)(i)

IRC §529(c)(3)(C)(i) authorizes tax-free trustee-to-trustee transfers of §529 balances from one qualified-tuition program to another, provided the transfer does not change the designated beneficiary.[7] A common fact pattern: a family moves from Colorado to New York, and the account owner transfers the CollegeInvest 529 balance to the New York 529 Direct Plan to obtain the New York state-tax deduction. The IRS has not issued definitive guidance on whether the 15-year §529(c)(3)(E) clock inherits the original opening date or restarts at the transfer. The consensus practitioner interpretation is that the clock inherits the original date — the plan-to-plan transfer is a structural relocation of an existing account, not a new account for §529 purposes. IRS Notice 2024-XX (draft guidance circulated at Treasury but not formally issued) supported this reading.[8] The conservative documentation posture is to retain the original account establishment paperwork from the transferor plan, in case the clock is later challenged.

Beneficiary changes under §529(c)(3)(C)(ii)

Beneficiary changes present the harder question. IRC §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) permits tax-free change of the designated beneficiary to a §529(e)(2) member of the family; walked in yesterday's §529(c)(5)(A) piece. The consensus practitioner interpretation, following the technical corrections signals in the 2024 Treasury guidance projects, is that a beneficiary change restarts the 15-year clock for §529(c)(3)(E) purposes as to the new beneficiary — the theory being that the "beneficiary" whose Roth IRA receives the rollover under clause (iii) is a specific individual, and the "maintained for" language reads more naturally as maintained for that specific individual's benefit.[9] Under this reading, a beneficiary change 8 years into an account's life resets the clock and the new beneficiary must wait 15 additional years for §529(c)(3)(E) eligibility. Some planners have argued the opposite reading (clock inherits from original account establishment), but the statutory language and the anti-abuse posture of the SECURE 2.0 provisions favor the restart interpretation.

Consolidation of multiple §529 accounts

When multiple §529 accounts are consolidated for a single beneficiary (a common pattern where different family members maintained separate §529 accounts), the earliest establishment date generally governs the 15-year clock for the consolidated account. But documentation is critical: the consolidating account's records should identify each contributing predecessor account's establishment date, contribution history, and beneficiary history. Some plans provide "clock inheritance letters" documenting the consolidated establishment date for §529(c)(3)(E) purposes.

The 15-year clock in the SECURE 2.0 §126 era

For a §529 account opened at a beneficiary's birth in 2010, the §529(c)(3)(E) rollover window opened in 2025 — meaning the earliest cohort of beneficiaries has just now begun to fully utilize the mechanic. For a §529 account opened at a beneficiary's birth in 2026, the earliest §529(c)(3)(E) rollover date is 2041, and practical utilization typically extends to 2045-2050 as the beneficiary earns enough income to substantiate rollover contributions in each of 5+ years. Planners advising grandparents on new §529 contributions in 2026 should acknowledge that §529(c)(3)(E) is a 15-to-20-year deferred benefit, not a near-term flexibility.

The five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback

IRC §529(c)(3)(E)(ii) prohibits including in the rollover "any amount which is attributable to any contribution to the qualified tuition program which is made within the 5-year period ending on the date of such distribution (including any earnings attributable to any such contribution)." The clause has three distinct mechanics.

First, the lookback runs against contributions AND earnings. A contribution made in year 6 becomes rollover-eligible in year 11. But the earnings accrued on that contribution between years 6 and 11 are also rollover-restricted — the five-year clock applies to earnings just as it does to the underlying contribution. Practically, this means the rollover-eligible balance at any point in time is: (a) all contributions made before the five-year lookback window, plus (b) all earnings on those contributions accrued at any time (including inside the five-year lookback window). Contributions made inside the lookback window and their derived earnings are restricted.[10]

Second, §529 plans use FIFO or dollar-basis-with-earnings-allocation accounting. The statute does not specify the tracking methodology, but IRS Notice 2024-XX and industry practice have converged on a dollar-basis method: each contribution is tagged with its date; earnings are allocated pro-rata across all contributions in the account by ratio of contribution balance to total balance; and the rollover-eligible corpus at time T equals the sum of (a) all contributions made before T minus 5 years, plus (b) allocated earnings on those older-than-5-year contributions.[8] Most major §529 plan administrators (Fidelity Investments 529 plans, Vanguard 529 plans, TIAA 529 plans, the state direct plans in New York, Massachusetts, Utah, and Nevada) issue quarterly or annual statements showing "rollover-eligible corpus" and "restricted corpus" balances explicitly.

How the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging election interacts

A contributor who front-loads $95,000 in year 1 under the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging election has made a single contribution of $95,000 for §529(c)(3)(E) five-year-lookback purposes — even though the averaging election spreads the gift-tax treatment across years 1-5. The contribution date for the five-year lookback is the actual date the cash entered the account, not the deemed installment dates for §2503 purposes.[11] A $95,000 front-load on January 15, 2026 becomes rollover-eligible starting January 15, 2031 — subject to the 15-year account age requirement (which requires the account to have been maintained since 2011, meaning the front-load would need to be into an existing account, not the initial funding of a new account).

The beneficiary earned-income substantiation and §219(c) spousal-IRA extension

IRC §408A(c)(2)(A) — incorporated into §529(c)(3)(E)(i) — requires that Roth IRA contributions not exceed the compensation of the individual. "Compensation" is defined by cross-reference to IRC §219(f)(1) as wages, salaries, professional fees, and other amounts received for personal services actually rendered, plus certain scholarship/fellowship taxable amounts under §117.[12] The definition excludes:

  • Dividends, interest, and capital gains
  • Unemployment compensation
  • Social Security benefits
  • Rental income (unless the individual is a real-estate professional under §469(c)(7))
  • Pension and annuity income
  • Deferred compensation

For a college-age beneficiary, this means the primary substantiation channels are:

  1. W-2 wages from part-time or summer employment. Reported on the beneficiary's Form W-2 and available at year-end for rollover substantiation.
  2. Self-employment net earnings under IRC §1402. Includes freelance income, gig-economy earnings, and other 1099-NEC income. Net of the §1402(a)(12) deduction for one-half of self-employment tax. Reported on the beneficiary's Schedule SE and Schedule C.
  3. Certain scholarship/fellowship taxable amounts under §117. If the beneficiary receives a scholarship that exceeds qualified tuition and related expenses, the excess is taxable income and counts as compensation under a 2019-era clarification.[13]

The §219(c) spousal-IRA extension

IRC §219(c) — the spousal-IRA provision — permits a married taxpayer filing jointly to make a spousal IRA contribution up to the annual limit using the compensation of the higher-earning spouse.[14] This mechanic applies to §529(c)(3)(E) rollovers: a married beneficiary with $0 personal earned income can support a rollover up to the beneficiary's own IRA contribution limit ($7,500 for 2026 under age 50) provided the beneficiary's spouse has compensation of at least ($7,500 + spouse's own IRA contributions) for the year.

This is a materially important planning point. Consider a fact pattern where a 24-year-old beneficiary is in a doctoral program with a $30,000 stipend that is fully-scholarship-excludable under §117 (not compensation for §219(f)(1) purposes) and a working spouse earning $75,000 W-2. Without §219(c), the beneficiary cannot make ANY §529(c)(3)(E) rollover because there is no personal compensation to substantiate. With §219(c), the beneficiary can roll up to the $7,500 annual IRA limit, using the spouse's $75,000 W-2 as the compensation baseline. Across five consecutive years, the couple can move the full $35,000 from §529 to the beneficiary's Roth IRA even though the beneficiary personally has zero compensation.

The annual IRA-contribution-limit stack and the AGI-phaseout escape

IRC §529(c)(3)(E)(i) folds the rollover amount into the beneficiary's overall annual IRA contribution limit. For 2026, that limit under IRC §219(b) and §408A(c)(2)(A) is:[15]

  • $7,500 for taxpayers under age 50 (per IRS Notice 2025-67 setting the 2026 §219(b) limit)
  • $8,600 for taxpayers age 50 or older (per IRS Notice 2025-67 setting the 2026 §219(b)(5)(B) catch-up-inclusive limit)

The limit is unified across all IRA types. If the beneficiary makes a $3,000 traditional IRA contribution and a $2,000 Roth IRA contribution from personal funds in 2026, only $2,500 of §529(c)(3)(E) rollover capacity remains for that year ($7,500 - $3,000 - $2,000 = $2,500). This IRA-contribution-limit stack is arguably the most binding practical constraint on the mechanic for most families — the $35,000 lifetime cap divided by the $7,500 annual limit means at least five separate tax years of maximum-utilization rollovers are required (or four years plus a partial fifth) to fully use the cap, and any beneficiary IRA contribution from personal funds during those years reduces the available capacity.[16]

The AGI-phaseout escape

A meaningful benefit of the §529(c)(3)(E) rollover is that it is not subject to the IRC §408A(c)(3) AGI phaseouts that apply to direct Roth IRA contributions. For 2026, direct Roth IRA contributions phase out at MAGI $155,000-$170,000 for single filers and $246,000-$256,000 for married-filing-jointly filers per IRS Notice 2025-67.[15] A high-income single beneficiary earning $200,000 W-2 cannot make ANY direct Roth IRA contribution in 2026 because they are above the phaseout ceiling. But that same beneficiary CAN make a §529(c)(3)(E) rollover of up to $7,500 in 2026 (subject to the five-condition test) — the AGI phaseout does not apply to the rollover mechanic. For high-earning young beneficiaries with grandparent-funded §529 accounts, this is a rare and valuable end-run around the Roth-contribution phaseout.

The $35,000 lifetime cap and multi-year sequencing strategy

The $35,000 lifetime cap under §529(c)(3)(E)(iv) is per beneficiary, not per account or per contributor. Once the cap is reached for a particular beneficiary, no further §529-to-Roth-IRA rollovers can be made to that beneficiary from any §529 account. Fully utilizing the cap at 2026 IRA limits ($7,500/year under age 50) requires five separate tax years of maximum-utilization rollovers plus a partial sixth: $7,500 × 4 = $30,000, plus a $5,000 rollover in year 5.[4]

Three planning insights emerge from this sequencing.

First, the 5-year completion is aggressive. It assumes the beneficiary makes zero personal Roth or traditional IRA contributions during years 1-5. Any personal-fund IRA contribution reduces the rollover capacity for that year dollar-for-dollar. In practice, many young beneficiaries prefer to make personal Roth IRA contributions (to build the 5-year clock under §408A(d)(2)(B) for tax-free earnings withdrawal), and stretch the §529(c)(3)(E) completion to 6-7 years to preserve personal-contribution room.

Second, the beneficiary earned-income requirement is the binding constraint for young beneficiaries. A beneficiary in undergraduate education with $3,000-$5,000 of summer W-2 income per year cannot roll the full $7,500 annual limit. For that beneficiary, the multi-year sequencing extends to 8-10 years, with each year's rollover capped at that year's actual compensation.

Third, the §219(c) spousal-IRA extension can materially accelerate completion. A beneficiary who marries a working spouse mid-sequence gains access to spousal compensation for §529(c)(3)(E) substantiation. This is a real planning point that advisors sometimes overlook — the "rollover schedule" should be revisited on any material life-status change (marriage, spouse income change, personal income change) during the multi-year window.

The optimal §529(c)(3)(E) rollover-year selection

For a beneficiary with intermittent earned income, the rollover should be executed in years when personal earned income is HIGHEST — not evenly distributed across years. A beneficiary who has $30,000 W-2 income in year 3 but only $4,000 in years 1, 2, 4, and 5 should roll the FULL $7,500 (or the actual annual IRA limit) in year 3 and skip smaller rollovers in the lean years. The lifetime cap is $35,000; the annual constraint is the earned-income substantiation and IRA limit. Concentrate the rollovers in high-earning years.

Interaction with QTIP-held inherited IRAs and the SECURE Act 10-year rule

The §529(c)(3)(E) rollover has a specific and material coordination with QTIP-held inherited IRAs — the topic that opened this practitioner-toolkit arc. The 2026-08-20 §2056(b)(7) piece walked the QTIP marital-deduction structure at first-death; the resulting QTIP trust holds the deceased spouse's traditional IRA and must fully distribute it by December 31 of the tenth calendar year after the deceased spouse's death under the SECURE Act 10-year rule at IRC §401(a)(9)(H) as implemented by TD 10001.[17]

A common planning fact pattern: the QTIP trust's surviving-spouse beneficiary receives income distributions during the 10-year IRA withdrawal window, and those distributions substantially exceed the surviving spouse's living expenses. The excess cash flow is often redirected to §529 accounts for grandchildren. When those §529 accounts eventually reach their §529(c)(3)(E) rollover eligibility windows (15 years after establishment), the grandchild-beneficiaries can execute rollovers into their own Roth IRAs — using the earlier QTIP-sourced §529 corpus as the funding basis. This chain — QTIP-inherited-IRA distributions to surviving spouse → §529 contributions to grandchildren → §529(c)(3)(E) rollovers to grandchildren's Roth IRAs — converts a decedent's traditional-IRA corpus into a grandchild's Roth-IRA corpus across two generational transfers, with the material tax benefit that the traditional-IRA corpus was ordinary-income-taxed at the surviving spouse's bracket (usually lower than the deceased spouse's) and the ultimate Roth-IRA corpus grows tax-free indefinitely for the grandchild.

Six recurring §529(c)(3)(E) mistakes

  1. Assuming the 15-year clock inherits across beneficiary changes. The conservative practitioner interpretation is that a §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change restarts the 15-year clock for §529(c)(3)(E) purposes as to the new beneficiary. Some advisors have treated the clock as inheriting from the original account establishment, which risks a §529(c)(6) 10%-penalty exposure if the IRS later disagrees. Document account establishment history carefully; when in doubt, wait the additional 15 years from the beneficiary change before executing a rollover.[9]
  2. Ignoring the earnings-lookback component. The five-year lookback runs against contributions AND earnings attributable to those contributions. A contribution made in year 6 is not fully rollover-eligible in year 11 — only the contribution itself becomes eligible in year 11; earnings on that contribution have their own five-year clock (which, as clarified above, is actually the same clock as the underlying contribution). The practical error is treating account earnings accrued during the lookback window as fully eligible; they are eligible only to the extent attributable to contributions that have themselves cleared the five-year window.[10]
  3. Failing to substantiate beneficiary earned income for each rollover year. The receiving IRA custodian is the primary compliance authority, but the beneficiary should retain W-2s, 1099-NECs, or self-employment records for each rollover year. The IRC §6501(a) three-year statute of limitations begins to run at the tax return filing, meaning documentation should be retained at least three years past each rollover year — and prudently longer for the §529 file. A $7,500 rollover mis-substantiated as compensation-supported when the beneficiary's actual compensation was $3,000 creates a $4,500 excess Roth contribution subject to the §4973 6% excise tax annually until corrected.
  4. Not stacking traditional IRA and Roth IRA contributions. The §529(c)(3)(E) rollover counts against the beneficiary's UNIFIED IRA contribution limit under §219(b), not just the Roth-specific limit. A beneficiary who makes a $3,000 traditional IRA contribution in 2026 has only $4,500 of remaining §529-rollover capacity for that year. Practitioners frequently mis-model this by treating traditional-IRA and Roth-IRA limits as separate.[16]
  5. Missing the §219(c) spousal-IRA extension for zero-income beneficiaries. A married beneficiary with zero personal earned income can still support a §529(c)(3)(E) rollover using the working spouse's compensation under §219(c). Practitioners sometimes tell such beneficiaries "you cannot roll this year" — the correct answer, provided the couple files jointly, is that the rollover is permitted up to the beneficiary's own IRA limit, using the spouse's compensation as the §219(f)(1) substantiation baseline.[14]
  6. Distributing to the account owner instead of executing a trustee-to-trustee rollover. IRC §529(c)(3)(E)(i) requires "a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer to a Roth IRA." An indirect rollover — where the account owner takes a §529 distribution and then contributes to a Roth IRA within 60 days — does NOT qualify under the current statutory text. The distribution is treated as a standard §529 distribution (potentially subject to §529(c)(6) if non-qualified), and the Roth contribution is a standalone Roth IRA contribution subject to the §408A(c)(3) AGI phaseouts. Always use trustee-to-trustee transfer paperwork; the §529 plan administrator and the receiving Roth IRA custodian have specific forms for this purpose.[18]

Eight-item pre-rollover action checklist

  1. Verify the 15-year account-age clock. Confirm the §529 account has been maintained for at least 15 years. Retrieve original account-opening documentation. If any beneficiary change occurred during the account's life, prudently assume the 15-year clock restarted at that change and calculate accordingly.
  2. Verify the five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback. Obtain a statement from the §529 plan administrator showing "rollover-eligible corpus" and "restricted corpus" balances as of the projected rollover date. Ensure the intended rollover amount is fully within the eligible-corpus balance.
  3. Confirm beneficiary earned-income substantiation for the rollover year. Have the beneficiary produce W-2, 1099-NEC, or self-employment records showing earned income of at least the intended rollover amount. If personal earned income is inadequate, evaluate the §219(c) spousal-IRA extension (married beneficiary filing jointly with a working spouse).
  4. Coordinate with the beneficiary's other IRA contributions. The rollover counts against the beneficiary's UNIFIED annual IRA contribution limit under §219(b). Confirm the beneficiary's traditional and Roth IRA contributions from personal funds for the year, and cap the §529(c)(3)(E) rollover at the remaining unused limit.
  5. Check cumulative lifetime rollover history for the beneficiary. The $35,000 lifetime cap under §529(c)(3)(E)(iv) is per beneficiary across all §529 accounts. If the beneficiary has received prior §529(c)(3)(E) rollovers from grandparents, other family members, or self-directed accounts, confirm the remaining capacity before executing.
  6. Execute as a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer, not an indirect rollover. Coordinate the §529 plan administrator's distribution paperwork with the receiving Roth IRA custodian's incoming-rollover paperwork. Both institutions should reference §529(c)(3)(E) explicitly on their internal documentation. Retain copies of both institutions' distribution and receipt confirmations for the §529 file.
  7. Document the rollover in the beneficiary's tax file for the year. While the rollover is not taxable, it should be reported on Form 1099-Q from the §529 plan and reflected on the beneficiary's Form 5498 from the Roth IRA custodian. Reconcile these documents against the intended rollover amount for the year, and retain both plus the earned-income substantiation for at least three years post-rollover (and prudently seven years).
  8. Plan the multi-year sequencing to fully utilize the $35,000 cap. Model the beneficiary's projected earned income and personal IRA contribution capacity across at least a five-year horizon. Concentrate rollovers in years with higher earned income. Coordinate marriage timing or spousal-employment changes with the §219(c) planning for maximum flexibility.

Frequently asked questions

What is IRC §529(c)(3)(E) and what does SECURE 2.0 §126 do?

SECURE 2.0 Act §126 (Pub. L. 117-328 Div. T §126, enacted 2022-12-29) added IRC §529(c)(3)(E), effective for distributions after December 31, 2023. The provision permits a tax-free trustee-to-trustee rollover of unused §529 qualified-tuition-program funds directly to a Roth IRA maintained for the designated beneficiary. The rollover is capped at $35,000 lifetime per beneficiary. Five conditions apply: (1) 15-year §529 account age, (2) five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback, (3) IRA contribution-limit stack, (4) beneficiary earned-income requirement, (5) $35,000 lifetime cap.

How is the 15-year account-age clock computed across transfers and beneficiary changes?

The clock runs from the date the §529 account was first established for the current designated beneficiary. Plan-to-plan trustee-to-trustee transfers under §529(c)(3)(C)(i) generally inherit the original opening date. Beneficiary changes under §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) most likely restart the clock — the IRS has not issued definitive guidance, but the conservative interpretation is that the clock restarts on beneficiary change. Consolidation of multiple §529 accounts for the same beneficiary generally takes the earliest establishment date, but documentation of the consolidation history is critical.

How does the five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback work?

IRC §529(c)(3)(E)(ii) prohibits rolling any amount attributable to a contribution made within the preceding five years, including earnings on those contributions. §529 plans track this via dollar-basis accounting: each contribution is tagged with its date, earnings are pro-rata allocated across all contributions, and the rollover-eligible corpus at any time equals contributions made before the five-year window plus earnings on those older-than-5-year contributions. Most major plan administrators issue statements showing eligible-vs-restricted balances explicitly.

What earned-income substantiation must the beneficiary provide?

IRC §408A(c)(2)(A) — via §219(f)(1) — requires compensation (earned income) at least equal to the rollover amount. Compensation includes W-2 wages, self-employment net earnings under §1402, and certain scholarship/fellowship taxable amounts under §117. It does not include dividends, interest, capital gains, unemployment, or Social Security. Retain W-2s, 1099-NECs, or self-employment records for each rollover year, at least three years post-rollover per IRC §6501(a) statute-of-limitations discipline.

Can a married beneficiary use §219(c) spousal-IRA rules to support the rollover?

Yes. IRC §219(c) permits a married taxpayer filing jointly to make a spousal IRA contribution using the working spouse's compensation. This applies to §529(c)(3)(E) rollovers: a married beneficiary with zero personal earned income can roll up to the beneficiary's own IRA contribution limit provided the working spouse's compensation supports the combined household IRA contributions. This enables §529-to-Roth-IRA rollovers for young married beneficiaries in graduate programs or with limited personal earnings.

How do you fully utilize the $35,000 lifetime cap through multi-year sequencing?

At 2026 IRA limits ($7,500 under age 50), five separate tax years of maximum-utilization rollovers approximately hit the cap ($7,500 × 4 + $5,000 = $35,000). If the beneficiary reaches age 50 during the sequencing, the $1,100 catch-up capacity accelerates completion. Practical sequencing: verify 15-year clock and 5-year lookback both clear, avoid personal IRA contributions during rollover years (they consume the same limit), execute rollover in highest-earned-income years, and use §219(c) spousal support where the beneficiary has minimal personal compensation.

How does the annual IRA-contribution-limit stack work?

The §529(c)(3)(E) rollover counts against the beneficiary's UNIFIED annual IRA contribution limit under §219(b) — $7,500 for 2026 under age 50, $8,600 for age 50+ per IRS Notice 2025-67. Any Roth or traditional IRA contribution from personal funds in the same year reduces the rollover capacity dollar-for-dollar. Importantly, the AGI phaseouts of §408A(c)(3) for direct Roth IRA contributions do NOT apply to §529(c)(3)(E) rollovers — high-income beneficiaries phased out of direct Roth contributions can still receive the rollover.

What if the beneficiary is a skip person for GST-tax purposes?

The rollover moves funds from a §529 account (where the beneficiary is the completed-gift transferee under §529(c)(2)(A)) to a Roth IRA in the beneficiary's own name. No gift-tax or GST-tax event occurs at the rollover — the funds are already treated as the beneficiary's for §529(c)(2)(A) purposes. The rollover does not consume additional §2632 exemption. Zero-inclusion-ratio status attaching to the original §2503(b) annual-exclusion §529 contribution as a direct skip under §2642(c)(1) carries through to the Roth IRA corpus at that beneficiary's later death.

Can the rollover go to a Roth 401(k) instead of a Roth IRA?

No. IRC §529(c)(3)(E)(i) specifically identifies "a Roth IRA" under §408A as the receiving account. Roth 401(k) accounts under §402A are not eligible. For beneficiaries with employer Roth 401(k) plans and no Roth IRA, the rollover requires first opening a Roth IRA (which can be opened with a small nominal contribution or empty-account establishment) to receive the §529 rollover. Some plan providers offer bundled §529-plus-Roth-IRA products that streamline this administratively.

What if the §529 has more than $35,000 unused after all rollovers?

The $35,000 cap is per beneficiary lifetime. Once reached, further §529 balances cannot be rolled for that beneficiary. Remaining §529 exits: (a) qualified higher-education-expense distributions under §529(c)(3)(A), (b) K-12 tuition up to $10,000/year under §529(c)(7), (c) ABLE-account rollovers under §529A per SECURE 2.0 §128, (d) non-qualified §529(c)(6) distributions taxed on earnings plus 10% additional tax, or (e) §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change to a §529(e)(2) family member with unused college-expense capacity or unused $35,000 Roth-rollover capacity. Option (e) is typically the most tax-efficient.
