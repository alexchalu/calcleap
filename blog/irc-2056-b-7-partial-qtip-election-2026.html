IRC §2056(b)(7) Partial-QTIP Election in 2026: How to Split a Marital Deduction to Preserve DSUE Portability, GST Exemption, and the $15M OBBBA Exemption

A full QTIP election defers estate tax to the second death and wastes the first spouse's exemption. A partial QTIP election — a fractional slice on Schedule M of Form 706 — lets an executor land tax at exactly $0 at both deaths, preserve DSUE portability, and capture GST exemption. Here is the statutory framework, the definite-portion rule, the OBBBA $15M math, three worked case studies, and the 90-day supplemental-election escape hatch that saves an over-elected estate.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 5, 2026 28 min read How we research

The estate tax marital deduction under IRC §2056(a) is unlimited — a decedent can give the surviving spouse any dollar amount of property without triggering federal estate tax. The catch is that whatever the surviving spouse inherits is added to her taxable estate at her later death.[1] Over one generation the marital deduction is not a tax break; it is a tax deferral. The couple pays the same estate tax that a single unmarried decedent would pay, just at the second death instead of the first.

The load-bearing planning move for high-net-worth married couples in 2026 is not to defer estate tax. It is to eliminate it by using both spouses' exemptions — the deceased spouse's exemption at the first death, and the surviving spouse's exemption at the second death. With the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) fixing the exemption at $15,000,000 per decedent in 2026 (permanent, inflation-indexed thereafter), a couple has $30,000,000 of combined shelter to work with.[2] Using both is the difference between paying zero federal estate tax and paying $6,000,000 of it on the second death of a $30M estate.

The Qualified Terminable Interest Property (QTIP) election under IRC §2056(b)(7) is the master mechanism for balancing these two exemptions. And the partial QTIP election — a fractional election under Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i) — is the fine-tuning knob that separates a competent estate plan from an optimized one. This guide is the practitioner-grade deep dive on how the partial election works, when to use it, how to draft the formula clause, how to coordinate it with DSUE portability under §2010(c) and reverse-QTIP GST planning under §2652(a)(3), and what to do when the election is filed wrong.

Why QTIP exists at all: solving the terminable-interest problem

The general rule for the marital deduction is deceptively simple: property that passes to the surviving spouse qualifies for the deduction. The complication is the terminable-interest rule under IRC §2056(b)(1), which disallows the deduction whenever the surviving spouse's interest will fail or terminate on the occurrence of an event or contingency and any other person (other than the surviving spouse) will thereby take an interest in the property.[3]

The terminable-interest rule exists because Congress wanted the marital deduction to defer tax, not to erase it. If a decedent could leave a life estate to his wife with a remainder to his children and still take a marital deduction, the property would escape estate tax at both deaths — deducted from the husband's estate under §2056(a) and not includible in the wife's estate under §2033 because she held only a life interest. The classic terminable-interest rule prevents this outcome by disallowing the marital deduction for any life estate.

Congress created three exceptions to the terminable-interest rule so that a spouse who wanted to defer tax could give the surviving spouse income for life while still controlling the remainder disposition: the estate trust (§2056(b)(5)), the general-power-of-appointment trust (§2056(b)(6)), and the QTIP trust (§2056(b)(7)). The QTIP was added by the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 and is by far the most flexible of the three because it lets the first-spouse decedent name the remainder beneficiary while still getting the deduction — a critical feature in blended families where the first spouse wants to guarantee that the children of a prior marriage will receive the corpus at the surviving spouse's death.[4]

The QTIP bargain in one line

The QTIP election is a bargain the executor strikes with the IRS: we will treat this property as marital-deduction property at the first death (no estate tax now) if you will treat it as includible in the surviving spouse's gross estate at the second death (estate tax at the second death). The mechanic for the includibility at the second death is IRC §2044, which sweeps QTIP-elected property back into the surviving spouse's gross estate.[5]

Section §2044 is not optional. Once an executor makes the QTIP election at the first death, the property is includible in the surviving spouse's estate no matter what the trust document says, no matter how the property has grown, no matter whether the surviving spouse ever received a distribution. The election is irrevocable. This is the load-bearing constraint that makes the partial-election lever powerful — deciding how much to elect at the first death is a decision the executor cannot walk back later.

The qualifying-income-interest-for-life test

For property to be QTIP-eligible under §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), the surviving spouse must have a qualifying income interest for life. This requires that:

  1. The surviving spouse is entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals; and
  2. No person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse during her lifetime.

The regulations under Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(d)(2) treat "all the income" as a fiduciary-accounting concept — the spouse is entitled to all income as defined by the trust's governing instrument (subject to state law), including a state-law reasonable trustee's discretion over principal-versus-income allocations. A trust that grants the spouse a mandatory annual income distribution but permits the trustee to accumulate capital gains inside the trust as principal does qualify. A trust that gives the trustee discretion to withhold ordinary income (dividends, interest, rent) does not qualify.[6]

The regulations also treat certain lifetime powers as fatal to QTIP status:

  • A power in anyone — the surviving spouse, the trustee, or a third party — to distribute principal to a person other than the surviving spouse during her lifetime disqualifies the trust. A power exercisable only after the surviving spouse's death is fine.
  • A power to accumulate ordinary income disqualifies the trust.
  • A power to invest in unproductive property that would defeat the surviving spouse's income right disqualifies the trust unless the spouse can compel the trustee to make the property productive under Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f)(4).

Rev. Rul. 2000-2 and its 2006 replacement

Retirement benefits payable to a QTIP trust present a peculiar wrinkle because the trust's "income" from the IRA is a plan distribution, not a fiduciary-accounting concept. Rev. Rul. 2000-2 originally required that the surviving spouse have both a right to compel the trustee to withdraw at least the required-minimum-distribution amount from the IRA and a right to have all of that RMD distributed out of the trust to her, on the theory that anything less would fail the qualifying-income-interest test.[7]

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 refined this framework with a "reasonable apportionment" analysis: the trust qualifies if it complies with a state statute that adopts one of the safe harbors in Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) — typically a state unitrust statute that pays the surviving spouse between 3% and 5% of the trust corpus annually. Every state has now enacted a form of the Uniform Principal and Income Act or the Uniform Fiduciary Income and Principal Act with these safe-harbor provisions; South Dakota, Alaska, and Delaware are the most flexible.[8]

The partial-election mechanic under §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)

The §2056(b)(7) election is made by the executor on Schedule M of Form 706 by listing the QTIP property and identifying it as elected property. If the executor does nothing, the property is not elected and does not qualify for the marital deduction.

The regulations at Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i) allow the election to cover:

  • The entire QTIP property (a full election), or
  • A fractional or percentage share of the entire property (a partial election), provided that the elected share reflects its proportionate share of income and appreciation.

The critical constraint is the "definite-portion" rule. A partial election is valid only if it covers a definite fractional or percentage share of the property. A pecuniary amount ($5,000,000 of a $10,000,000 trust, for instance) is not a definite portion unless the pecuniary amount is expressed as a formula that produces a determinable fraction as of the date of death.[9]

Why the definite-portion rule exists

A pecuniary election ($5M of a $10M trust) freezes the elected amount at date-of-death dollars. If the trust grows to $15M by the surviving spouse's death, the un-elected $5M share also grows — but only the originally elected $5M is includible in the surviving spouse's estate under §2044. A fractional election (50% of a $10M trust) grows proportionately: 50% of the $15M is included at the second death. The IRS insists on the fractional approach to prevent freeze-play gaming of the appreciation.

Drafting a valid formula clause

The safest drafting of a partial-QTIP election uses a fraction whose numerator is the dollar amount the executor wants to elect and whose denominator is the date-of-death value of the entire QTIP property. Example: "The executor elects to have qualified terminable interest property treatment apply to a fraction of the Trust A property, the numerator of which is $5,000,000 and the denominator of which is the federal estate tax value of the Trust A property as finally determined for federal estate tax purposes."

This construction has three virtues:

  • It is a definite portion. The fraction is determinable from the date-of-death Form 706 valuations, satisfying §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i).
  • It survives valuation adjustments. If the IRS challenges the reported values on audit and revalues the property upward by 10%, the fraction is adjusted automatically because the denominator moves with the revaluation.
  • It survives late-discovered assets. If an asset is discovered after Form 706 is filed and the trust value is restated, the fraction adjusts.

Why you would ever want a partial election

An executor who elects the full QTIP amount defers all estate tax to the second death — a simple, predictable outcome. But full election is almost always suboptimal in 2026, for four independent reasons:

Reason 1: Do not waste the deceased spouse's exemption

Every decedent has an Applicable Exclusion Amount of $15,000,000 in 2026 under §2010(c)(3), as amended by OBBBA §70105.[2] If the entire estate qualifies for the marital deduction, no exemption is used at the first death — the exemption becomes a Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion (DSUE) amount portable to the surviving spouse under §2010(c)(4).

DSUE portability sounds like a complete substitute for lifetime use of the exemption, but it has two structural weaknesses:

  • DSUE does not grow with appreciation. The deceased spouse's $15M exemption transfers as $15M and stays $15M no matter how the assets grow between the two deaths. Using the exemption at the first death by not electing marital treatment on that portion of the estate lets the appreciation on the un-elected corpus escape estate tax entirely. Over a 20-year second-death window at even 5% appreciation, that appreciation is roughly $25M on a $15M base — a $10M federal estate tax difference at 40%.
  • DSUE is lost on remarriage. Under §2010(c)(4)(B), the DSUE available to a surviving spouse is limited to the DSUE from the last deceased spouse. If the surviving spouse remarries and her new spouse dies before her, and the new spouse used all his exemption during life, the original DSUE evaporates. Using the exemption at the first death via a credit-shelter arrangement locks in the shelter regardless of remarriage.[10]

Reason 2: Preserve GST exemption via the reverse-QTIP election

The GST exemption under IRC §2631 is $15,000,000 per deemed transferor in 2026, and unlike the estate-tax exemption it is not portable between spouses. A QTIP election under §2056(b)(7) causes the surviving spouse to be treated as the transferor for GST purposes at her later death, wasting the deceased spouse's GST exemption entirely.

Section §2652(a)(3) fixes this by permitting the executor to make a reverse-QTIP election that treats the deceased spouse as the GST transferor for elected QTIP property, without disturbing the §2056(b)(7) marital-deduction election. This is the only way to preserve the deceased spouse's GST exemption when the assets are inside a QTIP trust.[11]

The reverse-QTIP election is all-or-nothing — it applies to all elected QTIP property in a given trust or none of it. The partial-election lever operates upstream: by electing only a fractional QTIP share equal to (or less than) the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption, the executor ensures that the reverse-QTIP election can capture 100% of the elected corpus for GST purposes without hitting the exemption ceiling.

Reason 3: Coordinate with a decoupled state estate tax

Sixteen states plus the District of Columbia impose an independent estate or inheritance tax in 2026, and most of them have exemption amounts far below the federal $15M. Massachusetts and Oregon exempt only $2M and $1M respectively; New York exempts $7.16M (2026); Illinois exempts $4M; Washington exempts $2.193M. Connecticut is highest at $15.2M in 2026.[12]

Some states permit a separate state-only QTIP election that does not have to match the federal — New York (NY Tax Law §954(a)(5)), Massachusetts (M.G.L. c. 65C §3A), Illinois (35 ILCS 405/2), Maryland (Md. Tax-Gen. §7-309), and a handful of others. Other states force federal-state QTIP conformity, which means a federal partial election that leaves an un-elected share exposed to federal tax also leaves the un-elected share exposed to state tax at the first death.

Executors in decoupled states must model the joint federal-state result before making the federal election. A common pattern is to make a federal partial election that uses only the state exemption at the first death, and separately make a full state QTIP election to defer state tax on any state-taxable excess — but this pattern only works in states that permit non-conforming state QTIP.

Reason 4: Correct a stale formula clause after OBBBA

Most wills and trusts drafted before July 4, 2025 used a formula clause tied to the "applicable exclusion amount as in effect at date of death." Those formulas were drafted assuming the TCJA-era exemption ($13,990,000 for 2025 decedents) would sunset to approximately $7,000,000 on January 1, 2026. When OBBBA §70105 permanently fixed the exemption at $15,000,000 for 2026 and made it inflation-indexed thereafter, thousands of formula clauses across the country either:

  • Over-funded the credit-shelter share. A formula that funds the credit-shelter with "an amount equal to the applicable exclusion amount" now funds $15M into the credit shelter — which may exhaust the entire estate for smaller-net-worth decedents and leave nothing for the surviving spouse.
  • Under-funded the marital share. A companion residuary marital gift now receives zero when the credit shelter absorbs the entire estate, potentially leaving the surviving spouse without the intended income stream.
  • Produced unintended state-tax exposure. A credit-shelter funded to the federal $15M in a state with a $2M exemption creates $13M of state-taxable property at the first death.

A partial QTIP election is one of the primary remedies. If the credit-shelter is over-funded by the formula, the executor can direct the excess into the QTIP trust and make a partial election calibrated to the amount actually needed to zero out estate tax after the deceased spouse's remaining exemption.

Coordinating with DSUE portability under §2010(c)

The Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion (DSUE) amount is defined in §2010(c)(4) as the lesser of (A) the basic exclusion amount ($15M in 2026) or (B) the excess of the basic exclusion amount over the amount used against the deceased spouse's tentative estate tax. In plain English: whatever exemption the deceased spouse does not use at the first death becomes DSUE portable to the surviving spouse — if a timely and complete Form 706 is filed.

The partial QTIP election is the lever that controls how much exemption is used vs. how much becomes DSUE. A worked scenario for a $30M combined estate:

You must file Form 706 to preserve DSUE

Under Treas. Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(1), DSUE portability is available ONLY if a complete and timely Form 706 is filed for the deceased spouse's estate. An estate below the federal filing threshold (currently $15M) has no independent obligation to file under §6018, but foregoing the "portability-only" Form 706 forfeits DSUE. Every executor of a married decedent should file even a $2M "portability-only" return — the filing is a low-fee protection against a much larger tax at the second death if the surviving spouse later accumulates wealth or inherits from a third party.

The reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3)

The Generation-Skipping Transfer (GST) tax under Chapter 13 of the Internal Revenue Code imposes a flat 40% tax on transfers to a beneficiary two or more generations below the transferor. Every individual has a GST exemption under §2631 ($15M in 2026, indexed with the estate-tax exemption).[13]

For QTIP-elected property, §2652(a)(1)(A) treats the surviving spouse as the transferor for GST purposes at her later death. This creates an obvious asymmetry: the surviving spouse's $15M GST exemption is available to shelter the QTIP corpus from the GST tax at her death, but the deceased spouse's $15M GST exemption is entirely wasted (there is no property in his estate for it to be applied against, because the QTIP property is deducted from his estate under §2056(b)(7)).

The reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) fixes this by allowing the executor to treat the deceased spouse as the transferor for GST purposes for elected QTIP property, without disturbing the §2056(b)(7) marital-deduction election. The mechanic:

  1. Executor files Form 706 and makes the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on Schedule M for the trust (or a fractional share of it).
  2. Executor makes the reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R-1 of Form 706 for the same property.
  3. Executor allocates deceased spouse's GST exemption on Schedule R to reduce the inclusion ratio on the reverse-QTIP property to zero.

The reverse-QTIP is all-or-nothing per QTIP trust — the executor cannot make it apply to a fraction of an elected QTIP. The upstream planning move is therefore to separate the QTIP trust into two trusts (one that will receive the reverse-QTIP election and be fully exempt from GST, and one that will not receive the reverse-QTIP and will be a "non-exempt" trust for GST purposes). This separation is typically done in the will or trust document, not by post-mortem drafting.

A partial §2056(b)(7) election combined with the trust separation gives the executor three lever positions:

  • Elect the full trust and reverse-QTIP the full trust — uses zero of the deceased spouse's estate-tax exemption but all $15M of his GST exemption on the QTIP corpus.
  • Elect a partial share and reverse-QTIP that share only — uses some of the deceased spouse's estate-tax exemption on the un-elected share and applies his GST exemption only to the elected share.
  • Elect the full trust and do NOT reverse-QTIP — the surviving spouse becomes the GST transferor; the deceased spouse's GST exemption is wasted.

Three worked case studies for 2026 decedents

His will directs $10M outright to his spouse Sarah (age 68) and $20M into a QTIP trust for Sarah's benefit, with the remainder to their three children. James has made no lifetime taxable gifts. Sarah's own separate assets total $2M as of James's death.

The partial-election lever is what lets the executor calibrate exactly how much to load into the credit shelter — larger un-elected share = more appreciation escapes tax, but also more risk that the un-elected share exceeds the deceased spouse's exemption and triggers first-death tax.

Case 2: The $50M estate with GST layer

Marcus dies July 8, 2026, leaving a $50M estate. His will directs $5M outright to his spouse Diana (age 62), $15M into a credit-shelter trust for Diana's lifetime benefit with remainder to their two adult children, and $30M into a QTIP trust for Diana's benefit with the remainder in continuing trust for their four grandchildren (a generation-skipping remainder). Diana's own assets total $3M.

Because the QTIP trust remainder passes to grandchildren, it is GST-sensitive. Without a reverse-QTIP election, Diana becomes the GST transferor at her death for the entire QTIP corpus — wasting Marcus's $15M GST exemption entirely.

Optimal 2026 election:

  1. Full §2056(b)(7) election on the $30M QTIP trust (defers estate tax to Diana's death).
  2. Reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) on the entire QTIP trust (Marcus is the GST transferor).
  3. Allocate Marcus's $15M GST exemption on Schedule R against the QTIP corpus (produces a 50% GST inclusion ratio on the trust — the $15M exempt half will pass to the grandchildren GST-free, the other $15M will be subject to GST tax at Diana's death).
  4. The credit-shelter trust already uses Marcus's $15M estate-tax exemption. Marcus's federal estate tax at first death: $0.

A partial-QTIP alternative: elect only a $15M fractional share (50%) of the QTIP trust, so the elected portion exactly matches Marcus's remaining GST exemption after any lifetime allocations. The un-elected $15M portion falls into the credit-shelter treatment — but Marcus's $15M estate-tax exemption is already used on the freestanding $15M credit-shelter trust, so the un-elected $15M of QTIP corpus is now taxable at the first death (40% × $15M = $6M tax). The partial election is not the right move in Case 2 because Marcus's credit-shelter trust already absorbed his estate-tax exemption; the marginal QTIP dollar has no credit-shelter shelter available.

The lesson: coordination between the credit-shelter trust, the QTIP trust, and the GST exemption must be modeled upfront. The partial-QTIP election is powerful only when there is remaining estate-tax exemption available to shelter the un-elected share.

Case 3: The stale formula clause

Priya dies September 20, 2026, leaving a $12M estate. Her will (drafted in 2019) contains a classic "credit-shelter formula" that reads:

Article IV — Credit-Shelter Trust "To the trustee of the Priya Family Credit-Shelter Trust, an amount equal to the largest amount that can pass free of federal estate tax by reason of the unified credit under §2010, reduced by the value of any property passing outside this Will that uses any portion of the unified credit; and to my spouse Ravi, outright, all the residue of my estate."

Under the pre-OBBBA scheduled sunset, the 2026 exemption was projected to be about $7M. The formula was drafted expecting the credit-shelter trust to receive about $7M and the residuary marital gift to Ravi to receive about $5M. Under OBBBA §70105, the 2026 exemption is $15M — so the formula funds the entire $12M estate into the credit-shelter trust, leaving Ravi with $0 outright.

The stale formula produces a defensible-in-theory but shocking-in-practice outcome. Ravi expected to inherit half the estate outright; Priya's children (from a prior marriage) are the credit-shelter remainder beneficiaries.

Post-mortem fixes for the stale formula:

  1. Qualified disclaimer by Ravi under IRC §2518. Ravi can disclaim the residuary marital gift within nine months of Priya's death, causing the disclaimed property to pass under state intestacy — which typically flows back into the credit-shelter trust or a separate testamentary trust. This does not fix the "Ravi gets nothing" problem; it makes it worse.
  2. Judicial reformation of the will under state law to conform the credit-shelter cap to the drafter's intent (pre-OBBBA numbers). Available in most states but requires evidence of intent and consent of all beneficiaries. Ravi and the children may not agree.
  3. Convert the entire credit-shelter share into a QTIP-eligible trust and make a full QTIP election. If the credit-shelter trust document happens to satisfy the qualifying-income-interest-for-life test under §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) — mandatory income to Ravi for life, no lifetime power to distribute principal to anyone but Ravi — the executor can retroactively convert the credit-shelter to a QTIP by making the §2056(b)(7) election on Schedule M. The property qualifies for the marital deduction, no first-death tax, Ravi gets the income for life, Priya's exemption becomes DSUE portable to Ravi. This is the cleanest fix if the trust document permits it.
  4. Partial QTIP election. If the credit-shelter trust does qualify as QTIP-eligible, the executor can elect a partial share to balance the outcome. Example: elect 40% of the $12M ($4.8M) as QTIP marital-deduction property to give Ravi a partial income right and defer that portion of tax; leave the remaining 60% ($7.2M) un-elected against Priya's remaining exemption. This uses $7.2M of Priya's $15M exemption; the remaining $7.8M becomes DSUE for Ravi.

The lesson: post-mortem partial-QTIP elections are the practitioner's most powerful tool for correcting stale formula clauses. Every executor should read the will against the current exemption before drafting Schedule M.

The deadline, extensions, and the supplemental-election escape hatch

The QTIP election must be made on Form 706, which is due nine months from the date of death under §6075(a). An automatic six-month extension is available under Treas. Reg. §20.6081-1 by filing Form 4768 before the original due date, extending the filing deadline to fifteen months from date of death.[14]

Once the election is made and the return is filed, the election is irrevocable — but there are three narrow escape hatches:

Supplemental election on a timely-filed return

If the original Form 706 is filed timely and the executor discovers within the return period that too much or too little was elected, Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(h) Example 6 permits a supplemental election on an amended Form 706 filed within the return period (through the extended deadline). This is the most common "escape hatch" and is available in every state.

Rev. Proc. 2016-49 revocation of unnecessary election

Rev. Proc. 2016-49 superseded the earlier Rev. Proc. 2001-38 framework for revoking an unnecessary QTIP election (i.e., one that was made but was not needed to reduce estate tax to zero). Under the 2016 procedure, the executor may request revocation only if:

  • The estate's federal estate tax liability is zero regardless of the election, AND
  • The DSUE amount is unaffected by the election, AND
  • No portability election is being made or has been made under §2010(c)(5).

The third condition is the trap. Every married-decedent estate is well-advised to make a portability election, which foreclosed the Rev. Proc. 2001-38 revocation for most estates. Rev. Proc. 2016-49 continues to withhold revocation from estates that have made a portability election. Practitioners now generally treat the QTIP election as fully irrevocable once made.[15]

Estate of Palumbo / Reformation route

If the QTIP election itself was made based on a factual mistake (misidentified property, transcription error, wrong tax value), some circuits recognize a state-law reformation remedy that reforms the underlying trust document to match the executor's intent, bringing the wrong-property election into alignment. Estate of Palumbo v. United States, 675 F.3d 234 (3d Cir. 2012), is the leading authority, though the case is narrowly cabined and has been rejected by other circuits. Do not plan on reformation as an escape hatch.

The §2519 disposition trap

QTIP-elected property is subject to a peculiar gift-tax rule under IRC §2519 that treats any disposition of any part of the surviving spouse's qualifying income interest as a gift of the entire remainder of the QTIP corpus. The intent is to prevent the surviving spouse from stripping the QTIP corpus during her lifetime and thereby escaping §2044 includibility at her death.[16]

The §2519 rule is broad. Any of the following triggers a deemed gift of the entire QTIP corpus (net of the income interest actuarially valued under §7520):

  • The surviving spouse disclaims a portion of the QTIP under §2518 (unless the disclaimer is timely and satisfies all technical requirements).
  • The surviving spouse consents to a distribution of principal from the QTIP to someone other than herself.
  • The trustee, with the surviving spouse's assent, moves the QTIP into a different trust with different remainder beneficiaries.
  • The surviving spouse enters into any transaction that reduces her income interest.

The §2519 exposure is a hidden cost of the QTIP election. A partial election reduces the exposure proportionally — a 50% QTIP election makes only 50% of the trust subject to §2519 — but the exposure remains real. Practitioners advising QTIP surviving spouses should treat any distribution of trust principal to a non-spouse beneficiary as a §2519-triggering event and model the deemed-gift tax before consenting.

State-tax nuances and the separate-QTIP option

Sixteen states plus D.C. impose their own estate or inheritance tax in 2026, and roughly half of them permit a state-only QTIP election that does not have to match the federal. The mismatch options are important because a federal partial election coordinated only for federal tax may inadvertently trigger state estate tax at the first death on the un-elected portion.

Massachusetts example

Massachusetts allows a separate state QTIP election under M.G.L. c. 65C §3A. A Massachusetts decedent with a $10M estate could file the federal Form 706 with a partial federal QTIP election (say 20%, electing $2M as marital and leaving $8M un-elected against the federal $15M exemption — federal tax $0), while simultaneously making a full Massachusetts QTIP election on the state return (electing 100% of the QTIP trust as Massachusetts marital-deduction property to defer the state's $10M × ~16% ≈ $1.6M tax to the second death). Without the state-only election option, the executor would face an ugly choice: waste state exemption at the first death to match the federal partial, or over-elect federally to defer state tax and waste the deceased spouse's federal exemption.

Drafting checklist for the trust document

A QTIP trust that is eligible for a partial election must be drafted to meet the qualifying-income-interest-for-life test on the elected share. Best practice is to draft the trust with these features:

  • Mandatory income distribution to the surviving spouse at least annually, defined by reference to state fiduciary-accounting rules or a unitrust percentage (3-5%) under a state safe-harbor statute.
  • No lifetime power in anyone — including the surviving spouse — to distribute principal to anyone other than the surviving spouse.
  • Trustee power to compel unproductive property to be made productive at the surviving spouse's request under Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f)(4).
  • Severance authority for the trustee to divide the trust into a QTIP-elected trust and a non-QTIP-elected trust after the executor's Schedule M election, so that GST and administration are cleaner.
  • State situs in a jurisdiction with modern trust law (South Dakota, Delaware, Nevada, Alaska) if the surviving spouse's income needs are variable and unitrust flexibility is desired.
  • No prohibition on making a partial election. Some older trust documents contain language directing the executor to make a full election, which arguably prevents partial election even when tax planning calls for it. Modern drafting expressly authorizes partial election.

Six most-expensive mistakes with partial-QTIP elections

  1. Failing to file Form 706 at all. DSUE is unavailable without a timely and complete Form 706. Every married-decedent estate should file even a "portability-only" return to preserve DSUE.
  2. Making a pecuniary election instead of a formula fractional election. A pecuniary election ($5M of a $10M trust) is not a definite portion under Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i) and can be treated by the IRS as invalid.
  3. Over-electing on the marital deduction. A full QTIP election wastes the deceased spouse's exemption, converts it to DSUE that does not appreciate, and produces materially more federal tax at the second death when there is meaningful appreciation between deaths.
  4. Missing the reverse-QTIP election. A §2056(b)(7) election without a §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election means the deceased spouse's GST exemption is wasted entirely on QTIP property with skip-person remainder beneficiaries.
  5. Ignoring state estate tax in decoupled states. A federal partial election that leaves an un-elected share exposed to first-death federal tax also leaves the un-elected share exposed to state estate tax in most decoupled states, unless a state-only QTIP is available and used.
  6. Consenting to a principal distribution triggering §2519. Any post-death disposition of any part of the surviving spouse's income interest is treated as a gift of the entire remainder under §2519. Surviving spouses should consult counsel before consenting to any trust modification.

An 8-item pre-election checklist for the executor

  1. Get an appraisal of every asset in the estate to date-of-death fair market value before drafting Schedule M. The partial-election fraction is meaningless without a defensible denominator.
  2. Confirm remaining exemption for the deceased spouse: $15,000,000 for 2026 decedents under §2010(c)(3), reduced by any lifetime taxable gifts on Form 709.
  3. Confirm the trust document permits a partial election and satisfies the qualifying-income-interest-for-life test under §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).
  4. Model the appreciation projection to the surviving spouse's expected death (life expectancy under §7520 or 20-year default). Larger un-elected share = more appreciation escapes tax but larger first-death exposure if un-elected exceeds exemption.
  5. Consider GST layer and reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) if any remainder beneficiary is a skip person (grandchild, or unrelated party more than 37.5 years younger).
  6. Confirm state estate tax posture and whether a separate state QTIP election is available in your state.
  7. Draft the formula clause as a fraction with numerator = amount to elect and denominator = date-of-death value as finally determined for federal estate tax purposes.
  8. File Form 706 timely (nine months + Form 4768 six-month extension if needed) with the completed Schedule M partial election, Schedule R-1 reverse-QTIP election if applicable, and the portability election under §2010(c)(5). Attach the trust document as required by the Schedule M instructions.

Frequently asked questions

What is a partial-QTIP election under IRC §2056(b)(7)?

A partial-QTIP election is the executor's decision to treat only a fractional or percentage share of qualified terminable interest property as marital-deduction property under IRC §2056(b)(7)(B)(iv), leaving the remaining share taxable at the first death. Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i) requires that the elected share be a "definite portion" — either a fractional/percentage share of the entire property or the entire property, but not a specific pecuniary amount unless a formula makes the amount definite. The election is made on Schedule M of Form 706 and is irrevocable once the return is filed.

Why would an executor make a partial QTIP election instead of a full one?

The most common 2026 reason is to fund the deceased spouse's remaining exemption at the first death so that the OBBBA $15,000,000 exemption is not wasted. A full QTIP election puts the entire trust into the surviving spouse's taxable estate (deferring but not eliminating tax on the growth); a partial election lets the executor use exactly enough of the deceased spouse's exemption at the first death to zero out federal estate tax and preserve DSUE portability while sheltering the balance from the surviving spouse's later estate. It also enables reverse-QTIP planning under §2652(a)(3) to preserve GST exemption.

What is the definite-portion rule for partial QTIP elections?

Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(b)(2)(i) requires that a partial QTIP election cover either the entire property or a fractional or percentage share of the entire property that reflects its post-election value. A pecuniary amount ($5,000,000, for example) is not a definite portion unless a formula ties it to the value of the property in a way that produces a determinable fraction as of the date of death. The safest drafting is a fraction whose numerator is the amount the executor wants to elect and whose denominator is the date-of-death value of the trust corpus.

Does the OBBBA $15M exemption change how partial-QTIP elections are used?

Yes. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), enacted July 4, 2025, made the increased Applicable Exclusion Amount permanent at $15,000,000 per decedent for 2026 (indexed annually thereafter). Before OBBBA, the increased exemption was scheduled to sunset to roughly $7,000,000 on January 1, 2026, and many QTIP trusts were drafted with formula clauses assuming the sunset. In 2026, those formulas either produce a much larger QTIP than the drafter intended (over-funding the marital share) or, in some cases, produce a zero QTIP because the credit shelter absorbs everything. Executors should review every formula clause against the OBBBA numbers before making an election.

How does a partial QTIP election interact with DSUE portability?

Property elected as QTIP under §2056(b)(7) qualifies for the marital deduction and does not use any of the deceased spouse's exemption. Property NOT elected as QTIP (the un-elected share of a partial election, or a separate credit-shelter share) uses the deceased spouse's exemption at the first death. Any exemption not used at the first death becomes the deceased spousal unused exclusion (DSUE) amount that transfers to the surviving spouse under IRC §2010(c)(4), but only if a timely and complete Form 706 is filed under Treas. Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(1). The partial election is the lever that sets how much exemption is used vs. how much becomes DSUE.

What is a reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3)?

A reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3) lets the executor treat the QTIP trust's grantor as the deceased spouse (rather than the surviving spouse) for GST-tax purposes only, while the §2056(b)(7) marital-deduction election remains intact. This is the mechanism for allocating the deceased spouse's GST exemption to the QTIP corpus, which is otherwise wasted because a surviving-spouse QTIP would use the surviving spouse's GST exemption at the second death. The reverse-QTIP election is separate from the §2056(b)(7) election, must be made on the same Form 706, and cannot be partial — it applies to the entire elected QTIP or none of it.

When is the deadline to make a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election?

The election must be made on the estate tax return (Form 706), which is due nine months from the date of death under IRC §6075(a). An automatic six-month extension is available under Treas. Reg. §20.6081-1 by filing Form 4768 before the original due date. Once made, the election is irrevocable. However, if the return was filed timely and the executor determines that too much or too little was elected, Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-7(h) Example 6 permits a supplemental election on an amended Form 706 filed within the return period — commonly the 15-month combined window. The 2001 Rev. Proc. 2001-38 revocation window for over-elected QTIPs was superseded by Rev. Proc. 2016-49, which requires the executor to show hardship.

Can I make a partial QTIP election on a will-based marital trust or does it have to be a separate QTIP?

The §2056(b)(7) election is available for any property in which the surviving spouse holds a qualifying income interest for life, regardless of whether the vehicle is a testamentary QTIP trust, an inter vivos QTIP trust (funded during life via IRC §2523(f)), a QTIP created by disclaimer under §2518, or even a QTIP-eligible legal life estate. What matters is that the property meets the qualifying-income-interest-for-life test under §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii): the surviving spouse must be entitled to all income for life, payable at least annually, with no person having power to appoint the property to anyone other than the surviving spouse during her lifetime.

How does a partial-QTIP election work with a decoupled state estate tax?

Sixteen states plus D.C. impose their own estate or inheritance tax as of 2026, and most of them use exemption amounts well below the federal $15M — Massachusetts and Oregon are $2M and $1M respectively, and New York is $7.16M (2026). If the state has a lower exemption than the federal, a full federal QTIP election that also qualifies for the state's marital deduction can defer state tax entirely; a partial federal election that leaves an un-elected share may trigger state estate tax at the first death on that un-elected share. Some states allow separate state-only QTIP elections — New York (NY Tax Law §954(a)(5)) and Massachusetts (M.G.L. c. 65C §3A) do; other states force federal-state conformity. Executors in decoupled states must model both taxes before electing.

What is the biggest mistake executors make with partial QTIP elections?

The single most expensive mistake is failing to file Form 706 at all when the estate is below the filing threshold. Under Treas. Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(1), DSUE portability is only available if a complete and timely Form 706 is filed — and an estate below the filing threshold has no independent filing obligation under §6018. Every executor of a married decedent's estate should file a "portability-only" Form 706 to preserve DSUE, even when the estate is $2M and no tax is due. The second-most-expensive mistake is over-electing on the marital deduction, which wastes the deceased spouse's exemption on property that will be re-taxed at the survivor's death — a partial election calibrated to the deceased spouse's remaining exemption is almost always better.
