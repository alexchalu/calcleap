IRC §170(c) Charitable Deduction for Individual Educational and Medical Support in 2026: The §501(c)(3) Qualifying-Organization Test, the "To" vs. "For-the-Use-of" Distinction, the §170(b) AGI Ceilings, the §170(f)(8) Contemporaneous-Written-Acknowledgment Rule, and the Coordination with §2503(e) Direct-Pay and §2513 Split-Gift Elections

The two prior pieces in this arc walked the gift-tax side of intra-family educational and medical support. IRC §2503(e) walked the unlimited gift-tax exclusion available when a donor pays tuition directly to a school or medical bills directly to a provider — no gift-tax return, no exemption cost. IRC §2513 walked the doubling mechanic that lets a married couple treat any third-party gift as made half by each. Both provisions live in Chapter 12 of the Code (gift tax); neither touches the donor's Form 1040 income-tax return. This piece walks the income-tax counterpart: IRC §170(c), the general charitable-contribution deduction, and the surprisingly narrow window through which a donor can deduct educational or medical support given to another individual. The short version: you can never deduct a payment made directly to an individual, no matter how needy — a §170(c) deduction requires routing through a §501(c)(3) qualifying charity. This piece covers the §170(c)(2) organization test, the "to" vs. "for-the-use-of" distinction that fixes the applicable §170(b) AGI ceiling (60%/50%/30%/20%), the §170(f)(8) contemporaneous-written-acknowledgment rule for $250+ contributions, the §170(f)(11) qualified-appraisal rule for property gifts over $5,000, the OBBBA §70424 §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor taking effect for the 2026 tax year, the OBBBA §70425 §170(o) $1,000/$2,000 above-the-line allowance for non-itemizers, and the coordination with §2503(e) direct-pay and §2513 split-gift elections in a coordinated family-giving plan.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 31, 2026 25 min read How we research

The §170(c) charitable-contribution deduction is the largest itemized deduction in the federal income-tax base by aggregate dollar volume, with the IRS Statistics of Income reporting $217 billion of individual §170 contributions on tax year 2023 returns (the most recent published data as of August 2026).[1] The provision has been a mainstay of the individual income tax since 1917, when Congress added it to the Revenue Act to encourage private philanthropy amid wartime rate increases. The current §170 has been reshaped by four major statutes: the 1969 Tax Reform Act (private-foundation percentage limits), the 1984 DEFRA reforms (§170(f)(8) contemporaneous-written-acknowledgment for $250+ gifts, added in 1993 by OBRA), the 2006 Pension Protection Act (donor-advised-fund rules under §4966 and §170(f)(17) small-gift substantiation), and the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which added the 0.5%-of-AGI floor under new §170(p) and the $1,000/$2,000 non-itemizer above-the-line allowance under new §170(o) — both effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.[2]

For the family-giving practitioner, §170(c) sits alongside §2503(e) and §2513 as the third leg of the intra-family transfer stool. Each addresses a distinct problem: §2503(e) removes tuition and medical support from the gift-tax base entirely (unlimited, no return); §2513 doubles the gift-tax capacity available to a married couple for other kinds of transfers (annual exclusion, §529 front-load, GST exemption); §170(c) provides the income-tax deduction for a contribution — but only when routed through a §501(c)(3) charity, not to the individual directly. The three provisions do not stack on the same dollar. They complement each other in a coordinated plan, and the planner's job is to allocate each dollar to the provision that delivers the best after-tax result for the family's total giving budget.

The §170(c) statutory framework and the §501(c)(3) qualifying-organization test

IRC §170(c) defines "charitable contribution" for purposes of the §170(a) deduction. The definition is a list of five permitted donee categories:[3]

IRC §170(c) — Charitable contribution defined

A contribution or gift to or for the use of — (1) A State, a possession of the United States, or any political subdivision of any of the foregoing, or the United States or the District of Columbia, but only if the contribution or gift is made for exclusively public purposes; (2) A corporation, trust, or community chest, fund, or foundation — (A) created or organized in the United States or in any possession thereof, or under the law of the United States, any State, the District of Columbia, or any possession of the United States; (B) organized and operated exclusively for religious, charitable, scientific, literary, or educational purposes, or to foster national or international amateur sports competition, or for the prevention of cruelty to children or animals; (C) no part of the net earnings of which inures to the benefit of any private shareholder or individual; and (D) which is not disqualified for tax exemption under section 501(c)(3) by reason of attempting to influence legislation; (3) A post or organization of war veterans; (4) In the case of a contribution or gift by an individual, a domestic fraternal society, order, or association, operating under the lodge system, but only if such contribution or gift is to be used exclusively for religious, charitable, scientific, literary, or educational purposes, or for the prevention of cruelty to children or animals; (5) A cemetery company owned and operated exclusively for the benefit of its members.

Category (2) — §170(c)(2) — is the workhorse. It reaches "a corporation, trust, or community chest, fund, or foundation" organized in the United States and operated exclusively for religious, charitable, scientific, literary, or educational purposes. This is functionally equivalent to a §501(c)(3) organization: the §170(c)(2)(B) purposes list mirrors the §501(c)(3) purposes list, the §170(c)(2)(C) private-inurement bar mirrors §501(c)(3), and the §170(c)(2)(D) legislative-influence limit mirrors the §501(c)(3) lobbying limit. In practice, if the donee is a §501(c)(3) organization listed in IRS Publication 78 (the Tax Exempt Organization Search database at apps.irs.gov/app/eos), the §170(c)(2) test is satisfied.[4]

What is NEVER a §170(c) qualifying donee: an individual. Treas. Reg. §1.170A-1(g) makes this explicit and categorical: "No deduction is allowable under section 170 for a contribution to a specific individual." The rule applies regardless of the individual's need, the humanitarian purpose of the transfer, the donor's motive, or the documentation of the individual's circumstances. A donor who sends $10,000 to a laid-off neighbor's GoFundMe to pay for cancer treatment receives no §170(c) deduction — the neighbor is an individual, not a §170(c)(2) charity. A donor who pays the tuition of a specific promising student attending a public university receives no §170(c) deduction — the student is an individual. A donor who contributes to a scholarship fund at their alma mater with instructions that the money be used for a designated student receives no §170(c) deduction — Rev. Rul. 62-113 and Rev. Rul. 79-81 hold that a "designated-recipient" gift is treated as a gift to the individual regardless of the charity's role as intermediary.[5]

The "designated recipient" trap is the single most common §170(c) failure for family-support giving

Donors routinely try to get a §170(c) deduction for support of a specific individual — a niece's college tuition, a family friend's medical bills, a coworker's rent — by routing the payment through a §501(c)(3) charity with instructions that the money be applied to the designated person. This does not work. Rev. Rul. 62-113, Rev. Rul. 79-81, and consistent Tax Court practice hold that when the donor specifies the individual recipient, the charity is a mere conduit and the transfer is treated as a non-deductible gift directly to the individual. To get a §170(c) deduction, the charity must have "full control and administrative discretion" over the disposition of the funds — the charity, not the donor, must decide which individuals benefit. This is the "control and discretion" doctrine of Rev. Rul. 66-79. It is possible to make a §170(c)-deductible contribution to a scholarship fund from which your niece could win an award on the merits alongside other candidates in a charitable class — but only if the charity's selection is genuinely independent.

The "to" vs. "for the use of" distinction and the §170(b) AGI ceilings

§170(c) opens with "A contribution or gift to or for the use of" a qualifying organization. The distinction between "to" and "for the use of" is not merely stylistic — it fixes the §170(b) AGI ceiling that applies to the deduction. A "to" contribution — cash or property transferred directly into the charity's operating funds — qualifies for the higher of the two applicable ceilings under §170(b). A "for the use of" contribution — most commonly a transfer in trust with the charity as beneficiary — is limited to 30% of AGI regardless of donee type under §170(b)(1)(B).[6]

The 60% ceiling for cash to public charities under §170(b)(1)(G) was enacted by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 §11023, effective for tax years 2018-2025, and continued indefinitely by OBBBA §70423 as amended for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. Before 2018 the ceiling was 50%; the temporary CARES Act 2020 provisions allowed up to 100% for certain 2020 and 2021 cash gifts.[7] For 2026, a taxpayer with $200,000 AGI can deduct up to $120,000 of cash contributions to public §501(c)(3) charities, with any excess carrying forward under §170(d)(1) for up to five years.

Public charities under §170(b)(1)(A)

§170(b)(1)(A) enumerates the "50% (now 60% for cash) charities" — the public charities that qualify for the higher AGI ceiling. The list includes churches and conventions of churches (§170(b)(1)(A)(i)); educational institutions with regular faculty, curriculum, and a regularly-enrolled student body (§170(b)(1)(A)(ii)); hospitals and medical research organizations (§170(b)(1)(A)(iii)); state-college endowment associations (§170(b)(1)(A)(iv)); governmental units under §170(c)(1) (§170(b)(1)(A)(v)); organizations that normally receive substantial public support (§170(b)(1)(A)(vi)); and certain agricultural research organizations (§170(b)(1)(A)(ix)).[8] The §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational-institution definition is the same definition that governs the §2503(e)(2)(A) direct-pay tuition exclusion, creating a clean coordination between the income-tax deduction and the gift-tax exclusion for gifts routed through the school.

The OBBBA §70424 §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor and the OBBBA §70425 §170(o) non-itemizer allowance

OBBBA (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) made two structural changes to the individual §170 deduction, both effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025 — the 2026 tax year is the first year the changes apply.[9]

OBBBA §70424 — new IRC §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor. §170(p)(1) provides that "in the case of any taxable year of an individual, the deduction otherwise allowable under this section shall be reduced by 0.5 percent of the taxpayer's adjusted gross income for such taxable year." The floor is a subtraction from the deductible amount — not a threshold that eliminates the deduction below the floor, but a permanent reduction in the deductible amount for every itemizing donor. §170(p)(2) coordinates the floor with the §170(d)(1) five-year carryover: the floor is applied to the current-year total before carryover computation, and any excess carried forward is not re-floored in the carryover year.

The floor is designed to eliminate very small itemized deductions (with an estimated Joint Committee on Taxation ten-year revenue score of $63.5 billion) and simplify the itemization decision for modest donors. For the family-support planner, the practical effect is that small designated-scholarship gifts to public charities lose some of their tax value at the margin — a $1,000 cash contribution at $200,000 AGI is now effectively a $0 deduction, because the floor at that AGI is $1,000. Concentrating giving into fewer, larger contributions preserves more of the deduction relative to the pre-2026 baseline.[10]

OBBBA §70425 — new IRC §170(o) non-itemizer above-the-line allowance. §170(o) provides a limited above-the-line adjustment to gross income under §62(a)(22) for cash contributions to §170(b)(1)(A) public charities made by taxpayers taking the standard deduction under §63(c). The allowance is capped at $1,000 for single filers and $2,000 for married-filing-jointly filers; it is not subject to the §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor; and it is not subject to any §170(b) percentage ceiling. §170(o)(3) coordinates with the itemized deduction: a taxpayer who itemizes cannot double-count contributions between §170(o) and Schedule A. §170(o)(4) restricts the contribution categories to (A) cash contributions (not property), (B) to §170(b)(1)(A) public charities (not private foundations or DAFs), and (C) not to §509(a)(3) supporting organizations.[11]

The §170(o) non-itemizer allowance is the largest 2026 §170 restructuring for modest donors

For 2026, the OBBBA-updated standard deduction under §63(c) is $16,500 (single) / $33,000 (MFJ) / $24,750 (HoH). A married couple with $80,000 of itemizable deductions (mortgage interest, SALT-capped state and local tax, medical, and $2,000 cash charity) will take the standard deduction rather than itemizing — and previously would have received no §170(c) deduction for their $2,000 cash charity. Under new §170(o), they receive the full $2,000 as an above-the-line adjustment, effectively deducting the entire contribution. For itemizing donors ($30,000+ of charitable giving in most cases), the §170(o) allowance is not additive to Schedule A — but for the substantial majority of taxpayers taking the standard deduction, §170(o) creates a new above-the-line lane worth up to $2,000 for MFJ filers annually.

The §170(f)(8) contemporaneous-written-acknowledgment rule and §170(f)(11) qualified-appraisal requirement

The §170 deduction is disallowed for failure of substantiation more often than for any other reason. §170(f)(8), §170(f)(11), and §170(f)(17) collectively impose four levels of substantiation, keyed to contribution size and type:[12]

The §170(f)(8) contemporaneous-written-acknowledgment rule is the most-litigated substantiation requirement. "Contemporaneous" means the acknowledgment must be received by the earlier of (a) the date the taxpayer files the return on which the deduction is claimed, or (b) the due date (including extensions) for filing that return. An acknowledgment obtained after the return is filed is not contemporaneous and does not cure a §170(f)(8) defect — the deduction is disallowed. In Durden v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2012-140, the Tax Court disallowed a $22,517 deduction for cash contributions to a church because the church's initial acknowledgment did not state whether goods or services were provided in exchange; the church issued a corrected acknowledgment after the return was filed, but the Tax Court held the corrected acknowledgment was not contemporaneous. The case is the leading modern authority on the strictness of the §170(f)(8) rule.[13]

The §170(f)(11) qualified-appraisal rule is equally strict. Mohamed v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2012-152, disallowed $18.5 million of real-estate contributions because the taxpayer prepared his own appraisals rather than obtaining independent qualified appraisals. The Tax Court held that self-prepared appraisals do not satisfy §170(f)(11)(E)'s "qualified appraiser" definition, and the taxpayer's substantial-compliance argument was rejected — the qualified-appraisal requirement is a per-se rule, not a facts-and-circumstances test. Publicly-traded securities are exempt from the appraisal requirement under §170(f)(11)(A)(ii)(I) because their FMV is objectively determinable by market close on the contribution date, but a Form 8283 Section A is still required for contributions valued between $500 and $5,000.[14]

Donor-advised funds, private foundations, and scholarship-fund vehicles

For a donor who wants to make §170(c)-deductible contributions targeted at educational or medical support of specific individuals (with charity discretion), three vehicle choices dominate:

Donor-advised funds (DAFs)

A DAF is a fund held by a §501(c)(3) sponsoring organization (typically a community foundation or a commercial sponsor like Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, or Vanguard Charitable) that accepts contributions and permits the donor to advise on subsequent grants. The donor gets a §170(c) deduction in the contribution year at the full 60%-of-AGI cash ceiling (or 30% for appreciated stock); the DAF holds the assets and makes grants over subsequent years at the donor's advisement, but the sponsoring organization retains legal control.[15] The critical §4966 limitation: a DAF cannot make grants to individuals (including scholarship grants to designated recipients), and grants that provide more than an incidental benefit to a donor, donor-advisor, or related party trigger a 20% excise tax on the sponsoring organization plus a 5% excise tax on the donor-advisor under §4967. Under IRS Notice 2017-73 (still the operative guidance as of August 2026), a scholarship grant from a DAF is permissible only if (1) selection is made by a committee that is not controlled by the donor, (2) selection is on an objective and nondiscriminatory basis, and (3) the recipient is not the donor's family member. For most family-support giving to specific individuals, a DAF is not the right vehicle — designated-recipient scholarships to family members are barred.

Private foundations

A private foundation is a §509(a) organization funded typically by a single family or corporation. A private foundation CAN make scholarship grants to individuals under IRC §4945(g), but only under a program approved in advance by the IRS pursuant to §4945(g)(1) (§117 tuition scholarships), §4945(g)(2) (Section 74(b) prizes), or §4945(g)(3) (specific-purpose grants). The advance-approval process requires filing a request with IRS Exempt Organizations detailing the objective-selection criteria, and the selection committee must not be controlled by the donor family. Failure of §4945(g) is a "taxable expenditure" triggering a 20% excise tax on the foundation plus a 5% excise tax on foundation managers who approved the payment. Private foundations are also subject to the §170(b)(1)(B) 30% AGI ceiling for cash contributions (not the 60% public-charity ceiling), the §170(b)(1)(D) 20% ceiling for appreciated-property gifts, and the §4940 net-investment-income excise tax of 1.39%.[16]

Scholarship funds at public charities (community foundations)

Most community foundations (Silicon Valley Community Foundation, New York Community Trust, Chicago Community Trust, Boston Foundation, etc.) offer designated-scholarship funds that are administered by the community foundation as a §501(c)(3) public charity, with the community foundation's own independent scholarship committee selecting recipients from a charitable class. The donor may endow a "Family X Scholarship for Chicago-area high school seniors pursuing STEM degrees" but cannot select individual recipients or restrict selection to family members. The donor receives the full 60%-of-AGI cash ceiling under §170(b)(1)(A) (community foundations are public charities under §170(b)(1)(A)(vi)) and full FMV deduction for appreciated-stock contributions under §170(e)(1)(B). This is typically the most efficient vehicle for a family that wants both (a) meaningful control over the scholarship's charitable purpose, and (b) full §170(c) deduction — trading only the selection of specific recipients (which cannot be a §170(c)-deductible activity in any vehicle) for the charitable class approach.[17]

The three provisions live in different chapters of the Code and serve different tax purposes:

The three do not stack on the same dollar, but they combine in a coordinated family-giving plan. Consider a grandparent making three transfers in 2026 for a grandchild's benefit:

  • Transfer 1: $80,000 tuition paid directly to the grandchild's private medical school (a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational institution). This is a §2503(e)(2)(A) qualified transfer — unlimited gift-tax exclusion, no Form 709, no §2010(c) exemption consumed. There is NO §170(c) income-tax deduction: the payment benefits the grandchild, not the school as an unrestricted donee. There is no §2513 election needed because §2503(e) transfers are not gifts under Chapter 12.
  • Transfer 2: $190,000 §529 contribution to the grandchild's Vanguard 529 Plan, elected under §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging and §2513 spousal gift-splitting. This IS a gift for gift-tax purposes but is fully absorbed by the doubled $38,000/year annual exclusion for the grandchild across 2026-2030. There is NO §170(c) income-tax deduction because a §529 contribution is not a §170(c) qualifying donee (it is an educational-savings account, not a §501(c)(3) organization); some states offer state-tax deductions for §529 contributions but no federal deduction is available.
  • Transfer 3: $50,000 cash contribution to the grandchild's medical school's general scholarship fund (open to all admitted students on need or merit basis, administered by the school's independent scholarship committee). This IS a §170(c) itemized deduction on the grandparent's Schedule A up to 60% of AGI under §170(b)(1)(G), less the §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor for 2026. It is NOT a gift for gift-tax purposes under Reg. §25.2503-3(b) (a contribution to a §501(c)(3) charity is not a taxable gift, whatever the donor's motive), so no Form 709 is filed and no §2503(e) or §2513 analysis is needed.

The three transfers together move $320,000 out of the grandparent's future taxable estate (the two gift-tax-exclusion transfers) and produce $50,000 of income-tax deduction (the §170(c) contribution). No dollar is double-counted; each provision reaches only the transfer it is designed to cover.[18]

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Elena (family-scholarship-fund at community foundation)

Elena, a 62-year-old physician with $650,000 AGI in 2026, wants to establish a scholarship fund honoring her late father — a first-generation immigrant who worked as a hospital orderly — for children of hospital service-workers pursuing nursing degrees. She contributes $250,000 of appreciated Google stock (basis $60,000, held over one year) to the New York Community Trust in December 2026, establishing the "Miguel Rodríguez Scholarship for Children of Hospital Service Workers."

§170(c) treatment:

  • The New York Community Trust is a §170(b)(1)(A)(vi) public charity. The contribution is "to" the charity (unrestricted transfer of the stock into the endowment).
  • The $250,000 FMV is deductible up to 30% of Elena's AGI = $195,000 (2026 limit under §170(b)(1)(C)(i) for long-term-capital-gain property to public charity).
  • Excess $55,000 carries forward under §170(d)(1) for up to five years, retaining 30% character.
  • §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor = $3,250. Applied against Elena's 2026 total §170(c) contributions before ceiling.
  • Deductible 2026: $195,000 − $3,250 = $191,750 (assuming no other 2026 §170(c) contributions and full itemization).
  • Federal tax savings at 37% bracket: $70,948. State (NY) tax savings at 6.85%: $13,135. Combined 2026 income-tax savings: $84,083.
  • NO §170(e)(1)(B) reduction to basis: long-term-capital-gain property to public charity qualifies for full FMV deduction. Avoids $190,000 of otherwise-taxable capital gain that would have been recognized on sale.

Charitable structure: The community foundation's independent scholarship committee selects annual recipients from a charitable class of hospital-service-worker children who apply for the scholarship; Elena has no selection role after the initial gift agreement establishing the fund's charitable purpose. Elena may recommend candidates for consideration but the committee has final authority.

Case 2: Marcus (attempted designated-recipient scholarship — the failure)

Marcus, a 55-year-old software engineer with $340,000 AGI in 2026, wants to help his high-school friend Kai's daughter attend a private university. Kai is unable to afford the $60,000 tuition. Marcus contacts the university's development office and asks whether he can make a $60,000 contribution earmarked specifically for Kai's daughter's tuition; the development office says yes and provides an acknowledgment letter stating "$60,000 contribution to the University in support of the education of [Kai's daughter's name]."

§170(c) treatment:

  • Under Rev. Rul. 62-113 and Rev. Rul. 79-81, a contribution to a §501(c)(3) charity that designates a specific individual as beneficiary is treated as a gift to the individual, not to the charity. The university is a mere conduit.
  • Marcus receives NO §170(c) deduction, notwithstanding the acknowledgment letter and the university's §501(c)(3) status.
  • The $60,000 payment does qualify as a §2503(e)(2)(A) qualified transfer because it is paid directly to the school on Kai's daughter's behalf — no gift-tax return, no §2010(c) exemption consumed.

The alternative that would have worked: Marcus could have contributed $60,000 to the university's general need-based financial-aid fund without designating Kai's daughter. The university's independent financial-aid committee could then have offered a $60,000 need-based grant to Kai's daughter (or to any other qualified student on the same objective basis). Marcus's contribution would be fully §170(c)-deductible; the financial-aid grant to Kai's daughter would be independently determined by the university. But that structure requires Marcus to give up the certainty that his money goes to Kai's daughter — a trade Marcus was unwilling to make. The lesson: §170(c) deductibility and designated-recipient certainty are mutually exclusive.[19]

Case 3: Diana and Robert (medical-hardship charitable-class fund)

Diana and Robert, a retired couple with $180,000 AGI in 2026, want to support cancer patients at their local hospital who face medical-bill hardship. They contribute $8,000 in cash to the hospital's Patient Assistance Fund, a §170(c)(2) fund operated by the hospital (itself a §170(b)(1)(A)(iii) medical-research organization) that pays medical bills for financially-eligible patients selected by the hospital's independent social-work team.

§170(c) treatment:

  • The hospital is a §170(b)(1)(A)(iii) public charity. The Patient Assistance Fund is a §170(c)(2) qualifying donee.
  • The $8,000 cash contribution is deductible up to 60% of AGI = $108,000 (well above contribution amount).
  • §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor for MFJ = $900. Applied to $8,000 leaves $7,100 deductible.
  • Diana and Robert take the OBBBA-updated 2026 MFJ standard deduction of $33,000, since their itemizable deductions (mortgage $0, SALT capped at $10,000, medical below 7.5% floor, charity $7,100) total ~$17,100 which is below the standard deduction.
  • Because they cannot itemize, they cannot claim the $7,100 as a Schedule A §170(c) deduction. But under new §170(o), they can claim $2,000 of the cash contribution as an above-the-line adjustment to gross income under §62(a)(22).
  • Federal tax savings at 22% bracket on the $2,000 above-the-line: $440. Their remaining $6,000 of the contribution provides no federal tax benefit in 2026.
  • Because they are non-itemizers, the §170(p) floor does not apply to the $170(o) above-the-line allowance — they get the full $2,000 benefit.

Charitable outcome: The $8,000 contribution supports approximately three cancer patients' medical-bill hardship over the following year, selected by the hospital's social-work team based on income eligibility and demonstrated need. Diana and Robert have no role in recipient selection; the hospital's independent process ensures §170(c) charitable-class treatment.

Six recurring §170(c) mistakes for individual educational or medical support

  1. Designating the individual recipient. Contributing to a §501(c)(3) with instructions that funds be applied to a specific named individual (a niece, a family friend, a coworker) — the deduction is disallowed under Rev. Rul. 62-113 and Rev. Rul. 79-81. Route the contribution to a charitable-class program administered by an independent committee.
  2. Paying directly to the individual. A GoFundMe contribution, a Venmo transfer to help with a medical bill, a check written to the family — none is §170(c) deductible under Treas. Reg. §1.170A-1(g). If the beneficiary is truly needy, coordinate with a §501(c)(3) hardship-fund that administers assistance on a charitable-class basis.
  3. Missing the §170(f)(8) contemporaneous-written-acknowledgment. For any single contribution of $250 or more, obtain the acknowledgment (stating amount, whether goods/services were provided, and value of any goods/services) BEFORE filing the return. An after-the-fact acknowledgment does not cure the defect — see Durden v. Commissioner.
  4. Ignoring the OBBBA §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor for 2026+. Small charitable gifts that were fully deductible in 2025 may be partially or fully absorbed by the floor for 2026. Concentrate gifts into fewer, larger contributions when possible to preserve deduction efficiency.
  5. Missing the §170(f)(11) qualified-appraisal requirement for property gifts over $5,000. The requirement is a per-se rule — self-prepared appraisals do not qualify (see Mohamed v. Commissioner). Publicly-traded securities are the only exception. Obtain the qualified appraisal within 60 days of the contribution date under Treas. Reg. §1.170A-17(a)(4).
  6. Assuming a §170(c) deduction stacks with §2503(e) direct-pay on the same dollar. The two provisions never overlap. §2503(e) direct-pay is a gift-tax exclusion for payments to schools and medical providers on behalf of specific individuals; §170(c) is an income-tax deduction for contributions to §501(c)(3) charities. A single dollar reaches one or the other, never both.

Eight-item pre-donation checklist for §170(c) educational and medical support giving

  1. Confirm the donee is a §170(c)(2) qualifying organization. Search the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search at apps.irs.gov/app/eos for the donee's EIN and confirm current §501(c)(3) status and public-charity classification under §170(b)(1)(A). Foreign charities generally do not qualify; verify treaty coverage for Canadian, Mexican, and Israeli donees.
  2. Confirm the transfer is NOT a designated-recipient contribution. If you want a §170(c) deduction, the donee charity must have independent discretion over the disposition of the funds. Family-scholarship funds at community foundations, hospital patient-assistance funds, and university general financial-aid funds all satisfy this test; earmarked contributions to a specific individual do not.
  3. Determine the applicable §170(b) AGI ceiling. Cash to public charity = 60%; long-term-capital-gain property to public charity = 30% FMV or 50% at basis election; cash to private foundation = 30%; long-term-capital-gain property to private foundation = 20%; any "for-the-use-of" transfer = 30%.
  4. Compute the OBBBA §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor. Subtract 0.5% × AGI from total §170(c) contributions before applying §170(b) ceilings. For 2026, this is the first year the floor applies. Small contributions may be partially or fully absorbed.
  5. Consider the §170(o) non-itemizer allowance if not itemizing. If the standard deduction ($16,500 single / $33,000 MFJ / $24,750 HoH for 2026) exceeds your total itemizable deductions, take the standard deduction plus up to $1,000 (single) / $2,000 (MFJ) of cash public-charity contributions as an above-the-line adjustment under §62(a)(22).
  6. Obtain the §170(f)(8) contemporaneous written acknowledgment. For every single contribution of $250 or more, obtain the CWA from the donee before filing the return. The CWA must state the amount, whether goods/services were provided, and a description and good-faith estimate of the value of any goods/services provided.
  7. Obtain the §170(f)(11) qualified appraisal for property gifts over $5,000. Publicly-traded securities are the only exception. For property gifts $500-$5,000, attach Form 8283 Section A. For property gifts over $5,000, attach Form 8283 Section B signed by qualified appraiser and donee. For gifts over $500,000, attach the full qualified appraisal.
  8. Coordinate with §2503(e) direct-pay and §2513 gift-splitting in the family plan. The three provisions serve different tax purposes and reach different transfer types. A comprehensive family-support plan uses §170(c) for charitable-class giving, §2503(e) for individual tuition and medical support paid directly to schools and providers, and §2513 for doubling annual-exclusion and §529 capacity between married-spouse donors.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take a §170(c) charitable deduction for a payment made directly to a struggling family?

No. §170(c) requires the donee to be a qualifying organization enumerated in §170(c)(1)-(5) — an individual is never a §170(c) qualifying donee, regardless of need or motive. If you want the deduction, route the payment through a §501(c)(3) hardship or scholarship fund that administers assistance on a charitable-class basis.

What is the difference between a gift "to" a charity and a gift "for the use of" a charity?

The distinction governs the §170(b) AGI ceiling. A gift "to" a public charity qualifies for the highest applicable ceiling (60% cash, 30% or 50% appreciated property). A gift "for the use of" a charity (typically in trust with a future interest passing to the charity) is limited to 30% of AGI regardless of donee type. Treas. Reg. §1.170A-8(a)(2) walks the line.

What is the OBBBA §70424 §170(p) 0.5%-of-AGI floor and when does it take effect?

OBBBA §70424 added new §170(p) imposing a 0.5%-of-AGI reduction to the individual charitable-contribution deduction, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025 — the 2026 tax year is the first year the floor applies. The floor is subtracted from total §170(c) contributions before applying §170(b) ceilings.

Can I direct a donor-advised fund to make a scholarship grant to my grandchild?

No. §4966 prohibits DAF grants to individuals; §4967 prohibits grants that provide more than an incidental benefit to a donor, donor-advisor, or related party. Grandchildren are related parties whose scholarship receipt would trigger both provisions. Use a §501(c)(3) scholarship organization with an independent selection committee open to a charitable class instead.

Does the §170(f)(8) contemporaneous-written-acknowledgment rule apply to all charitable contributions?

Only for single contributions of $250 or more. Under-$250 contributions require §170(f)(17) bank-record substantiation. The §170(f)(8) acknowledgment must be received by the earlier of the return-filing date or the return due date (with extensions); an after-the-fact acknowledgment does not cure the defect.

How does §170(c) coordinate with the §2503(e) direct-pay gift-tax exclusion?

The two provisions serve different tax purposes and do not stack on the same dollar. §2503(e) provides an unlimited gift-tax exclusion for tuition or medical payments made directly to a school or provider on behalf of a specific individual; §170(c) provides an income-tax deduction for contributions to a §501(c)(3) charity. A single dollar reaches one or the other. They complement each other in a coordinated family plan.

Can a §2513-elected married couple double the §170(c) charitable deduction?

No. §2513 is a gift-tax election with no application to the §170 income-tax deduction. A married couple filing jointly aggregates AGI and §170(c) contributions; the §170(b) ceilings apply to joint AGI. A §170(c) contribution is not a gift for gift-tax purposes under Reg. §25.2503-3(b) and does not appear on Form 709.

What documentation do I need for a §170(c) contribution of appreciated stock?

For publicly-traded securities valued $500-$5,000, Form 8283 Section A. For all other property over $5,000, §170(f)(11) requires a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser and Form 8283 Section B. Publicly-traded securities over $5,000 are exempt from the appraisal requirement under §170(f)(11)(A)(ii)(I). For property gifts over $500,000, attach the full appraisal.

Are contributions to foreign charities deductible under §170(c)?

Generally no. §170(c)(2)(A) requires U.S. organization. Narrow treaty exceptions exist for Canadian, Mexican, and Israeli charities. U.S. "friends of" §501(c)(3) organizations that regrant to foreign affiliates under the "discretion and control" doctrine of Rev. Rul. 66-79 remain deductible.

Do I need to itemize to claim a §170(c) deduction?

Traditionally yes — the §170 deduction is an itemized deduction on Schedule A. But new OBBBA §170(o) permits non-itemizers to deduct up to $1,000 (single) or $2,000 (MFJ) of cash contributions to public charities as an above-the-line adjustment under §62(a)(22) for 2026+. The above-the-line allowance is separate from the itemized deduction and not subject to the §170(p) floor.
