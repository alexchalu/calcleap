IRC §2523 Unlimited Spousal Marital Deduction in 2026: The Citizen-Spouse Rule, §2523(f) Lifetime QTIP, §2523(i) Noncitizen Denial, and the $194,000 Enlarged Annual Exclusion

Between two U.S. citizen spouses, gift tax essentially does not exist — the §2523(a) marital deduction is unlimited and stacks on top of every other exclusion in Chapter 12. Cross the citizenship line and the mechanic inverts: the unlimited deduction disappears, an enlarged $194,000 annual exclusion appears, and the §2056A QDOT becomes the estate-side pressure valve. This is the 2026 walkthrough of the section that moves more inter-spousal wealth than any other and traps more planners than any other.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 11, 2026 19 min read How we research

Between two U.S. citizen spouses, IRC §2523(a) grants an unlimited gift-tax marital deduction.[1] One spouse can hand the other a $500 million portfolio and neither owes a dollar of gift tax, neither files a Form 709 return capturing a taxable gift, and neither reduces the $15 million per-person basic exclusion amount that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, Public Law 119-21) made permanent for 2026 under §2010(c)(3).[2] That is the workhorse rule — and it is the reason that among high-net-worth married couples, the estate-tax problem always presents at the second death, not the first.

The mechanic has one enormous exception and several loud footnotes. The exception is §2523(i): when the donee spouse is not a U.S. citizen, the unlimited deduction disappears entirely. In its place, §2523(i)(2) substitutes an enlarged annual exclusion, which in 2026 is $194,000 under Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.42(2).[3] The footnotes are the terminable-interest bar under §2523(b), the §2523(e) power-of-appointment carve-out, the §2523(f) lifetime QTIP election, and the §2523(g) charitable-remainder trust exception — each a distinct rule with its own drafting requirements and its own way of quietly failing.

This guide walks §2523 from the statute forward: the unlimited deduction, the terminable-interest bar and its four statutory workarounds, the noncitizen-spouse pincer of §2523(i) plus §2056A QDOT, the interaction with §2513 gift-splitting and §2010(c) DSUE portability, and three worked case studies. Every dollar figure is the 2026 figure. Every rule traces to primary authority. The §2523 statutory architecture

IRC §2523 is Chapter 12's dedicated marital-deduction section, and it operates in parallel to the estate-tax marital deduction under §2056. The two sections mirror each other closely: §2523 covers lifetime (gift-tax) transfers, §2056 covers testamentary (estate-tax) transfers, and the terminable-interest bars and QTIP escape hatches use nearly identical statutory language.[1]

The nine operative subsections in 2026:

Reading the section straight through, §2523(a) is the rule and §§2523(b) through 2523(i) are, in different ways, either the traps or the escape hatches from the traps. Every planner should be able to spot which subsection they're in from the fact pattern alone.[4]

§2523(a) — the unlimited citizen-spouse deduction

The core rule is one sentence. §2523(a): "Where a donor transfers during the calendar year by gift an interest in property to a donee who at the time of the gift is the donor's spouse, there shall be allowed as a deduction in computing taxable gifts for the calendar year an amount with respect to such interest equal to its value."[1]

The three requirements for §2523(a) to fully deduct a transfer to zero:

  1. A completed gift. The donor must have parted with dominion and control; the transfer must be a "gift" under §2511 and Treas. Reg. §25.2511-1 through §25.2511-2. A revocable transfer or transfer subject to a retained power is not a completed gift and never reaches §2523 analysis.[5]
  2. An interest in property passing to a spouse. The donee spouse must be the donor's spouse at the time of the gift. Post-divorce transfers do not qualify unless they fall under §2516 (property-settlement transfers incident to divorce, which are treated as full consideration and are not gifts at all).
  3. Not a nondeductible terminable interest. If §2523(b) says the interest is a terminable interest and no §2523(e), (f), or (g) exception applies, the deduction is denied and the transfer becomes a taxable gift consuming §2010(c) lifetime exemption.

The three-word summary

Citizen. Present. Vested. If the donee spouse is a U.S. citizen, the gift is completed, and the interest is either outright or vested in ways §2523(e)–(g) blesses, the deduction is unlimited. Everything else is a footnote.

What "spouse" means for §2523

IRC §7701(a)(17) supplies the definition: "spouse" is a person who is a party to a marriage recognized as valid under the law of the jurisdiction in which the marriage was entered into. Following the Supreme Court's decisions in United States v. Windsor, 570 U.S. 744 (2013), and Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), and Rev. Rul. 2013-17, same-sex spouses lawfully married anywhere are "spouses" for all federal tax purposes regardless of the couple's current state of domicile.[6]

Common-law marriage is recognized for §2523 if valid under state law where entered into. Domestic partnerships and civil unions do not qualify (Rev. Rul. 2013-17 explicitly excluded them). A pending divorce that has not been finalized preserves spousal status through the date of the final decree.

Interaction with §1041 income-tax treatment

A completed gift between citizen spouses is not only free of gift tax under §2523(a); it is also free of income tax under IRC §1041, which provides that a transfer of property between spouses (or former spouses incident to divorce) is treated as a gift with carryover basis and holding period.[7] There is no gain recognition at transfer; the recipient spouse takes the transferor's basis under §1015. This lets citizen spouses freely rebalance between themselves for asset-protection, income-shifting, or basis-management purposes without triggering either tax.

Basis-management planning matters: property that carries over between spouses during life foregoes the §1014 date-of-death basis step-up that would otherwise apply to the sicker spouse's estate. The classical asymmetry: gifting from the healthy spouse to the sicker spouse produces a full basis step-up in the sicker spouse's estate (if the sicker spouse predeceases and holds the property at death) — a rule Congress bounded with the §1014(e) one-year lookback so a deathbed gift-and-devise strategy doesn't work if the transferor gets the property back within a year.

The terminable-interest bar under §2523(b)

§2523(b) is the terminable-interest rule and the reason so many well-intentioned transfers between spouses accidentally become taxable gifts. It denies the marital deduction "where, on the lapse of time, on the occurrence of an event or contingency, or on the failure of an event or contingency to occur, such interest transferred to the donee spouse will terminate or fail, and if — (A) the donor retains in himself, or transfers or has transferred (for less than an adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth) to any person other than such donee spouse … an interest in such property, and if by reason of such passing the person other than the donee spouse … may possess or enjoy any part of such property after the termination or failure of the interest so transferred to the donee spouse."[1]

Restated plainly: if the donor gives the spouse an interest that will end (a life estate, a term of years, an interest subject to a condition subsequent), and the property will then pass to someone other than the donee spouse, the marital deduction is denied for the transferred interest. This is the "surplus interest to a third party" bar. It exists to prevent the donor from cake-having-eating — the donor cannot deduct the spousal-transfer value while retaining the ability to direct the remainder to children or third parties tax-free.[8]

Examples of terminable interests that fail §2523(b)

  • Life estate with remainder to children. Donor transfers Blackacre to Spouse for life, remainder to Children. The spousal interest terminates at Spouse's death; property passes to a person other than Spouse. Marital deduction: denied. Result: the actuarial value of the life estate is a taxable gift to Spouse (consuming §2010(c) exemption); the remainder is a taxable gift to Children (also consuming §2010(c) exemption).
  • Interest in trust with distribution conditional on trustee discretion. Trustee may but need not distribute to Spouse; on Spouse's death, corpus passes to Children. Marital deduction denied unless §2523(f) QTIP election is made.
  • Retained income interest. Donor transfers Blackacre and reserves the right to income for life; remainder to Spouse. Because Donor retained the income interest, Spouse's interest is a "future interest" and §2523(a) is unavailable (also failing §2503(b) if it were a nonspousal transfer).

Examples that clear §2523(b) automatically

  • Outright transfer of cash, stock, or real property to spouse in fee simple. No termination; no third party; deduction fully allowed under §2523(a).
  • Joint tenancy with right of survivorship between spouses. §2523(d) treats the survivor's interest as the entire interest for marital-deduction purposes; deduction allowed.
  • Term life insurance on donor's life owned by spouse. Even if the interest could theoretically lapse (donor outlives policy term), the donor cannot retain a beneficial interest in the property, so the transfer is fully deductible.

§2523(e) — the life-estate-with-power-of-appointment exception

§2523(e) is the first statutory workaround to §2523(b). It permits a marital deduction for a transfer that would otherwise be a nondeductible terminable interest if the donee spouse has (1) a life income interest, and (2) a general power of appointment exercisable in favor of the donee spouse or her estate.[1]

The four requirements under §2523(e)(1)–(4):

  1. Income for life. The donee spouse must be entitled to all income for life, payable at least annually.
  2. General power of appointment. The donee spouse must have a general power to appoint the property "to herself or to her estate."
  3. Sole holder. The power must be exercisable "in all events" by the donee spouse alone.
  4. No restriction on exercise. No person can have a power to appoint any part of the property to anyone other than the donee spouse.

Because the donee spouse holds a general power of appointment, the property is includible in her gross estate at death under §2041 — which is exactly what Congress wanted. The marital deduction defers the transfer-tax event to the surviving spouse's death; the general power of appointment ensures the estate tax hits at that later point.[9]

§2523(e) is the historical predecessor to the modern §2523(f) QTIP mechanic. Its practical use has declined because donors typically want to control the remainder — they do not want the surviving spouse to redirect the property away from children. §2523(f) QTIP solved that problem, and §2523(e) is now used mainly for legacy trust structures and simple "everything to spouse with full flexibility" plans.

§2523(f) — the lifetime QTIP election

The §2523(f) lifetime QTIP election is the workhorse of high-value inter-spousal wealth transfer for donors who want to preserve remainder control. It parallels the estate-tax §2056(b)(7) QTIP election in nearly every mechanical respect, but operates in life rather than at death.[1]

Qualified terminable interest property — the four gates

Under §2523(f)(2), property is "qualified terminable interest property" if:

  1. The donee spouse is entitled to all income from the property for life, payable at least annually (§2523(f)(2)(B) cross-references §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I)).
  2. No person has a power to appoint any part of the property to anyone other than the donee spouse during the donee spouse's lifetime (§2523(f)(2)(B) cross-references §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II)).
  3. An election is made on Form 709 by the donor for the year of the transfer (§2523(f)(4)).
  4. The election is irrevocable once made (regulatory position at Treas. Reg. §25.2523(f)-1(b)(1)(iv)).[10]

The election is made on Schedule A of Form 709 by identifying the QTIP-eligible transfer and checking the appropriate election box. The election extends to the entire property or, under Treas. Reg. §25.2523(f)-1(b)(3), to a fractional or percentage share ("partial QTIP election"), so long as the fraction is stated as a fraction or percentage rather than a fixed dollar amount.[11]

Tax consequences after the election

Once the election is made, the marital deduction applies to the entire value of the property transferred (not just the actuarial value of the life-income interest). The donee spouse's income interest is not a nondeductible terminable interest, and the remainder is fully deductible as if it had passed outright to the donee spouse. Then:

  • Inclusion at donee spouse's death. The property is includible in the donee spouse's gross estate at date-of-death fair market value under §2044.[12] This is the deferral cost — the transfer tax is postponed to the donee spouse's death, but the growth during the donee spouse's remaining life is taxed at that time.
  • Distribution during donee spouse's life. A distribution from the trust to a third party during the donee spouse's life is treated as a gift by the donee spouse of the QTIP property under §2519 — the "disposition trap" that turns a small trustee distribution into a large deemed gift.[13] Our full §2519 disposition-trap deep-dive walks the mechanic and the drafting protections.
  • Basis step-up at donee spouse's death. Because the property is includible in the donee spouse's estate under §2044, it receives a §1014 basis step-up at that later death — the mirror-image basis benefit of a straight outright transfer.

Reverse-QTIP for GST planning

A lifetime QTIP election under §2523(f) can be paired with a reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3), which allows the donor to remain the "transferor" for generation-skipping transfer-tax purposes even though the property is QTIP'd for marital-deduction purposes.[14] The reverse election lets the donor allocate GST exemption immediately (at potentially lower valuations) rather than letting the exemption default to the surviving spouse at her death. See our companion §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP deep-dive for the full mechanic.

Rev. Proc. 2016-49 — the QTIP election "erase" mechanic

Rev. Proc. 2016-49 supersedes Rev. Proc. 2001-38 and clarifies that a §2523(f) QTIP election is not treated as void merely because the marital deduction was not needed to reduce the donor's gift tax to zero. This matters for portability-era planning: a donor may want to preserve remainder control via a QTIP structure even when annual-exclusion gifts and lifetime-exemption use would have zeroed the tax anyway, and Rev. Proc. 2016-49 makes clear the QTIP is still respected.[15]

§2523(g) — the charitable-remainder trust exception

§2523(g) permits the marital deduction where the donor transfers to a §664(d) charitable remainder trust in which the donee spouse is the only noncharitable beneficiary. Two structures qualify: a Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust (CRAT) paying a fixed dollar annuity to the spouse for life, and a Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT) paying a fixed percentage of the annually revalued trust corpus to the spouse for life. In both cases, the remainder must pass to a §170(c)-qualifying charity.[1]

The tax mechanics: the actuarial value of the spouse's annuity/unitrust interest is deductible as a §2523 marital gift, and the actuarial value of the charitable remainder is separately deductible as a §2522 charitable gift. Together the two deductions fully offset the gift-tax value of the transfer to zero. The spouse pays income tax on distributions under §664 four-tier rules; the charity receives the remainder tax-free at the spouse's death.

§2523(g) is a specialty tool. It is most useful when a donor wants to provide a lifetime income stream to a spouse who has independent wealth and does not need the corpus, and simultaneously lock in a charitable remainder. Because the §7520 rate and the payout percentage together determine the actuarial split between spouse and charity, the CRT design must clear the §664(d) 10% remainder test — the actuarial value of the remainder cannot be less than 10% of the initial trust corpus. Our §7520 valuation deep-dive covers the mechanic.

§2523(i) — the noncitizen-spouse denial

§2523(i)(1) is a switch. When the donee spouse is not a U.S. citizen at the time of the gift, the §2523(a) unlimited marital deduction is denied entirely. Every other subsection of §2523 becomes irrelevant — no §2523(e) power-of-appointment carve-out, no §2523(f) QTIP election, no §2523(g) CRT deduction is available for a noncitizen-spouse transfer.[1]

Congress was blunt about why. Absent §2523(i), a wealthy U.S. donor could transfer unlimited property to a noncitizen spouse, the spouse could then relocate offshore, and the entire transfer would escape U.S. transfer tax forever — the donor's Form 706 estate return would have already claimed the §2523 deduction and the U.S. lacks jurisdiction over the noncitizen's estate at her later death. §2523(i) closes that loophole by refusing the deduction at the front end, then §2523(i)(2) provides a substitute in the form of an enlarged annual exclusion, and §2056A creates a QDOT mechanism to preserve deferral at the first-spouse-death estate side.

Who is a "noncitizen" for §2523(i)?

The test is U.S. citizenship, not U.S. residency. A green-card-holder resident is a noncitizen for §2523(i) purposes even though the couple files a joint income-tax return under §6013(g). Dual citizens (U.S. plus another country) are citizens. A noncitizen spouse who naturalizes before the year in which the gift is made is a citizen for that year's transfers, so pre-naturalization gifting strategies must be timed against the citizenship acquisition date.

Domicile matters separately for the donor's exposure: a nondomiciliary donor is generally taxed only on gifts of U.S.-situs property under §2501(a)(2). But a U.S.-domiciled donor is taxed on worldwide gifts, and the §2523(i) noncitizen-spouse denial applies to those worldwide transfers regardless of whether the spouse is U.S.-domiciled.[16]

§2523(i)(2) — the $194,000 enlarged annual exclusion

Because §2523(i)(1) revokes the unlimited marital deduction for noncitizen-spouse transfers, Congress substituted an enlarged annual exclusion at §2523(i)(2). For 2026, the amount is $194,000 under Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.42(2).[3] The historical sequence:

Rules the enlarged exclusion inherits from §2503(b)

  • Present-interest requirement. §2523(i)(2) explicitly imports the §2503(b) present-interest test. Future-interest gifts to a noncitizen spouse get zero exclusion — the same result as any other future-interest gift.
  • Per-year, not per-donee-per-year. Because a person can only have one spouse at a time, the enlarged exclusion is effectively per-year. The donor cannot multiply the exclusion by gifting through separate accounts within a year.
  • Stacks with §2503(b) exclusions to other donees. The $194,000 to a noncitizen spouse is in addition to the $19,000-per-donee general annual exclusion to any other donees. A U.S. citizen donor with a noncitizen spouse and ten grandchildren can move $194,000 + (10 × $19,000) = $384,000 tax-free in 2026 without any Form 709 taxable gift.
  • No §2513 gift-splitting available for the enlarged exclusion. §2513 splits gifts to third parties; it does not apply to gifts between the spouses themselves. The noncitizen spouse cannot split a $194,000 gift from the citizen spouse back to herself under §2513.

Form 709 filing consequences

A gift of exactly $194,000 or less to a noncitizen spouse in 2026 that qualifies as a present-interest gift is fully excluded and does not, standing alone, require filing Form 709. A gift above $194,000 is a taxable gift for the excess, must be reported on Form 709 Schedule A, and reduces §2010(c) lifetime exemption dollar-for-dollar until exhausted. The 40% top gift-tax rate under §2502 then applies to further excess.[17]

§2056A QDOT — the estate-side companion to §2523(i)

The gift-side story ends with §2523(i)(2). The estate-side companion is §2056A, the Qualified Domestic Trust (QDOT), which allows a decedent's estate to claim the §2056(a) unlimited estate-tax marital deduction for property passing to a noncitizen surviving spouse — even though §2056(d) would ordinarily deny that deduction under the same policy that drives §2523(i).[18]

The four QDOT requirements under §2056A(a)

  1. Instrument requirement. The trust instrument must satisfy the §2056(b)(5), §2056(b)(7), or §2056(b)(8) requirements as if the surviving spouse were a citizen — most commonly drafted as a QTIP-format QDOT.
  2. U.S. trustee. At least one trustee must be a U.S. citizen or U.S. corporation with authority to withhold estate tax on principal distributions.
  3. Principal-distribution tax. The U.S. trustee must have the right to require withholding of §2056A(b) estate tax on any distribution of principal (other than a distribution for hardship). The tax equals the additional estate tax the decedent's estate would have paid if the QDOT property had not qualified for the marital deduction, computed at the marginal rate applicable to the decedent's estate.
  4. Balance taxed at death. The remaining balance in the QDOT at the surviving spouse's death is subject to §2056A(b) estate tax, computed as if the property were included in the decedent's estate.

Practical structure

The typical QDOT is a QTIP trust: surviving spouse gets all income for life, principal distributions require the U.S. trustee's consent and trigger §2056A(b) withholding, and the remainder at the surviving spouse's death is subject to §2056A(b) tax with any balance passing per the decedent's dispositive plan (often to children).

QDOT planning is expensive and administratively heavy. For couples where naturalization of the noncitizen spouse is expected within a reasonable timeframe, the standard planning move is to wait: complete naturalization first, then execute the marital transfers under §2523(a) without needing a QDOT or the enlarged $194,000 exclusion. Once naturalized, the §2523(i) denial does not apply — even to transfers made after the citizenship acquisition date. §2056A(b)(12) also allows a QDOT to convert if the surviving spouse naturalizes after the first-spouse death.

Coordination with §2513 gift-splitting

§2513 and §2523 are two different mechanics that both involve spouses, and they sometimes get confused. §2513 lets a married couple treat gifts made by one spouse to a third party as if made half by each spouse — doubling the §2503(b) $19,000 annual exclusion per donee to $38,000.[19] §2523 is the deduction for a gift made by one spouse to the other spouse. Their scopes do not overlap.

The interaction cases that matter:

  • Gift by citizen donor to citizen spouse of $500,000. §2523(a) unlimited deduction applies; no gift tax; no §2010(c) exemption use; no Form 709 taxable-gift line. §2513 is irrelevant because the gift is between the spouses.
  • Gift by citizen donor to third party (say, a grandchild) of $50,000. §2523 is irrelevant. §2513 election lets both spouses split the gift into two $25,000 halves; each half consumes one $19,000 annual exclusion under §2503(b), leaving $6,000 per spouse as a taxable gift. Both spouses file Form 709.
  • Gift by citizen donor to noncitizen spouse of $250,000 in 2026. §2523(a) unavailable due to §2523(i)(1). §2523(i)(2) exclusion of $194,000 applies. Excess $56,000 is a taxable gift consuming §2010(c) exemption. §2513 does not apply because it does not split spouse-to-spouse transfers.

Coordination with §2010(c) DSUE portability

The §2010(c)(4) portability election lets a surviving spouse inherit the unused §2010(c) exemption of the first-to-die spouse — the "Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion" (DSUE) amount. Portability is elected on Form 706 at the first death by the executor of the deceased spouse's estate.[20]

The relevant interaction with §2523: because lifetime inter-spousal transfers under §2523(a) are fully deductible and do not consume the transferring spouse's §2010(c) exemption, they do not reduce the DSUE amount that ports to the surviving spouse at the transferor's death. This creates a planning-neutral outcome — the wealthy spouse can move any amount of property to the less-wealthy spouse during life without any transfer-tax cost, and the wealthy spouse's full $15 million §2010(c) exemption remains available at death for allocation against non-spousal assets or portability to the surviving spouse.

Portability doesn't fix a noncitizen-spouse problem

DSUE portability under §2010(c)(4) is available only when the surviving spouse is a U.S. citizen (or the property passes to a QDOT that ultimately zeroes out the taxable-estate reduction). A noncitizen surviving spouse cannot inherit DSUE. The estate-planning move: if a noncitizen spouse is expected to naturalize, structure the first-spouse's Form 706 to make the portability election on the assumption that a QDOT preserves the marital-deduction while the naturalization completes; then the surviving spouse becomes eligible for full DSUE inheritance.

State-level treatment — Connecticut is the outlier

Only Connecticut imposes a state-level gift tax in 2026, under Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-640.[21] The Connecticut mechanic conforms to federal marital-deduction rules — an inter-spousal transfer that is deductible under §2523(a) at the federal level is likewise excluded from Connecticut gift-tax analysis. The Connecticut Form CT-706/709 due at the same time as the federal Form 709 does not require reporting of §2523(a) unlimited deductions.

Connecticut does not impose a separate noncitizen-spouse denial parallel to §2523(i). The Connecticut definitional path routes through federal §2523(a) applicability, so a Connecticut donor whose noncitizen-spouse transfer is denied §2523(a) at the federal level (and reduced by the $194,000 §2523(i)(2) exclusion) has the same taxable-gift result under the Connecticut mechanic, but Connecticut's own $13.99 million 2026 lifetime exemption applies to that state-level taxable gift.

Every other U.S. jurisdiction imposes no state-level gift tax. State estate taxes (in Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington, D.C., Illinois, Hawaii, and Washington State) have their own marital-deduction rules for testamentary transfers, most of which conform to §2056 (the estate-tax counterpart to §2523). Worked case studies

Case 1: The straightforward citizen-spouse basis-rebalance

David (68) and Priya (65) are U.S. citizens domiciled in Florida. David holds $8 million in low-basis (average basis $600,000) publicly traded technology stock in his individual brokerage account, appreciated over 20 years. Priya holds $2 million in cash and short-duration Treasuries. David's health is materially worse than Priya's. Their combined estate is $10 million; the OBBBA §2010(c) exemption ($15M × 2 = $30M with portability) fully covers them, so no federal estate tax is expected.

The basis-rebalance move: David gifts $4 million of the technology stock to Priya under §2523(a). §1041 provides carryover basis; the stock lands in Priya's brokerage at an average basis of $300,000. If David predeceases Priya, the remaining $4 million in his account gets a §1014 basis step-up to fair market value at his death, then passes to Priya via marital deduction — a full basis wash. If Priya predeceases David, she can direct the $4 million in her account via her estate plan to a QTIP or credit-shelter trust that David is the life-income beneficiary of, and the stock in her estate gets the §1014 step-up.

Result: David and Priya doubled their §1014 basis-step-up optionality without any gift-tax, income-tax, or estate-tax cost. §2523(a) enabled the entire move; §1041 made it income-tax neutral; §2010(c) coverage made the estate-tax accounting irrelevant. No Form 709 is required.

Case 2: The lifetime QTIP for a blended-family second marriage

Marcus (72) and Elena (58) married in 2022. Marcus has three adult children from his first marriage. Elena has no children. Marcus's net worth is $18 million; Elena's is $600,000. Marcus wants to guarantee Elena's lifetime income but also ensure that at Elena's death the corpus passes to his children (not Elena's siblings or Elena's future spouse if she remarries after Marcus's death).

The §2523(f) lifetime QTIP move: Marcus funds a $10 million lifetime QTIP trust in 2026. Elena is the sole life-income beneficiary; the trustee must distribute all income annually. On Elena's death, the remainder passes to Marcus's three children equally. Marcus elects §2523(f) treatment on his 2026 Form 709.

Consequences:

  • The §2523(a) marital deduction is fully available for the entire $10 million because of the §2523(f) election. No taxable gift; no §2010(c) exemption use; no Form 709 taxable-gift line for the QTIP transfer.
  • The $10 million (plus appreciation) is includible in Elena's gross estate at her death under §2044. Assuming Elena dies with $12 million in her own estate plus the $10 million (grown to $14 million by then) of QTIP corpus, her taxable estate is $26 million against her ported-plus-personal §2010(c) exemption. Whether estate tax is owed at Elena's death depends on Elena's lifetime gifts and her own portability elections.
  • Marcus also files a §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Form 709 Schedule R-1 to remain the "transferor" for GST purposes. This lets Marcus allocate his $15 million §2631(c) GST exemption to the QTIP at the (lower) 2026 valuation rather than letting the transfer default to Elena's exemption at her later death.
  • If Marcus wants the flexibility, he elects only a partial QTIP under Treas. Reg. §25.2523(f)-1(b)(3) — say, an 80% QTIP election — leaving 20% as a taxable gift consuming $2 million of his §2010(c) exemption. This preserves control over 80% of the corpus (marital deduction) while directly transferring 20% out of Marcus's own estate (using exemption).

Case 3: The noncitizen-spouse structure across a naturalization horizon

Rajiv (55) is a U.S. citizen; Amara (52) is an Indian citizen with a U.S. green card, U.S.-domiciled, married to Rajiv for 12 years. Rajiv's net worth is $45 million (technology equity, real estate); Amara's is $1.2 million. Rajiv wants to move $4 million of appreciated stock into Amara's name over the next 24 months for asset-protection reasons. Amara has begun her citizenship process; final naturalization interview is expected in 20 months.

The two-stage plan:

  1. 2026 pre-naturalization transfers. Rajiv gifts $194,000 to Amara in January 2026 under the §2523(i)(2) enlarged annual exclusion. No Form 709 required (assuming no other trigger). In 2027 (a full second calendar year before naturalization), Rajiv gifts another $200,000 or so (using the inflation-adjusted §2523(i)(2) figure for 2027, projected at approximately $200,000). Total moved tax-free: roughly $394,000 across two calendar years without any Form 709 taxable-gift line or §2010(c) reduction.
  2. 2028 post-naturalization transfer. After Amara's naturalization, the §2523(i) denial no longer applies. Rajiv can execute the remaining $3.6 million in a single transfer under §2523(a) unlimited citizen-spouse deduction. No Form 709 taxable-gift line; no §2010(c) reduction; no QDOT needed.

Total 2026-2028 wealth-transfer to Amara: $4 million. Total gift-tax cost: $0. Total §2010(c) exemption used: $0. The two-stage timing structure fully leveraged the §2523(i)(2) enlarged exclusion during the pre-naturalization years and preserved every dollar of Rajiv's $15M lifetime exemption for later use.

Six recurring mistakes in §2523 planning

  1. Assuming §2523(a) unlimited deduction covers a noncitizen-spouse transfer. The single most expensive §2523 mistake. A $2 million transfer to a noncitizen spouse is a $1,806,000 taxable gift ($2M − $194,000 exclusion) that consumes $1.806M of lifetime exemption or generates gift tax at the 40% top rate if exemption is exhausted. Always confirm citizenship, not residency.
  2. Creating a terminable interest without a §2523(e)–(g) escape hatch. Transferring Blackacre "to Wife for life, remainder to Children" without a §2523(e) power of appointment, §2523(f) QTIP election, or §2523(g) CRT structure produces a fully taxable gift of both the life estate and the remainder — no marital deduction anywhere in the transfer.
  3. Missing the §2523(f) election on Form 709. A lifetime QTIP structure that qualifies as QTIP property but for which the donor never checked the QTIP election box on Form 709 does not get the marital deduction. The election is affirmative and irrevocable; there is no "protective QTIP" concept — either the election is made in the year of transfer or the deduction is lost forever. Rev. Proc. 2016-49 preserves the election even when unnecessary, but does not resurrect a missed election.
  4. Confusing §2523 with §2513. §2523 deducts a spouse-to-spouse transfer. §2513 splits a spouse-to-third-party gift. Advisers occasionally attempt "§2513-splitting" of a spouse-to-spouse transfer, which does nothing — §2513 by its terms applies only to gifts made "to third parties."
  5. Ignoring §1014(e) on deathbed inter-spousal transfers. A gift from Healthy Spouse to Sick Spouse of appreciated property, with Sick Spouse then dying within one year and devising the property back to Healthy Spouse, is disallowed the §1014 basis step-up under §1014(e). The property retains its pre-transfer carryover basis. The §2523 deduction and §1041 income-tax neutrality still apply — but the basis-step-up scheme fails.
  6. Failing to run the DSUE portability accounting. A wealthy citizen spouse who has made extensive §2523(a) transfers to the less-wealthy spouse during life should still file the §2010(c)(4) portability election on the wealthy spouse's Form 706 at first death. The wealthy spouse's §2010(c) exemption was never touched by the §2523 transfers, so the full $15M exemption is available to port to the surviving spouse — but only if the portability box on Form 706 is checked.

Action checklist — before making an inter-spousal transfer over $100,000

  1. Confirm citizenship of both spouses in writing. Passport or naturalization certificate for each. If the donee spouse is not a U.S. citizen, do not proceed under §2523(a); use §2523(i)(2) enlarged annual exclusion for present-interest transfers up to $194,000, or plan the QDOT structure separately.
  2. Confirm the transfer is a completed gift. No retained powers, no revocability, no conditions precedent. A revocable trust funding is not a completed gift and does not need §2523 analysis at all.
  3. Identify whether the interest is a terminable interest. Outright fee-simple transfer = no. Life estate, term of years, or interest passing to a third party on termination = yes; escape hatch required.
  4. Choose the §2523(e)–(g) escape hatch if the interest is terminable. §2523(e) for a life-estate + general power of appointment; §2523(f) for lifetime QTIP with donor-controlled remainder; §2523(g) for a spouse-only CRT.
  5. Draft or confirm the trust instrument satisfies the applicable exception. All-income-for-life clause payable at least annually, no power to appoint to third parties during donee spouse's life, no restrictions on donee spouse's exercise of any applicable general power.
  6. File Form 709 with any required election. §2523(f) QTIP election in the year of transfer; §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP for GST if desired; §2513 election is separate and applies only to spouse-to-third-party gifts.
  7. Coordinate with §2010(c) DSUE portability tracking. Confirm that inter-spousal transfers do not reduce the transferor spouse's remaining exemption, and that the portability election will be filed at the transferor spouse's death.
  8. Update the transferor spouse's estate plan. QTIP inclusion at the donee spouse's death under §2044, along with §1041 carryover basis on lifetime transfers, changes the surviving-spouse's estate composition materially. Update revocable-trust dispositive plans and beneficiary designations to reflect the new asset location.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 2026 marital deduction under IRC §2523(a)?

Unlimited. A completed gift of any amount from one U.S. citizen spouse to another U.S. citizen spouse is fully deducted from the donor's taxable gifts under §2523(a), so long as the transferred interest is not a nondeductible "terminable interest" under §2523(b). There is no dollar cap. A billionaire spouse may transfer the entire estate to a citizen spouse during life without a dollar of gift tax and without consuming any §2010(c) lifetime exemption.

What is the terminable-interest rule under §2523(b) and how does §2523(f) override it?

§2523(b) disallows the marital deduction for a gift where the donee spouse's interest terminates on the passage of time or a stated event and property then passes to someone other than the donee spouse. §2523(f) creates a QTIP election workaround: if the property is "qualified terminable interest property" — the donee spouse is entitled to all income for life, payable at least annually, no person has a power to appoint any part to anyone other than the donee spouse during life — the donor may elect on Form 709 to treat the property as passing to the donee spouse, unlocking the marital deduction. The remainder is then included in the donee spouse's gross estate at death under §2044.

What is the 2026 annual exclusion for a gift to a noncitizen spouse?

$194,000 in 2026 under IRC §2523(i)(2), per Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.42(2). §2523(i)(1) denies the unlimited §2523(a) marital deduction whenever the donee spouse is not a U.S. citizen. Congress substituted an enlarged annual exclusion — the $194,000 2026 figure — which is a §2503(b)-style cap applied uniquely to citizen-to-noncitizen-spouse present-interest transfers. Above the cap, the transfer is a taxable gift consuming §2010(c) lifetime exemption. The enlarged exclusion is available even in years the donor makes ordinary §2503(b) gifts of $19,000 to other donees.

How is a lifetime §2523(f) QTIP election different from a Form 706 §2056(b)(7) QTIP election?

The mechanic is parallel but the tax event is different. §2523(f) is a gift-tax election, made on the donor's Form 709 in the year of the transfer, to unlock the unlimited marital deduction for a lifetime QTIP transfer. §2056(b)(7) is the estate-tax election on the deceased spouse's Form 706 for testamentary QTIP trusts. Both defer transfer-tax until the surviving spouse's death, both cause inclusion in the survivor's gross estate under §2044, and both permit reverse-QTIP GST elections under §2652(a)(3). The lifetime version is useful for asset-protection planning and for pre-funding a bypass structure while the wealthy spouse is still alive.

Can a citizen spouse gift-split with a noncitizen spouse under §2513?

Yes, as long as both spouses are U.S. residents at the time of the gift. §2513 requires both spouses to be U.S. citizens "or residents" — the residency test is a domicile-plus-visa test resolved on the facts. A noncitizen resident spouse (e.g., a green-card holder domiciled in the U.S.) may consent to gift-splitting, and the resulting split gifts get the $19,000 annual exclusion per donee for each split half. However, §2513 does NOT convert the underlying §2523(i) noncitizen denial for gifts BETWEEN the spouses; it only affects the treatment of gifts from either spouse to third parties.

What is a QDOT under §2056A and why is it the estate-side answer to §2523(i)?

A Qualified Domestic Trust under IRC §2056A is a special trust that lets a decedent's estate claim the §2056(a) unlimited marital deduction even though the surviving spouse is not a U.S. citizen — bypassing the §2056(d) noncitizen-spouse denial. The QDOT must have at least one U.S. trustee, must withhold §2056A(b) estate tax on principal distributions (equivalent to the tax the decedent's estate would have paid if the property had not qualified for the marital deduction), and the balance is subject to §2056A(b) tax at the surviving spouse's death. §2056A on the estate side and §2523(i)(2) $194,000 enlarged exclusion on the gift side are Congress's paired response to the perceived risk that a noncitizen spouse could take marital-deduction property offshore and defeat the estate tax.

Does an outright gift of appreciated stock to a citizen spouse trigger any tax?

No gift tax under §2523(a). No income tax under IRC §1041 — a transfer of property between spouses (or former spouses incident to divorce) is treated as a gift for income-tax purposes with a carryover basis. The recipient spouse takes the donor's basis and holding period. There is no §1015 basis step-down and no gain recognition at the transfer. This makes gifts of low-basis appreciated property between citizen spouses tax-cost-free while shifting future gain to the recipient. Coordination with §1014 death-time basis step-up planning is a separate optimization: gifting to the sicker spouse who is expected to predecease can produce a full basis step-up in property that would otherwise carry over.

Does §2523 apply to same-sex spouses?

Yes. Following United States v. Windsor, 570 U.S. 744 (2013), and Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), federal transfer-tax law treats same-sex married couples identically to opposite-sex married couples. IRC §7701(a)(17) defines "spouse" by reference to state-law marriage validity, and Rev. Rul. 2013-17 confirmed that same-sex spouses lawfully married in any jurisdiction are "spouses" for all federal tax purposes regardless of the couple's state of domicile. §2523(a) unlimited marital deduction, §2523(f) QTIP election, §2523(i) noncitizen denial, and §2513 gift-splitting all apply to same-sex married couples on the same terms.

What is the §2523(g) charitable-remainder trust exception?

§2523(g) permits the gift-tax marital deduction for a transfer to a charitable remainder trust (CRT) where the donee spouse is the ONLY noncharitable beneficiary — a CRAT or CRUT with the spouse as sole life annuity/unitrust recipient and a §170(c) qualifying charity as sole remainder beneficiary. The donor gets the full §2523 marital deduction for the spouse's annuity/unitrust interest and a §2522 charitable deduction for the actuarial value of the remainder. The spouse's interest is not treated as a nondeductible terminable interest under §2523(b) because §2523(g) explicitly carves it out. This is a rare planning tool primarily used when a donor wants to fund a spouse's lifetime income stream and lock in a charitable remainder disposition.

Does §2523 apply to gifts made incident to divorce?

Transfers "incident to divorce" occupy a special category. IRC §2516 provides that certain transfers between spouses (or former spouses) made pursuant to a written property-settlement agreement within a three-year window around a divorce decree are treated as full and adequate consideration and are not gifts at all — bypassing §2523 entirely. §1041 provides identical income-tax treatment (transferor's basis carries over, no gain recognition). For divorces where §2516 does not apply, the §2523(a) marital deduction is available only if the transfer occurs while the parties are still married; a post-divorce transfer between former spouses is a gift for §2503(b) analysis with no marital deduction.
