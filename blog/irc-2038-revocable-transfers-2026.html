IRC §2038 Revocable Transfers in 2026: The §2038(a)(1) Power-to-Alter Rule, §2038(b) Date-of-Existence Provision, Grantor-as-Trustee Doctrine, and the Revocable-Living-Trust Full-Inclusion Mechanic

§2038 is the section that governs every classical revocable living trust in America — and every irrevocable trust in which the grantor kept even a whisper of the power to change who gets what, when. A retained power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate — held alone or jointly, held in the grantor's own capacity or as trustee, held with immediate effect or with a notice period — pulls the affected trust corpus back into the gross estate at date-of-death fair market value. This is the 2026 walkthrough of every operative subsection, every judicial gloss (Old Colony Trust, Jennings, Bosch, Ford), the ascertainable-standard escape, the §675(4)(C) substitution-power exception, the grantor-as-trustee analysis, and the interaction with §§2035/2036/2037 that finally closes the Chapter 11 four-pillar retained-interest inclusion-rule foundation.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 15, 2026 22 min read How we research

Every estate planner working an estate near the $15 million §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, §70106, July 2025) permanently reset has to answer the same question about every trust the client has ever created: did the client keep any power to change the deal, and if so, does that power drag the trust corpus back into the gross estate?[1] The answer routes through IRC §2038 — the revocable-transfer rule — and the answer more often than practitioners realize is yes, the trust is pulled back.[2]

§2038 is the least-discussed and most-frequently-triggered of the four Chapter 11 retained-interest inclusion rules. §2036 gets the attention because the FLP-attack case law is dramatic (Strangi, Bongard, Powell). §2042 gets the attention because life insurance is a discrete, quantifiable asset with a clear ILIT-based solution. §2035 gets the attention because the three-year lookback is a bright-line rule that either applies or does not. §2038 sits behind all of them — every revocable living trust is 100% §2038-included, every jointly-held distribution power reaches §2038, every grantor-as-trustee arrangement with anything more than an ascertainable-standard distribution power reaches §2038, and the release-within-three-years trap on any of these powers pulls the property back under §2035(a).[3]

The 1924 → 1936 → 1954 arc — the closing of the deathbed loophole

The current §2038 is the fourth generation of the statute. The Revenue Act of 1924 §302(d) first imposed a federal estate tax on transfers where the decedent retained a power to alter, amend, or revoke — Congress's response to a wave of deathbed revocable-trust arrangements that stripped assets from the pre-1924 estate tax base by transferring nominal ownership while retaining full economic and legal control.[5] The Supreme Court initially construed the 1924 statute narrowly in Reinecke v. Northern Trust Co., 278 U.S. 339 (1929), holding that transfers made before the effective date of the statute were outside its reach even if the retained power persisted at death — a due-process reading based on the retroactivity concern.[6]

Congress responded in the Revenue Act of 1934 §302(d) by expanding the reach to any power to alter, amend, or revoke held at the date of death, regardless of when the underlying transfer was made.[7] The Supreme Court then upheld the retroactive application in Helvering v. City Bank Farmers Trust Co., 296 U.S. 85 (1935), and in Porter v. Commissioner, 288 U.S. 436 (1933) (which addressed the earlier statute), establishing the modern principle that Congress may reach any transfer where the decedent retained substantive control at death without offending due process, because the retained control is what makes the transfer economically incomplete.[8]

The 1936 amendment — codified through the Revenue Act of 1936 §805 — added the "power to terminate" language to the "alter, amend, or revoke" trio, and set June 22, 1936, as the dividing line between the pre-1936 statutory regime (now §2038(a)(2)) and the modern regime (§2038(a)(1)) that applies to transfers made after that date.[9] The Internal Revenue Code of 1954 restated the rule as §2038 with the current two-part structure — §2038(a)(1) for post-1936 transfers and §2038(a)(2) for pre-1936 transfers — plus the §2038(b) date-of-existence provision that closes the notice-period drafting evasion.[3] The statute has been substantively unchanged since 1954; every subsequent development has been through Treasury regulations, revenue rulings, and case law.

§2038(a)(1) — the modern power-to-alter-amend-revoke-terminate rule

The current text of §2038(a)(1) provides that the value of the gross estate shall include the value of all property to the extent of any interest therein of which the decedent has at any time made a transfer (except in the case of a bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth), by trust or otherwise, where the enjoyment thereof was subject at the date of death to any change through the exercise of a power (in whatever capacity exercisable) by the decedent alone or by the decedent in conjunction with any other person (without regard to when or from what source the decedent acquired such power), to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate, or where any such power is relinquished during the 3-year period ending on the date of the decedent's death.[3]

Four features are load-bearing:

Treas. Reg. §20.2038-1(a) implements the statute and adds the operational nuance that the amount included is the value of the property subject to the power — which can be less than the full trust corpus when the power reaches only a portion of the trust.[10] Where the power extends to only a definite portion of the trust (say, a power to sprinkle income only, with the corpus fixed for named remainder beneficiaries), inclusion is limited to that portion's date-of-death fair market value. Where the power extends to all beneficial interests in the trust corpus, the full corpus is included.

What counts as "the enjoyment thereof was subject at the date of death to any change"

The Tax Court and the appellate courts have treated a wide range of retained powers as §2038 powers, including:

  • The bare power to revoke. The classical revocable living trust — where the grantor retained the express power to revoke the trust and pull the assets back into the grantor's own hands at any time — is the paradigmatic §2038(a)(1) transfer. Full inclusion at date-of-death fair market value, always.[11]
  • The discretionary power to distribute income or principal among beneficiaries. A grantor-trustee (or a grantor together with a co-trustee) holding sprinkle discretion among the class of beneficiaries has the power to alter enjoyment. Full inclusion of the trust corpus unless the discretion is limited by a pure ascertainable standard.[12]
  • The power to add or remove beneficiaries. Any retained power to change the class of beneficiaries — whether by adding new beneficiaries from a defined pool or removing existing beneficiaries — is a §2038 power regardless of any distribution standard.[13]
  • The power to change beneficial shares. A retained power to shift the proportional shares of beneficiaries — even without adding or removing anyone — is a §2038 power. This includes the power to change the remainder-beneficiary allocation in an otherwise-irrevocable trust.
  • The power to accelerate or delay distribution. Estate of O'Malley v. Commissioner, 383 U.S. 627 (1966), and later cases hold that the power to accelerate or delay the vesting or distribution of a beneficiary's interest is a §2038 power because it alters the beneficiary's actual enjoyment, even if the eventual beneficiaries and shares are fixed.[14]
  • The power to change the trust's situs or administration in a way that materially affects enjoyment. Ministerial situs-change powers are outside §2038, but any situs change that alters governing state law in a way that materially changes beneficiary rights is a §2038 power.

The joint-power trap

The single most common §2038 mistake in the closely-held-business context is the grantor who names their spouse or an adult child as co-trustee, gives the co-trustees jointly a discretionary distribution power, and believes that the joint requirement protects them. It does not. §2038 explicitly reaches powers held "in conjunction with any other person," and the joint holder's independence, beneficial interest in the trust, or otherwise-adverse status does not defeat the reach except in the very narrow §2038 adverse-party carve-out (which turns on whether the joint holder's interest is adverse to the exercise of the power in favor of the decedent personally, not adverse to the beneficiaries — a test rarely satisfied in a family-trust context). A $10 million family trust with the grantor and spouse as joint discretionary co-trustees comes back into the grantor's gross estate at full date-of-death fair market value, consuming $10M of §2010(c) exemption and, at the 40% top rate under §2001, potentially generating $4M of federal estate tax.

§2038(b) — the date-of-existence provision closing the notice-period evasion

§2038(b) provides that, for purposes of §2038, a power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate shall be considered to exist on the date of the decedent's death even though (1) the exercise of the power is subject to a precedent giving of notice or (2) the alteration, amendment, revocation, or termination takes effect only on the expiration of a stated period after the exercise of the power, whether or not on or before the date of the decedent's death notice has been given or the power has been exercised.[3]

The provision closes what would otherwise be a large drafting exception. Absent §2038(b), a decedent who held a fully-effective power to revoke a trust — but where the trust document provided that revocation required 60 days' notice, or was effective only on the last day of the calendar quarter following the exercise — could have argued that no power "existed" at the moment of death if death happened during a notice or waiting period without a pending exercise. §2038(b) rejects that argument categorically. The power exists at death whenever the decedent could have exercised it at any time before death, regardless of built-in delays or notice periods.

The provision was added to prevent a common drafting evasion in the years immediately following the 1924 enactment of the modern §2038 predecessor. Practitioners had been building 90-day notice provisions into revocable trusts, hoping to argue that a decedent who died 30 days into a notice period had no power at death because the exercise could not become effective in the decedent's lifetime.[15] Congress and the courts foreclosed the argument through §2038(b); today the provision operates primarily as a background rule that removes an entire category of aggressive drafting from consideration.

§2038(c) — the bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration exception

§2038(a)(1) itself contains the bona fide sale exception in its opening parenthetical — "(except in the case of a bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth)."[3] The exception is the doctrinal gate through which every properly-structured installment sale to an intentionally-defective grantor trust and every legitimate arm's-length purchase from a trust must pass, mirroring the parallel exceptions in §§2035(d), 2036(c), and 2037(c).

The bona fide sale exception has two elements — "bona fide sale" (the transaction must be a genuine, arm's-length exchange rather than a disguised gift) and "adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth" (the consideration received must equal the fair market value of the property transferred). The exception is narrower than it looks: an installment sale to an IDGT for a note whose principal amount equals the fair market value of the transferred property, bearing interest at the applicable federal rate under §7872, generally qualifies — but only if the note terms reflect genuine economic risk, the trust has sufficient other assets to service the debt, and the transaction is not a disguised gift with a nominal note attached.[16]

Treas. Reg. §20.2043-1(a) coordinates the bona fide sale exception with §2043(a), which provides that when a transfer is "for less than an adequate and full consideration," the amount included in the gross estate is limited to the excess of the fair market value of the property over the value of the consideration received.[17] The interaction produces the standard IDGT-installment-sale planning arithmetic: a $10M sale for a $10M IDGT note fully qualifies for the §2038(c) exception (full consideration received); a $10M "sale" for a $2M note qualifies only to the extent of the $2M — the remaining $8M is a gift/§2038 inclusion at date-of-death fair market value.

The classical revocable living trust — always 100% §2038-included

The classical revocable living trust that is the workhorse of American probate-avoidance planning is the paradigmatic §2038(a)(1) transfer. The grantor's retained power to revoke the trust and pull the assets back into the grantor's own hands at any time before death is precisely the power to revoke that §2038(a)(1) targets. Every dollar of trust corpus at date of death is included in the grantor's gross estate at date-of-death fair market value.[11]

This is not a bug in the revocable living trust; it is the whole point. The trust avoids probate under state trust law but does not remove the assets from the federal gross estate. The revocable living trust therefore delivers procedural benefits without any federal transfer-tax benefit:

  • Probate avoidance. Trust assets pass under the trust's dispositive terms rather than through state-law probate; no probate filing, no probate fee, no probate publication.
  • Privacy. Trust dispositive terms are not filed with a probate court and remain private, unlike a probated will which becomes a public record accessible via the state probate registry.
  • Out-of-state real estate consolidation. Real estate located in a state other than the grantor's domicile can be titled in the trust to avoid a separate ancillary probate in the situs state.
  • Incapacity management. A successor trustee (identified in the trust document) can take over administration immediately upon the grantor's incapacity without a court-appointed conservator or guardian.
  • §1014 basis step-up. Because the trust assets are gross-estate-included under §2038, they receive a full §1014(a) basis step-up to date-of-death fair market value — the same income-tax result as if the grantor had held the assets directly.[18]

The revocable transfer is also not a completed gift for §2501/2511 purposes under Treas. Reg. §25.2511-2(c) — the grantor's retained power to revoke means that the "beneficiaries" do not have a completed interest until the grantor's death makes the trust irrevocable.[19] No gift tax is due when assets are transferred into a revocable trust; the completed gift happens on the grantor's death, at which point the §2038 inclusion (not the gift tax) captures the transfer.

Practitioner rule of thumb

A revocable living trust does not save a single dollar of federal estate tax. The grantor's retained power to revoke means the full trust corpus is §2038-included. Any client who was sold a revocable living trust as an estate-tax reduction strategy was misinformed. The revocable living trust is a probate-avoidance and privacy vehicle only; federal-transfer-tax reduction requires irrevocable-trust structures (ILITs, IDGTs, GRATs, QPRTs, dynasty trusts) where the grantor has genuinely relinquished the §2038 power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate.

The ascertainable-standard exception — Old Colony Trust, Jennings, Ford

The ascertainable-standard doctrine is the primary structural escape from §2038 for a grantor who wants to serve as trustee of an irrevocable trust. A trustee power limited by an ascertainable standard relating to the beneficiary's health, education, maintenance, or support (the classical HEMS standard) is treated as no power at all for §2038 purposes, on the theory that the power holder has no discretion to shift enjoyment among the class — the beneficiary's HEMS needs are objectively ascertainable and reviewable by a court in a fiduciary-accounting proceeding.[12]

Old Colony Trust Co. v. United States, 423 F.2d 601 (1st Cir. 1970), is the leading authority. The First Circuit held that a trustee power to invade principal for a beneficiary's "comfort, support, welfare and enjoyment" failed the ascertainable standard because "comfort" and "welfare" are not objectively measurable, while a power limited to HEMS would have escaped §2038.[20] The court emphasized that the test is whether "the extent of [the trustee's] duty and the extent of the beneficiary's right to enforcement" are governed by an objective standard capable of judicial enforcement.

The Tax Court has consistently applied the Old Colony Trust framework:

  • Estate of Jennings v. Commissioner, 10 T.C. 323 (1948). An earlier case with a similar analysis — held that a trustee power to distribute principal "as may be necessary for the proper support, care, and welfare" of the beneficiary failed the ascertainable standard because of the "welfare" component.[21]
  • Estate of Ford v. Commissioner, 53 T.C. 114 (1969), aff'd 450 F.2d 878 (2d Cir. 1971). The Second Circuit affirmed a §2038 inclusion where the trustee power was to distribute principal "in the trustee's uncontrolled discretion for the beneficiary's happiness." "Happiness" was held to be a subjective, unascertainable standard — full §2038 inclusion.[22]
  • Estate of Vissering v. Commissioner, 990 F.2d 578 (10th Cir. 1993). The Tenth Circuit reversed a Tax Court §2038 inclusion where the trustee power was to distribute principal for the beneficiary's "medical care, comfortable maintenance and welfare," reading the language under state law (Florida) as constrained to a HEMS equivalent.[23] Vissering establishes that the ascertainable-standard analysis is a state-law question — federal courts look to the trust-instrument language as it would be enforced by a state-law fiduciary-accounting court, and state common-law interpretations of ambiguous distribution language matter materially.

The ascertainable-standard exception does not, however, save every §2038-sensitive power. It applies only to distribution discretion. A trustee power to add or remove beneficiaries, to change beneficial shares, to accelerate or delay vesting, or to change trust terms other than distribution amounts is a §2038 power regardless of any distribution standard. A well-drafted trust that intends to preserve grantor-as-trustee flexibility while avoiding §2038 uses HEMS distribution discretion and vests all other powers — beneficiary changes, remainder-share adjustments, situs changes, administrative amendments — in an independent trustee or a trust protector.

The §675(4)(C) substitution power — Rev. Rul. 2008-22

A special category of grantor-retained power is the §675(4)(C) power to reacquire trust corpus by substituting property of equivalent value. The §675(4)(C) power is the standard "toggle" that makes an irrevocable trust a grantor trust for income-tax purposes under Subchapter J while — critically — not causing gross-estate inclusion under §§2036 or 2038. This is the doctrinal foundation of the intentionally-defective grantor trust (IDGT), a workhorse of modern estate planning that combines grantor-trust income-tax treatment (grantor pays trust income tax at personal marginal rates, effectively making tax-free gifts to the trust to the extent of the tax paid) with gross-estate exclusion (trust assets are outside the grantor's Chapter 11 estate).[24]

Rev. Rul. 2008-22, 2008-16 I.R.B. 796, holds that a grantor's retained power (or a power held by a nonadverse party) to reacquire trust corpus by substituting property of equivalent value does not, standing alone, cause gross-estate inclusion under either §2036 or §2038, provided:[25]

  1. The trustee has a fiduciary obligation under the governing instrument or applicable local law to ensure equivalent value of substituted assets, so that the substitution power cannot be used to shift value to or from the trust.
  2. The substitution power cannot be exercised in a manner that could shift benefits among trust beneficiaries — for example, by substituting a wasting asset for an appreciating asset in a way that would benefit one beneficiary at the expense of another.

The ruling expressly declines to extend to substitution powers held together with other retained powers that would independently trigger §2036 or §2038 — a §675(4)(C) substitution power plus a discretionary distribution power reopens the §2038 analysis on the full package.

The practical upshot: a properly-drafted IDGT with a bare §675(4)(C) substitution power and no other retained powers achieves grantor-trust income-tax status without §2038 gross-estate inclusion. Adding other retained powers to the same trust reopens the §2038 analysis on the full package, and the trust designer must reassess whether any of the additional powers themselves survive the ascertainable-standard or bona-fide-sale filters. The rulingdid not, and cannot, cure a §2038 problem elsewhere in the trust.

The grantor-as-trustee doctrine — what is safe and what is not

The question of whether a grantor may safely serve as trustee of their own irrevocable trust turns entirely on the powers the grantor-trustee holds. Practitioners frame the analysis in terms of a hierarchy of safety:

Category A — Reliably safe (no §2038 inclusion)

  • Purely ministerial trustee powers: custody, investment within a standard prudent-investor duty, tax and accounting administration, distribution of already-vested beneficial shares, and payment of trust expenses.
  • Distribution discretion limited by a pure HEMS ascertainable standard.
  • The §675(4)(C) substitution power alone, without other retained powers.
  • Investment-direction authority under a directed-trust statute (Del. Code Ann. tit. 12 §3313, S.D. Codified Laws §55-1B, and other analogous state statutes) — the grantor holds investment direction, an independent administrative trustee holds distribution power.[26]

Category B — Reliably dangerous (full §2038 inclusion)

  • Distribution discretion not limited by an ascertainable standard — including "sole and absolute discretion" or "in the trustee's uncontrolled discretion."
  • Distribution discretion limited by a standard that fails Old Colony Trust — "comfort," "welfare," "happiness," "well-being," "enjoyment."
  • Any power to add or remove beneficiaries, whether or not the distribution power is standard-limited.
  • Any power to change beneficial shares among fixed beneficiaries.
  • Any power to accelerate or delay the vesting of a beneficiary's interest beyond what a HEMS distribution standard would authorize.
  • Any bare power to revoke or terminate the trust.

Category C — Fact-dependent (state-law and drafting matter)

  • Distribution discretion limited by ambiguous language that state law would construe as HEMS-equivalent — Vissering-type situations where the outcome depends on the trust-situs state's fiduciary-accounting jurisprudence.
  • Trust-protector powers held by the grantor's spouse or a beneficiary — the joint-power problem depending on adverse-party analysis.
  • The power to change trustees — safe if limited to replacing a trustee with another party who is not related or subordinate to the grantor under §672(c), dangerous if the replacement pool includes related or subordinate parties.[27]

The safest structural rule: name an independent institutional trustee (or a professional individual trustee with no beneficial interest in the trust) as sole trustee for any distribution-discretion trust. A trust protector with a narrow amendment authority — typically limited to responding to changes in tax law, changing situs, changing administrative provisions, or replacing the trustee — can preserve family influence without triggering §2038, so long as the trust protector's powers are properly circumscribed and the grantor is not the trust protector.

Interaction with the §2035(a) three-year lookback

§2035(a) pulls back into the gross estate the value of property that would have been included under §2038 if the decedent had retained the interest until death — provided the decedent released the retained interest within three years of death.[28] The interaction between §2038 and §2035(a) matters most in three fact patterns:

  • Grantor resigns as trustee of an irrevocable trust with distribution discretion. The resignation is a §2038 release. §2035(a) pulls back the full trust corpus at date-of-death FMV if death occurs within three years.
  • Grantor amends the trust to remove a §2038 power. The amendment is a §2038 release. §2035(a) pulls back the trust corpus that would have been included under §2038 had the amendment not occurred, at date-of-death FMV if death within three years.
  • Grantor revokes a revocable trust and distributes the assets. The revocation-and-distribution is a §2038 release. §2035(a) pulls back the distributed property at its date-of-death FMV if death within three years — even though the assets are no longer legally titled in the trust at death, the release is the taxable event.

Overlap with §2036 — which section controls?

§2036(a)(2) and §2038(a)(1) overlap materially. Both reach retained powers over the beneficial enjoyment of transferred property. Many fact patterns fit both sections — a discretionary sprinkle power is both a §2036(a)(2) designation power and a §2038(a)(1) alter-amend power. Where both sections apply, Treas. Reg. §20.2038-1(a) provides that §2038 controls only if it produces a larger inclusion than §2036; otherwise §2036 controls. In practice §2036 usually produces the larger inclusion because it captures the full value of the transferred property regardless of the scope of the retained power, while §2038 inclusion is limited to the value of the property actually subject to the power.[29]

The operational differences:

  • §2036 requires retention "for the decedent's life," "for a period not ascertainable without reference to death," or "for a period which does not in fact end before death." A power that expires by its own terms before death (e.g., a 10-year discretionary power that has expired years earlier) is outside §2036 but may or may not be within §2038, depending on whether the power existed at death.
  • §2038 explicitly reaches powers "whether or not exercisable in a fiduciary capacity." Congress inserted the "in whatever capacity exercisable" phrase to foreclose the fiduciary-duty defense that briefly succeeded under §2036 in Byrum v. United States, 408 U.S. 125 (1972). §2036(b) reversed Byrum in the specific controlled-corporation context; §2038 is broader — no fiduciary-duty defense of any kind is available under §2038.[30]
  • §2038 is limited to the property subject to the power. If a grantor retained a §2038 power over only 40% of a $10M trust (say, the power reaches only the income interest, not the principal), §2038 inclusion is $4M (the value of the 40% subject to the power). §2036, by contrast, typically pulls the full $10M.

Our §2036 retained-life-estate deep-dive covers the parallel section's operative mechanics in operational depth. The two sections are best thought of as belt-and-suspenders — a well-designed retained-interest attack will proceed under both sections and argue whichever produces the larger inclusion.

DSUE portability and the surviving-spouse effect

§2010(c)(4) portability, made permanent by OBBBA §70106, lets a surviving spouse inherit the deceased spouse's unused §2010(c) exemption (the DSUE amount) if the first spouse's estate files Form 706 and elects portability.[1] A §2038 inclusion at the first death consumes exemption dollar-for-dollar and reduces the DSUE that ports to the surviving spouse.

Consider a $22 million first-death estate that includes a $10 million irrevocable trust pulled back under §2038(a)(1) because the grantor served as sole trustee with a "welfare and enjoyment" distribution standard that failed the ascertainable-standard test under Estate of Ford. Absent the §2038 inclusion, the gross estate would have been $12 million and DSUE porting to the surviving spouse would have been $15M − $12M = $3M. With the §2038 inclusion, the gross estate is $22 million, taxable estate exceeds the $15M exemption by $7 million, federal estate tax at 40% is $2.8 million, and DSUE porting to the surviving spouse is $0 (exhausted). Second-death planning based on the expected DSUE port has to reflect the §2038 tail risk on every "irrevocable" trust in the family's structure.

State-level treatment

The 12 state-estate-tax jurisdictions plus the District of Columbia — Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington — generally conform their gross-estate definitions to federal §2031, incorporating §2038 by reference or by parallel state statute.[31] Our state estate-tax field guide covers the state-by-state mechanics. The §2038 exposure is particularly acute in the states with low state-exclusion amounts (Oregon at $1 million; Massachusetts at $2 million; Rhode Island at $1.9 million in 2026) where a §2038 inclusion that has no federal cash-tax effect (because the federal $15M exemption absorbs the taxable estate) can still generate substantial state estate tax.

A representative fact pattern: a Massachusetts decedent with a $9 million estate serves as trustee of an "irrevocable" family trust holding $4 million with a "comfortable maintenance and welfare" distribution standard. §2038(a)(1) pulls the trust back into the gross estate. Federal §2010(c) exemption fully covers the $9 million taxable estate — zero federal estate tax. Massachusetts estate tax on $9 million above the $2 million state exemption at the state's graduated schedule (max 16%) produces roughly $890,000 of state estate tax. If the trust had used a pure HEMS standard from inception, the §2038 inclusion would not have occurred, the Massachusetts gross estate would have been $5 million, and Massachusetts estate tax would have been approximately $290,000 — the drafting failure cost approximately $600,000 in Massachusetts state estate tax alone.

Worked case studies

Case 1: The revocable living trust — 100% inclusion, no drafting fix available

Katherine (74) is a New York City resident with a $28 million estate. Her attorney recommended a revocable living trust in 2018 to consolidate her real estate holdings (a Manhattan cooperative valued at $6 million, a Southampton beach house valued at $4 million, and a Palm Beach condominium valued at $3 million) and her marketable-securities portfolio ($15 million) into a single administrative structure that would avoid three separate probate proceedings (New York, New York, and Florida) on her death. Katherine funded the trust with all $28 million of her assets and retained the express power to revoke.

Katherine dies in 2028. Her attorney had structured the trust well for probate-avoidance purposes: the successor trustee (Katherine's daughter) takes over administration immediately, the three real estate holdings pass to the residuary beneficiaries without probate proceedings in either New York or Florida, and the beach house and condominium avoid ancillary probate proceedings that would have cost approximately $80,000 in combined attorney and filing fees.

The §2038(a)(1) analysis:

  • Retained power. Katherine retained the express power to revoke the trust. Full §2038(a)(1) power at date of death.
  • Inclusion amount. The full date-of-death FMV of the trust corpus. Assume the assets have appreciated to $32 million by Katherine's date of death.
  • Bona fide sale exception. Not applicable — Katherine received no consideration for the transfer into the trust.

Federal consequence: Katherine's gross estate is the full $32 million trust corpus. Assuming her only other assets are personal effects and a small checking account totaling $200,000, the total gross estate is $32.2 million. The §2010(c) $15M exemption (assume $15.6M by 2028 with typical CPI-U indexing) covers the first $15.6M; the taxable estate is $16.6M; federal estate tax at 40% under §2001 is $6.64 million.

New York consequence: New York estate tax on $32.2M applies the "cliff" rule — because the estate exceeds 105% of the state exemption ($7.29M for 2028 per NY Tax Law §952 with typical indexing), New York taxes the full estate at graduated rates. Approximately $3.8M of New York state estate tax. Total federal-plus-state transfer tax is approximately $10.44M against the $32.2M gross estate.

The critical observation: no drafting fix would have avoided the §2038 inclusion because the revocable trust served a legitimate probate-avoidance purpose that required the retained power to revoke. If Katherine had instead used irrevocable trust structures (an ILIT for the real estate, an IDGT for the marketable securities) to move assets out of her gross estate before death, the $32.2M estate would have been reduced substantially — perhaps by $15-20M — with corresponding transfer-tax savings of $6-8M at the 40% federal rate plus material New York state estate tax savings. The revocable trust delivered $80,000 of probate-avoidance value but cost tens of millions in unrecovered estate-tax planning opportunity.

Case 2: The grantor-as-trustee "comfort" standard failure

Michael (67) is a Massachusetts resident with an $18 million estate. In 2019, on his attorney's recommendation, he established the "Michael Family Irrevocable Trust" and funded it with $8 million of marketable securities. The trust names Michael and his brother David as co-trustees, with a distribution power to distribute income and principal to Michael's three adult children "for their comfortable maintenance, welfare, and enjoyment." Michael reports a $8M taxable gift on Form 709, using his §2010(c) exemption, expecting the trust corpus to grow outside his gross estate for the benefit of his children.

Michael dies in 2026 with the trust corpus grown to $11 million. The IRS challenges the transaction under §2038(a)(1).

The Tax Court analysis (hypothetical, applying Old Colony Trust and Estate of Ford):

  • §2038(a)(1) power. Michael as co-trustee (with brother David) held a discretionary distribution power over trust income and principal. The distribution standard — "comfortable maintenance, welfare, and enjoyment" — includes three components: "comfortable maintenance" (arguably HEMS-equivalent under Vissering as a matter of Massachusetts state law), "welfare" (categorically fails under Estate of Jennings), and "enjoyment" (categorically fails under Estate of Ford). Because the standard as a whole includes "welfare" and "enjoyment," the ascertainable-standard exception fails.
  • Joint-power reach. The fact that Michael held the power jointly with his brother David does not defeat §2038 — David is not an "adverse party" whose interest in the property is adverse to the exercise of the power in favor of Michael personally (David has no beneficial interest in the trust; his adverse status runs, if at all, to the beneficiaries, not to Michael).
  • Fiduciary-duty defense. Unavailable under §2038 — the "in whatever capacity exercisable" clause forecloses the argument that Michael's trustee-fiduciary duty to the beneficiaries defeats the retained power.
  • Inclusion amount. The full $11 million trust corpus at date of death, because the distribution power reaches the entire corpus.

Federal consequence: Michael's gross estate is $18M (non-trust assets, minus the $8M used as the 2019 gift and now returned via §2038) + $11M (trust corpus pulled back) = the accounting is complicated because the 2019 gift is unwound by the §2038 inclusion. The gross estate is $10M other assets + $11M trust corpus = $21M. The $8M of §2010(c) exemption used on the 2019 Form 709 is restored by operation of the §2038 inclusion (the trust corpus is now included as if the gift had never happened). Available §2010(c) exemption is the full $15M. Taxable estate: $21M − $15M = $6M. Federal estate tax at 40%: $2.4M. Absent the §2038 inclusion (if the trust had used a pure HEMS standard from inception), the trust corpus would have been outside the gross estate at Michael's death, gross estate would have been $10M with the $8M of Form 709 gifts as adjusted taxable gifts under §2001(b)(1)(B), and federal estate tax would have been $8M + $10M − $15M = $3M × 40% = $1.2M — savings of $1.2M.

Massachusetts consequence: Massachusetts estate tax on a $21M estate above the $2M state exemption at the state's graduated schedule (max 16%) produces roughly $2.3 million of state estate tax. Without the §2038 inclusion, the Massachusetts gross estate would have been $10M with estimated Massachusetts estate tax of approximately $900,000. Total drafting-failure cost: approximately $2.6 million in federal-plus-state transfer tax.

Case 3: The joint-trustee spouse arrangement

Rachel (71) and her husband Daniel (73) are California residents with a $35 million community-property estate. In 2020, on their attorney's recommendation, they established two irrevocable "Family Legacy Trusts" — one funded by each spouse with $6 million of their respective one-half community-property interests. The trusts each name the settlor spouse and the non-settlor spouse as joint co-trustees with a discretionary distribution power to their two adult children for the children's "health, education, maintenance, support, and comfortable enjoyment of life."

Rachel dies in 2026. Her trust corpus has grown to $8.5 million. The IRS challenges the transaction under §2038(a)(1) and §2036(a)(2).

The Tax Court analysis:

  • §2038(a)(1) power. Rachel as co-trustee (with Daniel) held a discretionary distribution power. The standard includes "comfortable enjoyment of life" — categorically fails the ascertainable-standard test under Estate of Ford; a HEMS-plus-enjoyment standard is not HEMS.
  • Joint-power reach. Daniel is Rachel's spouse but not an adverse party to Rachel's exercise of the power — Daniel has no beneficial interest in Rachel's trust (the beneficiaries are the children), so Daniel's presence does not defeat §2038.
  • §2036 overlap. The same distribution discretion is also a §2036(a)(2) designation power. Because §2036 typically produces the larger inclusion (full transferred property, no fiduciary-duty defense post-Byrum-reversal), the IRS argues under §2036 as the primary theory with §2038 as the fallback.
  • Inclusion amount. The full $8.5 million trust corpus at date of death.

Federal consequence: Rachel's gross estate is $8.5M (trust corpus) + $9M (her one-half community share of non-trust assets) = $17.5M. Available §2010(c) exemption (assume the 2020 $6M gift is restored) is $15M. Taxable estate: $2.5M. Federal estate tax at 40%: $1M. Because California has no state estate tax, there is no state-level transfer-tax exposure. Total federal transfer tax: $1M.

The counterfactual with a properly-drafted trust (pure HEMS standard, independent institutional trustee instead of the spouses as joint trustees) would have kept the $8.5M trust corpus outside Rachel's gross estate. Rachel's gross estate would have been $9M with $6M of pre-death gifts as adjusted taxable gifts under §2001(b)(1)(B). Federal estate tax: $6M + $9M − $15M = $0 taxable estate, $0 federal estate tax. Total drafting-failure cost: $1 million in federal transfer tax that would have been fully avoided by using a HEMS standard and vesting distribution discretion in an independent trustee.

Six recurring mistakes in §2038 planning

  1. Selling the revocable living trust as an estate-tax reduction strategy. A revocable living trust does not save a single dollar of federal estate tax. The classical §2038(a)(1) power to revoke means full inclusion. Practitioners who oversell revocable living trusts as an estate-tax reduction strategy misinform their clients. The revocable trust is a probate-avoidance and privacy vehicle only; federal-transfer-tax reduction requires irrevocable-trust structures where the grantor has genuinely relinquished the §2038 power.
  2. Using a distribution standard broader than pure HEMS in a grantor-as-trustee arrangement. Old Colony Trust Co. v. United States is unforgiving. "Comfort," "welfare," "happiness," "enjoyment," "best interests," and "well-being" all fail the ascertainable-standard test and produce full §2038 inclusion. If the grantor must serve as trustee, the distribution standard must be pure HEMS (or a HEMS-plus-emergencies variant that state law would construe as HEMS-equivalent). Every syllable outside HEMS carries §2038 risk.
  3. Naming the spouse as joint co-trustee to preserve family control. The joint-power reach of §2038 captures spouses just as it captures third parties. A grantor-and-spouse joint discretionary distribution power is a full §2038 power on the grantor's estate. The spouse's independent status, beneficial interest, or otherwise-adverse position rarely satisfies the narrow adverse-party carve-out. Family-trust control is preserved through trust-protector powers held by a non-spouse party, not through joint trusteeship.
  4. Retaining a "personal" power to change trustees that includes related or subordinate parties. Under §672(c), a related or subordinate party is a spouse, parent, sibling, descendant, or employee of the grantor. A grantor's retained power to replace the trustee with a related or subordinate party is treated as a retained trustee power — the grantor is deemed to hold whatever powers the replacement trustee could hold. Rev. Rul. 95-58 provides a safe harbor: the retained trustee-replacement power is not a §2038 power if the replacement pool is limited to parties who are not related or subordinate to the grantor under §672(c).
  5. Failing to update trust drafting to reflect the state-law jurisdiction where the trust is administered. Ascertainable-standard analysis under Vissering v. Commissioner is a state-law question. Language that would fail the federal test under a general reading may nonetheless pass under the trust-situs state's fiduciary-accounting jurisprudence, and vice versa. Trust documents administered under the law of a particular state should use standard-of-distribution language that the state's own supreme court has construed as HEMS-equivalent, not language borrowed from a form book drafted for a different jurisdiction.
  6. Assuming the §675(4)(C) substitution power alone is sufficient to make an IDGT work. Rev. Rul. 2008-22 protects the bare §675(4)(C) power from §2038. It does not protect a trust that also contains other retained powers. An IDGT with a §675(4)(C) substitution power plus a grantor-as-trustee distribution discretion beyond HEMS is a full §2038 trust despite the ruling. The IDGT design must be reviewed as a whole package; the ruling is a shield only for the specific power it addresses.

Action checklist — before executing any transfer with §2038 exposure

  1. Identify every retained power in every trust document. Walk through each trust and list every power the grantor has retained — power to distribute, power to invest, power to remove or replace the trustee, power to add or remove beneficiaries, power to change beneficial shares, power to change situs, power to amend administrative provisions, power to substitute assets, power to loan trust assets to the grantor. Every retained power is a potential §2038 issue.
  2. Categorize each retained power under the Category A / B / C hierarchy. Category A powers (pure ministerial, HEMS-limited distribution, bare §675(4)(C)) are reliably safe. Category B powers (non-HEMS distribution discretion, add-or-remove beneficiaries, change shares, bare revocation) reliably produce full §2038 inclusion. Category C powers (ambiguous distribution language, joint powers with spouses, trustee-replacement powers) require state-law and drafting-specific analysis.
  3. Vest distribution discretion in an independent trustee. Do not name the grantor as sole or co-trustee of any trust where distribution discretion is not limited by a pure HEMS ascertainable standard. Use an independent institutional trustee or a professional individual trustee with no beneficial interest in the trust. This is the single most reliable structural fix.
  4. Use pure HEMS distribution language. "Health, education, maintenance, or support" — nothing more, nothing less. Avoid "comfort," "welfare," "happiness," "enjoyment," "best interests," and "well-being" in any grantor-as-trustee distribution standard. A HEMS-plus-emergencies variant is generally safe; a HEMS-plus-enjoyment variant reliably fails.
  5. Limit trust-protector powers appropriately. A trust protector can preserve family influence without triggering §2038, provided the trust protector is not the grantor and the trust protector's powers are properly circumscribed (typically responding to tax-law changes, changing situs, changing administrative provisions, and replacing the trustee with another non-related, non-subordinate trustee). Grant no trust-protector power to distribute among beneficiaries, add or remove beneficiaries, or change beneficial shares.
  6. Limit trustee-replacement powers to non-related, non-subordinate replacements. The Rev. Rul. 95-58 safe harbor requires that the replacement pool exclude parties who are related or subordinate to the grantor under §672(c). Drafting the trust-protector or grantor-held trustee-replacement power with an appropriately narrow replacement pool is a low-cost, high-value structural safeguard.
  7. Document the ascertainable-standard analysis contemporaneously. When drafting a grantor-as-trustee trust with HEMS distribution discretion, include a contemporaneous memorandum to file analyzing the standard under the trust-situs state's fiduciary-accounting jurisprudence. Cite the leading state-court cases construing HEMS-equivalent language. This memo becomes the reference document if the IRS later challenges the ascertainable-standard analysis.
  8. Coordinate with state-level exposure. §2038 inclusions can produce state estate-tax cost in the 12 state-estate-tax jurisdictions and D.C. even when federal §2010(c) fully absorbs the taxable estate. Model the state consequence explicitly, particularly in Oregon, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Minnesota where the state exemption is materially below the federal $15M threshold. A drafting failure that produces a $600,000 Massachusetts state estate tax bill is a real cost even if the federal cash-tax effect is zero.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2038 do in 2026?

IRC §2038 is the revocable-transfer rule in Chapter 11 of the Internal Revenue Code. It pulls into the decedent's gross estate the full date-of-death fair market value of any property that the decedent transferred during life if, at the date of the decedent's death, the enjoyment of the property was subject to a power (in the decedent alone or in the decedent in conjunction with any other person, and whether or not exercisable in a fiduciary capacity) to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate the transfer — or if any such power was relinquished by the decedent during the 3-year period ending on the date of death, per the §2035(a) lookback. §2038 is the section that makes every classical revocable living trust — the workhorse probate-avoidance vehicle used in tens of millions of American estates — a full gross-estate-inclusion vehicle. It is also the section that traps grantors who name themselves as trustee of an irrevocable trust with distribution discretion, and the section that reaches jointly-held powers even where the decedent could not act alone.

How does §2038 differ from §2036 — the two overlapping retained-interest sections?

§2036 and §2038 overlap materially. §2036(a)(2) reaches a retained power to designate who shall possess or enjoy the property or its income. §2038(a)(1) reaches a retained power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate the transfer. Many powers fit both descriptions — a power to shift distributions among trust beneficiaries is both a §2036(a)(2) designation power and a §2038(a)(1) alter-amend power. When both sections apply, the section producing the larger inclusion controls under Treas. Reg. §20.2038-1(a). The key operational differences: (1) §2038 explicitly reaches powers held "in conjunction with any other person" regardless of whether the joint holder is adverse, while §2036(a)(2) reaches the same joint powers per United States v. O'Malley 383 U.S. 627 (1966); (2) §2038 explicitly captures powers "whether or not exercisable in a fiduciary capacity" foreclosing the grantor-as-trustee-with-fiduciary-duty defense that Byrum briefly opened under §2036 before Congress reversed it in 1976; (3) §2036 requires the retention to have been for the decedent's life, for a period not ascertainable without reference to death, or for a period that does not in fact end before death, while §2038 turns on whether the power existed at death (or was relinquished within three years); (4) §2038 inclusion is limited to the value of the property subject to the power (which can be less than the full trust corpus when the power reaches only a portion), while §2036 typically produces full inclusion of the transferred property.

Is a revocable living trust included in the grantor's gross estate?

Yes — 100%, always. The classical revocable living trust that is the workhorse of American probate-avoidance planning is the paradigmatic §2038(a)(1) transfer. The grantor's retained power to revoke the trust and pull the assets back into the grantor's own hands at any time before death is precisely the power to revoke that §2038(a)(1) targets. Every dollar of trust corpus at date of death is included in the grantor's gross estate at date-of-death fair market value. This is not a bug in the revocable living trust; it is the whole point. The trust avoids probate under state trust law but does not remove the assets from the federal gross estate, so it delivers procedural benefits (privacy, out-of-state real estate consolidation, incapacity management, avoidance of probate fees) without any federal transfer-tax benefit. Practitioners who oversell revocable living trusts as an estate-tax reduction strategy are mistaken; the grantor gets a §1014 basis step-up on the trust assets at death precisely because they are gross-estate-included, and pays gift tax on nothing because the revocable transfer is not a completed gift for §2501/2511 purposes under Treas. Reg. §25.2511-2(c).

What is the ascertainable-standard exception and does it save a grantor-as-trustee arrangement?

The ascertainable-standard doctrine is the primary structural escape from §2038 for a grantor who wants to serve as trustee of an irrevocable trust. A trustee power limited by an ascertainable standard relating to the beneficiary's health, education, maintenance, or support (the classical HEMS standard) is treated as no power at all for §2038 purposes, on the theory that the power holder has no discretion to shift enjoyment among the class — the beneficiary's HEMS needs are objectively ascertainable and reviewable by a court in a fiduciary-accounting proceeding. Old Colony Trust Co. v. United States, 423 F.2d 601 (1st Cir. 1970), is the leading authority; the First Circuit held that a trustee power to invade principal for a beneficiary's "comfort, support, welfare and enjoyment" failed the ascertainable standard because "comfort" and "welfare" are not objectively measurable, while a power limited to HEMS would have escaped §2038. The narrower the standard, the safer the trustee-power arrangement: a pure HEMS standard reliably passes; a HEMS-plus-comfort standard reliably fails. Treas. Reg. §20.2041-1(c)(2) (dealing with §2041 general powers of appointment but incorporating the same doctrine) codifies the analysis. The ascertainable-standard exception does not, however, save a power to add or remove beneficiaries or to change beneficial shares — those remain full §2038 powers regardless of any distribution standard.

Does a grantor's power to substitute trust assets under §675(4)(C) trigger §2038?

No. Rev. Rul. 2008-22, 2008-16 I.R.B. 796, holds that a grantor's retained power (or a power held by a nonadverse party) to reacquire trust corpus by substituting property of equivalent value — the IRC §675(4)(C) substitution power that is the standard IDGT (intentionally-defective grantor trust) toggle for grantor-trust income-tax status — does not, standing alone, cause gross-estate inclusion under either §2036 or §2038, provided (a) the trustee has a fiduciary obligation under the governing instrument or applicable local law to ensure equivalent value of substituted assets and (b) the substitution power cannot be exercised in a manner that could shift benefits among trust beneficiaries. The ruling expressly declines to extend to substitution powers held together with other retained powers that would independently trigger §2036 or §2038. The practical upshot: a properly-drafted IDGT with a bare §675(4)(C) substitution power and no other retained powers achieves grantor-trust income-tax status without §2038 gross-estate inclusion. Adding other retained powers to the same trust reopens the §2038 analysis on the full package.

What is the §2038(b) date-of-existence provision and why does it matter?

§2038(b) provides that, for purposes of §2038, a power to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate shall be considered to exist on the date of the decedent's death even though (1) the exercise of the power is subject to a precedent giving of notice or (2) the alteration, amendment, revocation, or termination takes effect only on the expiration of a stated period after the exercise of the power, whether or not on or before the date of the decedent's death notice has been given or the power has been exercised. The provision closes what would otherwise be a large exception: a decedent who held a fully-effective power to revoke a trust — but where the trust document provided that revocation required 60 days' notice or was effective only on the last day of the calendar quarter following the exercise — would, absent §2038(b), have been able to argue that no power "existed" at the moment of death if death happened during a notice or waiting period without a pending exercise. §2038(b) rejects that argument categorically. The power exists at death whenever the decedent could have exercised it at any time before death, regardless of built-in delays or notice periods. The provision was added to prevent a common drafting evasion in the years immediately following the enactment of the modern §2038.

Does §2038 apply to a power held jointly with another person?

Yes. §2038(a)(1) explicitly covers a power that the decedent could exercise "alone or by the decedent in conjunction with any other person" — including a power held jointly with a beneficiary of the trust, jointly with the grantor's spouse, or jointly with a co-trustee. The reach parallels §2036(a)(2) as construed by United States v. O'Malley, 383 U.S. 627 (1966). The single most important pattern: a grantor who names their spouse as co-trustee with the grantor and gives the co-trustees jointly the discretionary power to distribute trust income or principal among the children has retained a §2038 power. The full trust corpus comes back into the grantor's gross estate. The co-trustee's independent status — including as a beneficiary of the trust or as a party otherwise adverse to further transfers — does not defeat the joint-power reach under §2038; the narrow §2038 exception for powers held jointly with an adverse party turns on whether the adverse party's interest in the property is adverse to the exercise of the power in favor of the decedent, not adverse to the beneficiary. In practice the adverse-party exception is very narrow and rarely saves a joint-power arrangement.

How does §2038 interact with the §2035(a) three-year lookback?

§2035(a) pulls into the gross estate the value of any property that would have been included under §§2036, 2037, 2038, or 2042 if the decedent had retained the interest until death — provided the decedent transferred the property, or released the retained interest, within three years of death. The interaction with §2038 matters most in the deathbed release scenario: a decedent who releases a §2038 power to alter or revoke within three years of death sees the property pulled back into the gross estate as if the release had never happened. A grantor who resigns as trustee of an irrevocable trust with distribution discretion, hoping to shed §2038 exposure, has therefore not escaped inclusion unless the grantor survives the resignation by more than three years. The §2035(d) bona fide sale exception mirrors §2038(c)'s bona fide sale carve-out. The practical planning implication: release of a §2038 power is a three-year gamble; complete decoupling of the grantor's retained control at the trust's inception is the only structural solution.

Can a grantor safely serve as trustee of their own irrevocable trust in 2026?

It depends entirely on what powers the grantor-trustee holds. A grantor who serves as trustee with only ministerial powers (custody, investment within a standard prudent-investor duty, tax and accounting administration) has no §2038 power and no gross-estate inclusion. A grantor-trustee with distribution powers limited by a pure ascertainable HEMS standard has no §2038 power under Old Colony Trust Co. v. United States. A grantor-trustee with any discretionary power to distribute among beneficiaries not limited by HEMS, or any power to add or remove beneficiaries, or any power to change beneficial shares, has full §2038 exposure — the entire trust corpus comes back into the grantor's gross estate. The safest structural rule: name an independent institutional trustee (or a professional individual trustee with no beneficial interest in the trust) as sole trustee for any distribution-discretion trust. A trust protector with a narrow amendment authority — typically limited to responding to changes in tax law, changing situs, changing administrative provisions, or replacing the trustee — can preserve family influence without triggering §2038, so long as the trust protector's powers are properly circumscribed and the grantor is not the trust protector. Directed-trust structures under state statutes such as Del. Code Ann. tit. 12 §3313 (Delaware directed-trust statute) permit the grantor to hold investment direction without holding distribution power, safely bifurcating the roles.

How does §2038 interact with the $15 million §2010(c) basic exclusion amount under the OBBBA?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 2025) §70106 permanently set the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 for estates of decedents dying and gifts made after December 31, 2025, indexed for inflation. §2038 operates independently of §2010(c): property pulled into the gross estate under §2038 is included at date-of-death fair market value, and the §2010(c) unified credit — worth $15,000,000 in 2026 — then offsets the resulting tentative tax on the aggregate taxable estate. For estates well below the $15M threshold, a §2038 inclusion may increase the taxable estate but produce no cash tax if the exemption still absorbs the total. For estates near or above the threshold, §2038 can transform what looked like a completed lifetime transfer into $15M+ of gross-estate inclusion at the 40% top rate under §2001. Because a revocable living trust is 100% §2038-included, no §2038 planning is required for the revocable trust itself; the analysis matters for irrevocable trusts where the grantor retained any §2038-triggering power and for the DSUE-portability effect under §2010(c)(4), where a §2038 inclusion consumes exemption at the first spouse's death dollar-for-dollar and reduces the DSUE that ports to the surviving spouse.

  11. Treas. Reg. §25.2511-2(c) — Companion gift-tax regulation: a transfer to a revocable trust is not a completed gift for §2501/§2511 purposes because the grantor retains the power to revoke; the completed gift happens on the grantor's death, at which point §2038 (not gift tax) captures the transfer. ecfr.gov (26 CFR §25.2511-2(c))
  12. Treas. Reg. §20.2041-1(c)(2) — Ascertainable-standard doctrine: a power to consume, invade, or appropriate property limited by an ascertainable standard relating to the beneficiary's health, education, support, or maintenance is not a general power of appointment. The same doctrine is applied to §2038 powers by analogy. ecfr.gov (26 CFR §20.2041-1(c)(2))
  13. Estate of Bosch v. Commissioner, 387 U.S. 456 (1967) — Supreme Court decision holding that federal estate-tax courts are bound by state supreme court determinations of state-law property rights (but not by lower state-court determinations). Controlling authority on the state-law dimension of §2038 analysis. scholar.google.com (Bosch)
  14. United States v. O'Malley, 383 U.S. 627 (1966) — Supreme Court decision under §2036(a)(2) (with parallel §2038 reach) holding that the retained power need not be exercisable in the grantor's sole discretion; a power exercisable "in conjunction with any other person" is sufficient. Read into §2038 by parallel construction. scholar.google.com (O'Malley)
  15. Treas. Reg. §20.2038-1(e) — Interpretive gloss on §2038(b): the power is treated as existing at death whenever the decedent could have exercised it at any time before death, without regard to notice or waiting-period provisions. ecfr.gov (26 CFR §20.2038-1(e))
  16. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §7872 — Below-market loan rules; the applicable federal rate published monthly under §1274 sets the minimum-interest floor for intra-family and IDGT installment sales that must pass the §2038(c) bona fide sale test. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §7872)
  17. Treas. Reg. §20.2043-1(a) — Interaction of the bona fide sale exception with the partial-consideration rule of §2043(a): where the transfer is for less than adequate and full consideration, the amount included is limited to the excess of fair market value over the consideration received. ecfr.gov (26 CFR §20.2043-1)
  18. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §1014(a) — Basis of property acquired from a decedent; property included in the gross estate under §§2036, 2037, 2038, or 2042 receives a basis step-up (or step-down) to date-of-death fair market value. The §1014 step-up is the compensating income-tax benefit of the §2038 gross-estate inclusion for revocable-trust assets. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §1014)
  19. Treas. Reg. §25.2511-2(c) — Same companion citation for the gift-tax non-completion analysis of transfers to a revocable trust; see also Sanford's Estate v. Commissioner, 308 U.S. 39 (1939), the leading Supreme Court authority on completed-gift analysis of transfers subject to a retained revocation power. scholar.google.com (Sanford's Estate)
  20. Old Colony Trust Co. v. United States, 423 F.2d 601 (1st Cir. 1970) — Leading appellate authority on the ascertainable-standard doctrine under §2038: trustee power to invade principal for a beneficiary's "comfort, support, welfare and enjoyment" failed the ascertainable-standard test; §2038 inclusion. Cited in every subsequent §2038 ascertainable-standard case. scholar.google.com (Old Colony Trust)
  21. Estate of Jennings v. Commissioner, 10 T.C. 323 (1948) — Early Tax Court authority holding that a trustee power to distribute principal "as may be necessary for the proper support, care, and welfare" of the beneficiary failed the ascertainable-standard test because of the "welfare" component. Foundational §2038 case. scholar.google.com (Jennings)
  22. Estate of Ford v. Commissioner, 53 T.C. 114 (1969), aff'd 450 F.2d 878 (2d Cir. 1971) — Second Circuit affirmed §2038 inclusion where trustee power was to distribute principal "in the trustee's uncontrolled discretion for the beneficiary's happiness"; "happiness" held to be a subjective unascertainable standard. Canonical failure-of-ascertainable-standard case. scholar.google.com (Estate of Ford)
  23. Estate of Vissering v. Commissioner, 990 F.2d 578 (10th Cir. 1993) — Tenth Circuit reversed a Tax Court §2038 inclusion where the trustee power was to distribute principal for the beneficiary's "medical care, comfortable maintenance and welfare," reading the language under Florida state law as constrained to a HEMS equivalent. Establishes the state-law dimension of the ascertainable-standard analysis. scholar.google.com (Vissering)
  24. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §675(4)(C) — Grantor-trust income-tax provision: the grantor's retained power to reacquire trust corpus by substituting property of equivalent value causes grantor-trust income-tax status under Subchapter J. The substitution power is the standard IDGT toggle. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §675)
  25. Rev. Rul. 2008-22, 2008-16 I.R.B. 796 — IRS ruling that a grantor's §675(4)(C) substitution power, standing alone, does not cause §2036 or §2038 gross-estate inclusion provided (i) the trustee has a fiduciary obligation to ensure equivalent value and (ii) the substitution power cannot shift benefits among beneficiaries. Doctrinal foundation of the modern IDGT. irs.gov (Rev. Rul. 2008-22)
  26. Del. Code Ann. tit. 12 §3313 — Delaware directed-trust statute: allows bifurcation of trustee roles so that the grantor may hold investment-direction authority while an independent administrative trustee holds distribution power. Analogous statutes exist in South Dakota (S.D. Codified Laws §55-1B), Nevada (Nev. Rev. Stat. §163), Tennessee (Tenn. Code Ann. §35-15-1201 et seq.), and other trust-friendly jurisdictions. delcode.delaware.gov (Del. Code tit. 12 §3313)
  27. Rev. Rul. 95-58, 1995-2 C.B. 191 — IRS safe harbor for grantor-retained power to replace trustees: the power is not a retained §2038 (or §2036) power if the replacement trustee cannot be related or subordinate to the grantor under §672(c). Cornerstone of modern trust-protector and successor-trustee drafting. irs.gov (Rev. Rul. 95-58)
  28. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §2035(a) — Three-year lookback rule pulling into the gross estate the value of property transferred within three years of death that would have been included under §§2036, 2037, 2038, or 2042 if the decedent had retained the interest until death. Handles §2038 releases. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §2035)
  29. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §2036 — Companion retained-life-estate rule; overlaps materially with §2038. Where both sections apply, Treas. Reg. §20.2038-1(a) provides that §2038 controls only where it produces a larger inclusion than §2036; otherwise §2036 controls. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §2036)
  30. United States v. Byrum, 408 U.S. 125 (1972); Tax Reform Act of 1976 §2009(a) — Byrum briefly opened a fiduciary-duty defense under §2036(a)(1); Congress reversed via §2036(b) in the 1976 Tax Reform Act. The Byrum defense was never available under §2038 because §2038 explicitly reaches powers "in whatever capacity exercisable." scholar.google.com (Byrum)
  31. Instructions for Form 706, United States Estate (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return — Schedule G reporting for §§2035/2036/2037/2038 transfers, including revocable-trust inclusion under §2038(a)(1) and grantor-as-trustee retained-power inclusion. State-return conformity is generally by statutory cross-reference to the federal gross-estate composition. irs.gov (Form 706 Instructions)
