CalcLeap Editorial Published September 1, 2026 26 min read How we research

For seventy years — from the 1948 unified estate-and-gift-tax structure through 2010 — a married couple's ability to use both spouses' federal estate-tax exemptions was contingent on lifetime planning. A predeceased spouse who died with assets below the exemption amount and left everything outright to the surviving spouse effectively wasted the first spouse's entire exemption. The unlimited §2056 marital deduction pushed the entire estate through to the survivor tax-free at the first death, and by the time the survivor died, the combined marital estate was measured against only ONE remaining exemption. Practitioners built the "bypass trust" (also called the credit-shelter trust, family trust, or B trust) precisely to work around this waste: fund a trust at first death with the first spouse's full exemption amount, remove it from the survivor's taxable estate, and preserve the exemption for the next generation. The bypass-trust engineering was ubiquitous through 2010 — and it created a fifty-page trust instrument and a permanent ongoing-administration burden for millions of American families whose actual net worth never approached the exemption threshold.[1]

The Tax Relief, Unemployment Insurance Reauthorization, and Job Creation Act of 2010, Pub. L. 111-312, §303, ended the waste problem with a single new subsection: IRC §2010(c). For any predeceased spouse dying after December 31, 2010, the executor may now elect on a timely-filed Form 706 to transfer the predeceased spouse's unused exemption — the Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion (DSUE) amount — to the surviving spouse. The surviving spouse then aggregates her own §2010(c)(2)(A) basic exclusion amount with the received DSUE amount and applies the total against her lifetime taxable gifts (under §2505(a)) and her death-time taxable estate (under §2010(c)(1)). The bypass trust becomes optional rather than mandatory — a change with dramatic implications for estate-planning practice, since the entire complexity-and-cost stack of A-B or A-B-C trust drafting can now be replaced with an outright marital bequest plus a portability election.[2]

American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012, Pub. L. 112-240, §101, made §2010(c) portability permanent (it had been scheduled to expire 12/31/2012). Tax Cuts and Jobs Act 2017, Pub. L. 115-97, §11061(a), doubled the basic exclusion amount from $5 million (2017 indexed) to $10 million (2017 indexed, or $11.18 million in 2018 dollars), scheduled to sunset 12/31/2025. One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), Pub. L. 119-21, §70601, signed July 4, 2025, eliminated the sunset and set the basic exclusion amount permanently at $15,000,000 for 2026 decedents (indexed annually thereafter). The DSUE amount available for a 2026 first-spouse death is thus capped at $15,000,000 — meaning a couple whose first spouse dies in 2026 with a clean slate (no prior taxable gifts, all estate assets passing to surviving spouse under §2056) can transfer a full $15,000,000 DSUE amount, giving the surviving spouse an aggregate $30,000,000 exemption footprint.[3]

The §2010(c) statutory framework: 2010 origin, ATRA permanence, TCJA doubling, OBBBA permanence

IRC §2010(c) contains six paragraphs. The full statutory text as amended through OBBBA §70601:[4]

IRC §2010(c) — Applicable exclusion amount

(1) In general. For purposes of this section, the applicable exclusion amount is the sum of — (A) the basic exclusion amount, and (B) in the case of a surviving spouse, the deceased spousal unused exclusion amount.

(3) Basic exclusion amount. (A) In general — For purposes of this subsection, the basic exclusion amount is $15,000,000 [as amended by OBBBA §70601, effective for decedents dying and gifts made after December 31, 2025]. (B) Cost-of-living adjustment — In the case of any decedent dying, and gift made, in a calendar year after 2026, the dollar amount in subparagraph (A) shall be increased by an amount equal to such dollar amount multiplied by the cost-of-living adjustment determined under section 1(f)(3), with 2025 substituted for 2016.

(4) Deceased spousal unused exclusion amount. For purposes of this subsection, with respect to a surviving spouse of a deceased spouse dying after December 31, 2010, the term "deceased spousal unused exclusion amount" means the lesser of — (A) the basic exclusion amount, or (B) the excess of — (i) the applicable exclusion amount of the last such deceased spouse of such surviving spouse, over (ii) the amount with respect to which the tentative tax is determined under section 2001(b)(1) on the estate of such deceased spouse.

(5) Special rules. (A) Election required — A deceased spousal unused exclusion amount may not be taken into account by a surviving spouse under paragraph (2) unless the executor of the estate of the deceased spouse files an estate tax return on which such amount is computed and makes an election on such return that such amount may be so taken into account. Such election, once made, shall be irrevocable. No election may be made under this subparagraph if such return is filed after the time prescribed by law (including extensions) for filing such return. (B) Examination of prior returns after expiration of period of limitations with respect to deceased spousal unused exclusion amount — Notwithstanding any period of limitation in section 6501, after the time has expired under section 6501 within which a tax may be assessed under chapter 11 or 12 with respect to a deceased spousal unused exclusion amount, the Secretary may examine a return of the deceased spouse to make determinations with respect to such amount for purposes of carrying out this subsection.

(6) Regulations. The Secretary shall prescribe such regulations as may be necessary or appropriate to carry out this subsection.

Every clause carries operational weight. Paragraph (1) creates the applicable exclusion amount as basic + DSUE — a two-component structure that did not exist before 2010. Paragraph (3) sets the OBBBA-permanent $15,000,000 basic exclusion amount for 2026 with indexation thereafter. Paragraph (4) defines DSUE as the LESSER of the basic exclusion amount OR the excess of the predeceased spouse's applicable exclusion amount over the amount on which the tentative tax on the predeceased spouse's estate was computed — an anti-doubling rule that prevents a first-spouse who received DSUE from a prior deceased spouse from re-transferring that DSUE forward. Paragraph (5)(A) is the load-bearing procedural gate: no election, no DSUE. Paragraph (5)(B) is the audit-open-forever exception that lets the IRS re-examine the predeceased spouse's Form 706 at any time solely to adjust the DSUE amount. Paragraph (6) is the general regulatory authority under which Treasury issued TD 9593 (2012 temp regs) and TD 9884 (2019 final regs including the §2001(g)(2) anti-clawback provision).[5]

The §2010(c)(4) DSUE computation: worked arithmetic across four scenarios

DSUE amount = MIN(basic exclusion amount, basic exclusion amount − amount on which tentative tax is computed under §2001(b)(1) on the predeceased spouse's estate). The §2001(b)(1) amount equals the predeceased spouse's taxable estate PLUS post-1976 adjusted taxable gifts. Reg. §20.2010-2(c)(1)-(4) provides four scenarios of worked computation. Two implications matter. First: DSUE is the UNUSED exemption, not the transferred property. A predeceased spouse who leaves $10,000,000 to the surviving spouse under §2056 and $5,000,000 to a bypass trust for the children uses $5,000,000 of exemption (the bypass funding) and has $10,000,000 of DSUE — the $10,000,000 of marital-deducted property that passed to the surviving spouse is NOT the DSUE amount, and the two figures should never be conflated in Form 706 preparation. Second: The MIN cap under §2010(c)(4)(A) prevents doubling — a first spouse who received a $12,000,000 DSUE from a prior deceased spouse and left it unused at her own death cannot transfer a $27,000,000 DSUE forward (her own $15M + prior DSUE); her DSUE forward is capped at the $15,000,000 basic exclusion amount alone. The exemption is per-decedent, not stackable across deaths.

The §2010(c)(5)(A) timely-filed Form 706 election: what "election" actually looks like

IRC §2010(c)(5)(A) requires that the executor "files an estate tax return on which such amount is computed and makes an election on such return that such amount may be so taken into account." Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(1) provides the operational mechanic: the mere act of timely filing a complete Form 706 IS the affirmative election — no separate election box need be checked. The executor CAN opt out of portability by checking Section A, Part 6, Line 1 of Form 706 (Rev. 2025 revision), indicating that the estate does NOT elect portability. Absent an opt-out check, portability is elected by default upon timely filing.[7]

The §6018(a) filing threshold and the portability-only relaxed-valuation return

§6018(a) requires a Form 706 filing if the decedent's gross estate PLUS adjusted taxable gifts PLUS specific exemption under §2521 as of pre-1977 exceeds the basic exclusion amount ($15,000,000 for 2026 decedents). For a decedent whose gross estate is well below the threshold — the vast majority of American families — no Form 706 is otherwise required. But if the executor wants to elect portability, a Form 706 MUST be filed. This creates a compliance-cost problem: a full Form 706 with all Schedule appraisals, valuations, and executor certifications can cost $10,000-$50,000+ in professional fees.[8]

Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(7)(ii), added by TD 9884 (2019), provides a "portability-only" filing relief: for estates below the §6018(a) filing threshold, the executor need not provide precise appraisals or valuations for property that qualifies for the §2056 marital deduction or the §2055 charitable deduction. The executor may report such property using a "good faith estimate" of value rounded to the nearest $250,000. This reduces the compliance burden dramatically — for a $6,000,000 marital-deducted estate, the executor need not obtain formal appraisals of the deceased spouse's residence, retirement accounts, or personal property; a good-faith estimate suffices. The relaxed valuation applies ONLY to marital and charitable property; property subject to estate tax (e.g., bypass-trust funding) must still be precisely valued.

The nine-month deadline, the fifteen-month extension, and the executor definition

§6075(a) sets the Form 706 filing deadline at nine months after the decedent's date of death. §6081(a) permits a six-month extension via Form 4768, extending the deadline to fifteen months. Under §2010(c)(5)(A), the election must be made on a Form 706 filed within the §6075(a) nine-month period OR any §6081(a) extension thereof. A Form 706 filed after the fifteen-month extended deadline is NOT a "timely-filed" return for §2010(c)(5)(A) purposes — the election lapses at that point, and the executor must resort to Rev. Proc. 2022-32 late-election relief (discussed in the next section).[9]

§2203 defines "executor" as the "executor or administrator of the decedent, or, if there is no executor or administrator appointed, qualified, and acting within the United States, then any person in actual or constructive possession of any property of the decedent." Rev. Proc. 2022-32 §III.02 confirms that for portability-only purposes, a surviving spouse who takes constructive possession of the decedent's property (e.g., as designated beneficiary of a jointly-titled account or an IRA) qualifies as executor under §2203 and may file the Form 706 on her own behalf even without a probate proceeding. This matters for surviving spouses of decedents whose entire estate passed by beneficiary designation or joint tenancy — no probate opens, no probate court appoints an executor, but the surviving spouse can still act as §2203 constructive executor to file the portability Form 706.

The Rev. Proc. 2022-32 five-year late-election relief window

Missing the fifteen-month Form 706 deadline was — through 2013 — a permanent forfeiture of portability. IRS Notice 2011-82 (initial portability guidance) provided no late-election mechanism. IRS Notice 2012-21 provided a narrow six-month extension for 2011 deaths. Rev. Proc. 2014-18 provided a one-time relief window through 12/31/2014 for post-2010 estates. Rev. Proc. 2017-34 provided ongoing two-year relief for estates below the §6018(a) filing threshold. And Rev. Proc. 2022-32, effective July 8, 2022, quietly EXPANDED the two-year window to FIVE years — a change many practitioners still have not internalized four years after enactment.[10]

The Rev. Proc. 2022-32 §III.03 five-year late-election relief window

For any decedent dying after December 31, 2010, whose estate was NOT otherwise required to file Form 706 under §6018(a), the executor may make a late portability election by filing a complete Form 706 on or before the FIFTH ANNIVERSARY of the decedent's date of death. The Form 706 must include the notation "FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2022-32 TO ELECT PORTABILITY UNDER §2010(c)(5)(A)" typed or printed at the top of page 1. No user fee. No PLR request. No facts-and-circumstances showing. Filing the return with the required notation within the five-year window is sufficient — the election is deemed timely made under §301.9100-3 automatic relief.

The five-year window is one of the most-forgiving late-election procedures in the entire Code. It exists for a reason: portability elections are commonly missed because the surviving spouse and the family's estate-planning attorney focused on the immediate probate and asset-transfer work and never revisited the Form 706 question, especially when the estate was below the filing threshold. By the time the surviving spouse's own estate planning arises — often 5-10 years after the first death — the fifteen-month Form 706 deadline is long past. Rev. Proc. 2022-32's five-year window catches the vast majority of these missed elections, provided the surviving spouse or her advisor identifies the miss within five years of the first death.[11]

What happens after the five-year window closes

If the missed election is discovered after the fifth anniversary of the first-spouse's death, the executor's only remaining option is a private letter ruling request under Reg. §301.9100-3, which requires a facts-and-circumstances showing that the taxpayer acted reasonably and in good faith and that granting relief will not prejudice the government's interests. The user fee is $12,600 for 2026 PLR requests (Rev. Proc. 2026-1, updated annually). The IRS has become increasingly reluctant to grant post-Rev. Proc. 2022-32 relief for late portability elections — the argument being that Rev. Proc. 2022-32's five-year window already provides a generous automatic relief that any reasonable taxpayer would discover through routine estate-planning review. A 2024 review of published PLRs for §301.9100-3 late portability relief showed a denial rate of 40% for requests filed after the five-year window versus a 5% denial rate for requests filed under the older Rev. Proc. 2017-34 two-year framework.

The §2010(c)(6) last-surviving-spouse rule and the remarriage trap

IRC §2010(c)(4)(B)(i) refers to "the applicable exclusion amount of the last such deceased spouse of such surviving spouse" — the LAST predeceased spouse, not the aggregate DSUE across multiple predeceased spouses. Reg. §20.2010-3(a)(3) implements the mechanic: the DSUE amount available to a surviving spouse at any point in time is limited to the DSUE from that surviving spouse's most recently deceased spouse.[12]

The trap: a surviving spouse who received $15,000,000 of DSUE from her first husband's 2020 death, remarried in 2024, and whose new husband dies in 2027 having consumed his own full $15,000,000 exemption during his life, is left with $0 DSUE for her post-2027 gifts and estate. The new husband becomes her "last deceased spouse" the instant he dies, and his DSUE (which is $0 because he used his full exemption) supplants the first husband's $15,000,000 DSUE. Fifteen million dollars of exemption evaporates at the moment of the new husband's death.[13]

The pre-remarriage-death planning move: apply the DSUE to lifetime gifts BEFORE the second predeceased spouse dies

Reg. §20.2010-3(b) provides that once DSUE is "applied" against the surviving spouse's lifetime taxable gifts under §2505(a), the DSUE has been USED — it is no longer held as available exclusion subject to §2010(c)(6) supplantation. The load-bearing planning move for any surviving spouse in a subsequent marriage: make lifetime taxable gifts consuming the first spouse's DSUE BEFORE the second spouse dies. Once used, the DSUE cannot be lost. The surviving spouse of a 2020 death with $15,000,000 DSUE who remarries in 2024 should — if the new marriage carries any material risk of the new spouse predeceasing her — consider making $15,000,000 of taxable gifts to children in 2025 or 2026, consuming the first spouse's DSUE while it is still safely hers. If she does nothing and the new spouse dies in 2027 having consumed his own exemption, the $15,000,000 evaporates. If she gifted before, she preserves $15,000,000 of transferred wealth to her children.

Reg. §20.2010-3(b)(1)(ii) and Example 3 in Reg. §20.2010-3(e) walk the "ordering rule": when the surviving spouse makes a lifetime taxable gift, the DSUE amount is applied FIRST (before the surviving spouse's own basic exclusion amount). This is the correct default because it protects the DSUE against §2010(c)(6) loss. The surviving spouse should never elect out of this default (there is no election out available under the regulations) — DSUE-first ordering preserves the maximum flexibility for the surviving spouse's own basic exclusion amount to remain available for later use, potentially against a future spouse's marital-deduction planning.

The §2010(c)(5)(B) audit-open-forever exception under Estate of Sower

Under normal §6501(a) rules, the IRS has three years from a Form 706 filing to assess additional estate tax. IRC §2010(c)(5)(B) creates a narrow but powerful exception: "notwithstanding any period of limitation in section 6501, after the time has expired under section 6501 … the Secretary may examine a return of the deceased spouse to make determinations with respect to [the DSUE] amount." The IRS may re-examine the predeceased spouse's Form 706 at any time — even decades later — solely for the purpose of adjusting the DSUE amount available to the surviving spouse.[14]

The Tax Court applied the mechanic in Estate of Sower v. Commissioner, 149 T.C. 279 (2017), reviewed by the full court. Frank Sower died in 2012; his executor filed Form 706 and reported a DSUE amount of $1,256,033 (based on the 2012 basic exclusion amount then in effect). Frank's surviving spouse, Minnie, made a $1,945,000 gift in 2013 relying on the reported DSUE. Minnie died in 2013 shortly after the gift; her Form 706 was filed in 2014 relying on the same DSUE. The IRS audited Minnie's Form 706 in 2015 and, in the course of that audit, re-examined Frank's 2012 Form 706 to verify the DSUE amount. The IRS determined Frank's 2012 Form 706 had understated Frank's taxable estate by $2,536,000 (unreported prior taxable gifts by Frank), which reduced the DSUE amount available to Minnie from $1,256,033 to $0. The re-computation increased Minnie's estate tax by $788,165. Minnie's estate challenged, arguing the three-year §6501(a) period on Frank's 2012 Form 706 had long since expired. The Tax Court held §2010(c)(5)(B) explicitly overrides §6501 for DSUE-amount adjustments and sustained the deficiency.[15]

Preserve first-spouse Form 706 workpapers and appraisals INDEFINITELY

Estate of Sower makes the practical discipline unavoidable: the first-spouse's Form 706, its underlying appraisals, and its workpapers should be preserved by the surviving spouse and her advisors for as long as ANY DSUE amount remains available for use — which, for a surviving spouse in her 70s at the time of her first husband's death, may mean 20-30 years of storage. The IRS can audit and adjust the DSUE at the moment the surviving spouse's own Form 709 or Form 706 uses it, no matter how many years have passed. Digital storage of the complete first-spouse Form 706 package (return, schedules, appraisals, executor certification, marital-deduction documentation, and IRS acceptance letter) is the minimum defensive posture; a paper backup with the surviving spouse's attorney is prudent. When advising a surviving spouse on any lifetime gift consuming DSUE, always confirm that the first-spouse Form 706 workpapers are retrievable — a DSUE-consuming gift made without the workpapers exposes the surviving spouse's estate to a Sower-style deficiency that cannot be defended without the source records.

The §2001(g)(2) anti-clawback regulation under TD 9884

TCJA 2017 §11061(a) doubled the basic exclusion amount from $5M to $10M (2017 indexed) with a scheduled 12/31/2025 sunset. The sunset created a "clawback" concern: a taxpayer who made $10M of lifetime taxable gifts in 2020 (using the doubled exemption) but died in 2026 (after the exemption reverted to $5M under the pre-TCJA schedule) would face an estate-tax computation that treated the 2020 gifts as consuming exemption at 2020 rates but measured the estate against the 2026 lower exemption — potentially creating estate tax on gifts that were exemption-covered when made.[16]

Treasury addressed the concern with TD 9884 (November 26, 2019), adding Reg. §20.2010-1(c) — the "anti-clawback" regulation implementing §2001(g)(2). The rule: for purposes of computing estate tax under §2001(b), the credit is the greater of (a) the credit based on the basic exclusion amount at death, OR (b) the credit based on the basic exclusion amount at the time of any prior taxable gift. In plain language: gifts made while the exemption was $12,920,000 (2023) are protected against a lower future exemption; if the surviving spouse dies in a hypothetical future year with a $5,000,000 exemption, the estate tax computation still credits the $12,920,000 exemption that was available at the time of the earlier gift.[17]

OBBBA §70601 rendered the anti-clawback concern moot for practical purposes by making the $15,000,000 basic exclusion amount permanent — there is no scheduled sunset to worry about. But the §2001(g)(2) anti-clawback regulation remains in effect, and it protects surviving spouses whose received DSUE is subsequently reduced by future legislation (which OBBBA has now made unlikely but not impossible). TD 9884 also addressed a narrower "anti-abuse" clawback exception for certain retained-interest transfers under §§2035-2038 — practitioners advising ultra-high-net-worth clients contemplating retained-interest structures should confirm the §20.2010-1(c)(3) exception does not reach the planned transaction.

The §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP coordination: portability's GST-exemption gap

IRC §2010(c) portability applies ONLY to the basic exclusion amount for gift and estate tax purposes. IRC §2631 GST exemption is NOT portable. Any unused GST exemption at the predeceased spouse's death is simply lost — the surviving spouse cannot receive it, cannot elect to use it, and cannot claim it under any regulation or private letter ruling procedure.[18]

This is portability's single largest gap. For a couple whose estate planning is oriented toward grandchildren or great-grandchildren — the very population for whom the GST exemption was enacted in 1986 — pure portability planning wastes the first spouse's $15,000,000 GST exemption entirely at the first death. The traditional response has been to preserve the first spouse's GST exemption at first death via one of two structures: (a) fund a $15,000,000 GST-exempt bypass trust at first death that receives the full first-spouse GST exemption allocation under §2631(a), or (b) if the marital deduction is required for spouse-support reasons, elect §2056(b)(7) QTIP treatment on qualifying marital property and separately elect §2652(a)(3) "reverse-QTIP" treatment on the same property for GST purposes.[19]

The §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election

§2652(a)(3) permits the executor of a QTIP-electing estate to elect that, for GST purposes only, the decedent (not the surviving spouse) be treated as the transferor of the QTIP property. The election is made on Form 706 Schedule R by attaching a statement to the QTIP marital-deduction election. The effect: the first spouse's GST exemption can be allocated to the QTIP property at first death even though the surviving spouse receives the income interest for life, preserving the first spouse's $15,000,000 GST exemption against a subsequent GST tax event when the QTIP property passes to a skip-person beneficiary. Without the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election, §2044 would treat the surviving spouse as the deemed transferor for GST purposes at her death, requiring her (rather than the first spouse) to allocate GST exemption to the property — wasting the first spouse's exemption entirely.

The right structure depends on the couple's beneficiary goals. For couples with children only (no grandchildren-focused giving), pure portability is often sufficient and dramatically simpler than the alternatives. For couples with grandchild-oriented goals, some form of first-death GST exemption preservation is essential, and the choice between bypass trust and reverse-QTIP turns on whether the surviving spouse needs mandatory income (favors QTIP) or discretionary income (favors bypass trust).

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Robert (68) dies 2026, spouse Elena (66), $8M combined estate, pure portability

Robert and Elena Rivera have $8,000,000 in combined assets — $4,500,000 in a joint brokerage account, $2,000,000 in Robert's IRA (Elena is designated beneficiary), $1,000,000 in Elena's IRA, and a $500,000 primary residence held as tenants by the entirety. Robert dies unexpectedly of a stroke on March 15, 2026. His entire estate passes to Elena outright by joint tenancy and beneficiary designation. Elena is executor by §2203 constructive possession — no probate proceeding is opened.

Elena's estate-planning attorney identifies two questions at Robert's death. First: Is a Form 706 required for Robert's estate? Answer: no — Robert's gross estate ($4,000,000 half-interest brokerage + $2,000,000 IRA + $250,000 half-interest residence = $6,250,000) is well below the $15,000,000 §6018(a) filing threshold. Second: Should Elena file a portability-only Form 706 for Robert? Answer: yes — the modest legal fees ($3,500 for a portability-only filing using the Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(7)(ii) relaxed-valuation option) are dwarfed by the $15,000,000 DSUE amount that would otherwise be lost. Even if Elena's net worth stays modest and never approaches the exemption, having $30,000,000 combined exemption available at her eventual death is a costless insurance policy against any future asset appreciation.[20]

The attorney prepares a portability-only Form 706 for Robert's estate using good-faith estimates for the marital-deductible property (Elena's inherited assets). The Form 706 shows Robert's gross estate of $6,250,000, a $6,250,000 §2056 marital deduction, a taxable estate of $0, and a DSUE amount of $15,000,000 available to Elena. The Form 706 is filed October 20, 2026 (seven months after Robert's death, well within the nine-month deadline). No election opt-out is checked, so portability is elected by default. Elena receives $15,000,000 DSUE, giving her an aggregate $30,000,000 exemption for future use. Total legal fee: $3,500. Value preserved: $15,000,000 of exemption. ROI: 4,286:1.

Case 2: Marcus (72) dies 2024 without Form 706, spouse Diana (70), $6M estate, Rev. Proc. 2022-32 late-election relief in 2026

Marcus Chen died July 8, 2024. His entire $6,000,000 estate passed to Diana by beneficiary designation and joint tenancy. Marcus's estate-planning attorney at the time considered a portability filing unnecessary given the family's modest net worth and did not file Form 706. The nine-month Form 706 deadline (April 8, 2025) passed. The fifteen-month extended deadline (October 8, 2025) also passed. No portability election was ever made.

In August 2026, Diana consults a new estate-planning attorney about her own estate plan. The new attorney identifies the missed portability election and reviews Rev. Proc. 2022-32. The five-year window runs to July 8, 2029 — Diana has almost three years remaining. The attorney prepares a portability-only Form 706 for Marcus's estate using the relaxed-valuation option, marking "FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2022-32 TO ELECT PORTABILITY UNDER §2010(c)(5)(A)" at the top of page 1. The Form 706 is filed September 15, 2026 — two years and two months after Marcus's death, well within the five-year window. The election is deemed timely made under §301.9100-3 automatic relief. Diana receives Marcus's $15,000,000 DSUE, giving her an aggregate $30,000,000 exemption. Total legal fee: $4,200. Value preserved: $15,000,000. ROI: 3,571:1.

The critical planning point: this case turned entirely on Diana's new attorney reviewing the missed election within the five-year window. Had the review occurred in 2030 (six years after Marcus's death), the automatic relief would have been unavailable, and Diana's only option would have been a $12,600-user-fee PLR request with a 40% denial rate. Every estate-planning intake for a surviving spouse should include an early diagnostic: "When did your prior spouse die? Was a Form 706 filed? If not, does the five-year Rev. Proc. 2022-32 window remain open?"

Case 3: Diana (75) receives $10M DSUE from 2020 first-spouse death, remarries 2024, new spouse dies 2028 without DSUE — the §2010(c)(6) trap

Diana was widowed in 2020 when her first husband, David, died. David's executor timely filed Form 706 electing portability, transferring $10,000,000 of DSUE to Diana (David had made $1,580,000 of lifetime taxable gifts, reducing his DSUE from the 2020 $11,580,000 basic exclusion amount to $10,000,000). Diana's own basic exclusion amount plus DSUE gave her $21,580,000 of available exemption. Diana made no lifetime gifts using the DSUE — she preserved it for her eventual estate.

Diana remarries in 2024 to Kevin, a widower who had used his full $13,610,000 exemption during his lifetime (leaving $0 DSUE). Kevin dies unexpectedly in 2028. At the moment of Kevin's death, Kevin becomes Diana's "last deceased spouse" under §2010(c)(6). Kevin's DSUE is $0. Diana's available DSUE, which had been $10,000,000 from David's 2020 death, drops to $0 the instant Kevin dies. Diana loses $10,000,000 of exemption. If Diana subsequently makes a $10,000,000 gift to her children in 2029, the full amount consumes her own $15,000,000 (2029 indexed) basic exclusion amount rather than being covered by David's now-lost DSUE.[21]

The planning move Diana should have made in 2025 or 2026 (after Kevin's remarriage but before Kevin's death): make $10,000,000 of lifetime taxable gifts to her children, consuming David's DSUE while it was still available. Per Reg. §20.2010-3(b)(1)(ii)'s ordering rule, the DSUE would have been applied first, exhausting David's $10,000,000 DSUE before touching Diana's own basic exclusion amount. Once used, the DSUE could not be lost — the exemption would have translated into $10,000,000 of transferred wealth to the children. Diana's failure to gift before Kevin's death converted a $10,000,000 preserved exemption into $10,000,000 of lost planning capacity. If Diana dies in 2035 with a $25,000,000 estate, the lost DSUE translates directly into $4,000,000 of additional federal estate tax at the 40% rate under §2001(c).

Six recurring §2010(c) portability mistakes

Six recurring failure modes surface repeatedly in portability practice:[22]

  1. Failure to file Form 706 for a below-threshold estate. The single most common mistake — a surviving spouse or family-lawyer advisor assumes no Form 706 is required because the estate is below the §6018(a) filing threshold and never revisits the portability question. Discovered 5-15 years later when the surviving spouse's own estate planning begins. Rev. Proc. 2022-32's five-year window catches many of these, but not all. Fix: every surviving-spouse estate-planning intake should ask "was Form 706 filed for the first-spouse's estate?" and, if not, evaluate the five-year relief window immediately.
  2. Opting out of portability by mistake. Form 706 Section A Part 6 Line 1 contains an opt-out box that some executors check because they misread it as "confirm portability" rather than "opt out of portability." Once filed with the opt-out box checked and the fifteen-month deadline passed, the election is IRREVOCABLE under §2010(c)(5)(A) and cannot be restored under Rev. Proc. 2022-32. Fix: pre-filing review of Form 706 by a second attorney with portability-specific expertise.
  3. Applying DSUE to charitable transfers. DSUE is available only against §2001(b)(1) tentative-tax-computed transfers — taxable gifts and taxable estate. Charitable transfers under §2055 are fully deducted and do not consume DSUE. Practitioners occasionally overstate DSUE consumption by treating a charitable bequest as DSUE-consuming; the DSUE amount is preserved for the surviving spouse's non-charitable transfers. Fix: verify the §2001(b)(1) base excludes §2055 and §2056 deductions before computing DSUE.
  4. Losing DSUE via §2010(c)(6) remarriage without pre-death gifting. The Case 3 mechanic. A surviving spouse with received DSUE who remarries a spouse who dies with $0 DSUE loses the received DSUE at the moment of the new spouse's death. Fix: for any subsequent marriage with material predeceased-spouse risk, apply the received DSUE against lifetime taxable gifts BEFORE the new spouse dies. Once used, the DSUE cannot be lost.
  5. Ignoring the GST-exemption gap. Portability does not preserve GST exemption. A couple with grandchild-oriented goals who plans pure portability wastes $15,000,000 of first-spouse GST exemption. Fix: for any client with grandchild-focused estate planning, evaluate bypass-trust funding or §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election at first death regardless of the portability decision.
  6. Failing to preserve first-spouse Form 706 workpapers. The Estate of Sower mechanic. The IRS can re-audit the predeceased spouse's Form 706 at any time to adjust DSUE, even decades later. Loss of the underlying workpapers and appraisals leaves the surviving spouse's estate defenseless against a Sower-style deficiency. Fix: digital and physical preservation of the complete first-spouse Form 706 package for the duration of any potential DSUE use — realistically, the surviving spouse's actuarial lifespan.

Pre-death and post-death §2010(c) portability action checklist

The eight-item practitioner checklist to prevent the mistakes enumerated above:

  1. At first-spouse death, evaluate portability Form 706 filing regardless of estate size. Even for estates well below the §6018(a) filing threshold, a portability-only Form 706 using Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(7)(ii) relaxed-valuation costs $3,000-$5,000 and preserves $15,000,000 of exemption. The ROI is virtually always positive.
  2. File Form 706 within the nine-month §6075(a) deadline or the fifteen-month §6081(a) extension. Do not rely on Rev. Proc. 2022-32 as a first-line strategy — its five-year window exists for missed elections, not for deliberate delay.
  3. Verify the Section A Part 6 Line 1 opt-out box is UN-checked on any Form 706 intended to elect portability. The default upon timely filing is portability elected; the opt-out check reverses that default. Pre-filing review of the completed Form 706 by a second attorney is prudent.
  4. Preserve the first-spouse Form 706 package indefinitely. The complete Form 706, all Schedules, appraisals, workpapers, executor certification, marital-deduction documentation, and the IRS acceptance letter should be stored in digital and physical copies retrievable at any point during the surviving spouse's lifetime. Estate of Sower makes this non-optional.
  5. Track the surviving spouse's marital status. Any remarriage after receiving DSUE creates §2010(c)(6) supplantation risk at the new spouse's eventual death. Diagnostic conversation at engagement of any surviving-spouse client should include "any subsequent marriage, current or contemplated?"
  6. For any subsequent marriage, evaluate pre-remarriage-death DSUE consumption via lifetime gifting. If the received DSUE is significant and the new spouse's own exemption is fully consumed (or will be consumed by planned lifetime gifts), the surviving spouse should evaluate making lifetime taxable gifts consuming the received DSUE before the new spouse dies.
  7. For grandchild-oriented estate goals, do not rely on portability alone. The GST-exemption gap requires first-spouse GST-exemption preservation via bypass-trust funding, §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election, or another §2631(a) allocation mechanism.
  8. For any surviving-spouse client whose first spouse died 2011-2029 without a filed Form 706, evaluate Rev. Proc. 2022-32 late-election relief immediately. The five-year window runs from the first-spouse's date of death. Missed windows require PLR requests at $12,600 user fees with rising denial rates.

Frequently asked questions

What is the DSUE amount under IRC §2010(c)?

The Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion (DSUE) amount is the portion of a predeceased spouse's unified gift and estate tax exemption that was not consumed by the predeceased spouse's lifetime taxable gifts and death-time taxable estate. For 2026 first-spouse deaths, DSUE is capped at the $15,000,000 basic exclusion amount. When the executor makes a portability election on a timely-filed Form 706, the DSUE becomes available to the surviving spouse for lifetime gifts (under §2505(a)) and her death-time estate (under §2010(c)(2)). Portability is what allows a married couple to reach an effective $30,000,000 combined exemption without lifetime estate-tax planning.

Is filing Form 706 mandatory to elect portability?

Yes — no Form 706, no portability. IRC §2010(c)(5)(A) requires a timely-filed Form 706 on which the DSUE amount is computed and the election is made. Timely filing IS the election under Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(1); no separate election box need be checked. For estates below the §6018(a) filing threshold, Reg. §20.2010-2(a)(7)(ii) provides a relaxed-valuation "portability-only" filing that reduces the compliance cost dramatically.

What is the deadline?

Nine months from date of death under §6075(a), extendable to fifteen months under §6081(a) via Form 4768. Missing both deadlines: Rev. Proc. 2022-32 provides five-year automatic late-election relief for estates below the §6018(a) filing threshold, with no user fee and no PLR request required.

What is the §2010(c)(6) last-surviving-spouse rule?

DSUE available to a surviving spouse is limited to the DSUE from her MOST RECENTLY deceased spouse — not the aggregate of DSUE across multiple predeceased spouses. If a surviving spouse remarries and the new spouse dies with $0 DSUE, the surviving spouse LOSES the DSUE from the earlier predeceased spouse the instant the new spouse dies. The pre-death gifting move preserves the received DSUE: apply it against lifetime taxable gifts before the new spouse dies.

What is the §2010(c)(5)(B) audit-open-forever exception?

The IRS may re-examine the predeceased spouse's Form 706 at any time — even decades later — solely to adjust the DSUE amount available to the surviving spouse. The three-year §6501(a) assessment period does not apply. Estate of Sower v. Commissioner, 149 T.C. 279 (2017), sustained a $788,165 deficiency where the IRS reduced a first-spouse's reported DSUE five years after the first spouse's death, cascading into additional estate tax on the surviving spouse's return.

Does portability apply to the GST exemption?

No. §2010(c) applies only to the basic exclusion amount for gift and estate tax purposes. GST exemption under §2631 is NOT portable — any unused GST exemption at the first-spouse's death is lost. For grandchild-oriented estate plans, preserve first-spouse GST exemption via bypass-trust funding or §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election.

How does portability interact with the OBBBA $15M permanent exemption?

OBBBA §70601 made the $15,000,000 basic exclusion amount permanent effective 1/1/2026. Any DSUE elected on a 2026-or-later Form 706 is capped at the $15,000,000 basic exclusion. The 2019 TD 9884 anti-clawback regulation under §2001(g)(2) remains in effect and protects surviving spouses against any future exemption reduction.

Can same-sex or non-traditional surviving spouses use portability?

Yes — for all federal-tax-recognized marriages under Rev. Rul. 2013-17 following United States v. Windsor. IRS Notice 2017-15 provided retroactive late-election relief for post-Windsor same-sex marriages. Common-law marriages recognized under state law also qualify. Civil unions and domestic partnerships that are not "marriage" under state law do NOT qualify.

What if the first spouse died before 2011?

No portability available. §2010(c) applies only to decedents dying after December 31, 2010. For a surviving spouse whose first husband died in 2009 (no portability), remarried in 2015, and whose second husband died in 2022 (Form 706 filed), only the 2022 DSUE is available — subject to §2010(c)(6) supplantation risk on any subsequent marriage.

How does the §2010(c)(4) DSUE computation work?

DSUE = MIN(basic exclusion amount, basic exclusion amount − (taxable estate + post-1976 adjusted taxable gifts)). Example: 2026 first spouse with $6M taxable estate and $1.5M lifetime taxable gifts has DSUE = $15M − ($6M + $1.5M) = $7.5M. The computation appears on Schedule R of Form 706 (or the equivalent DSUE-computation worksheet in the 2025 Form 706 revision). Reg. §20.2010-2(c)(1)-(4) walks the arithmetic with numeric examples.
