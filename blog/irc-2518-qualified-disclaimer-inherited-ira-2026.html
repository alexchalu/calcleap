IRC §2518 Qualified Disclaimer for QTIP-Held Inherited IRAs in 2026: The Nine-Month Deadline, the Four-Part Test, and the Failed-Disclaimer §2519 Trap

The August 21 field guide walked IRC §2519 — the constructive-gift-of-the-entire-underlying-property mechanic that springs when a surviving spouse disposes of any part of her qualifying-income-interest in QTIP property. Mistake #5 in that piece flagged the failed IRC §2518 disclaimer as the highest-probability §2519 trigger a general-practice attorney overlooks. Today's piece walks §2518 in isolation: the four-part statutory test, the nine-month deadline that starts on the decedent's date of death and cannot be extended, the no-acceptance-of-benefits rule that a single distribution can break, the unqualified-refusal requirement, the passage-without-direction rule, and the §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception that makes disclaimer into a marital-deduction QTIP trust the single most powerful post-mortem planning move on an inherited-IRA fact pattern. With state-law UDPIA overlay, six recurring failure modes, and a worked $6M inherited-IRA case study.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 22, 2026 25 min read How we research

Every practitioner advising a surviving spouse, an adult child, or a residuary-trust beneficiary in the first nine months after a decedent's death has to answer a specific federal-tax question: should the beneficiary accept the inherited interest, disclaim it in whole, or disclaim it in part? The answer determines who ultimately bears the estate tax at the decedent's death, whose lifetime exemption absorbs the transfer, whose gross estate will include the property at their own later death, and — for retirement accounts — which distribution timetable applies under the SECURE Act 10-year rule.[1] The statutory vehicle for that choice is IRC §2518. Get it right, and the disclaimed interest is treated for federal transfer-tax purposes as if the disclaimant never received it. Get it wrong, and the failed disclaimer is recharacterized as a §2511 gift or — where the disclaimant was a QTIP life beneficiary — a §2519 disposition triggering a constructive transfer of the entire underlying property.[2]

This piece walks §2518 end to end. It sits as the sibling to the August 21 IRC §2519 field guide in the retirement-inheritance sub-cluster's practitioner-toolkit arc: 2026-08-17 non-UFIPA §409 field guide, 2026-08-18 §104 power to adjust, 2026-08-19 §106 unitrust conversion, 2026-08-20 IRC §2056(b)(7) QTIP election, 2026-08-21 IRC §2519 gift-treatment mechanics, and today's IRC §2518 qualified-disclaimer mechanics. A practitioner who understands all six pieces can walk the full inherited-IRA-plus-QTIP mechanic from the deceased spouse's Form 706 election through the nine-month disclaimer window, the year-10 SECURE Act acceleration, and the surviving spouse's own death — without hitting either a §2518 disclaimer defect at the front, a §2519 gift-tax trap in the middle, or a §2056(b)(7) qualification defect at any point.[3]

The IRC §2518 statutory framework

IRC §2518(a) reads: "For purposes of this subtitle, if a person makes a qualified disclaimer with respect to any interest in property, this subtitle shall apply with respect to such interest as if the interest had never been transferred to such person."[2] That single sentence is the load-bearing rule of the whole regime. When the disclaimer is qualified — meaning it satisfies all four requirements of §2518(b) — the disclaimant is treated as if she never received the interest. No IRC §2511 gift tax on the passage to the successor. No IRC §2519 disposition. No inclusion in the disclaimant's gross estate under IRC §§2033, 2038, 2044. The property is treated for federal transfer-tax purposes as passing directly from the decedent to the successor named in the governing instrument or under state intestacy law.

IRC §2518(b) sets out the four requirements that a disclaimer must satisfy to be qualified:

  1. §2518(b)(1) — Written and unqualified. The refusal must be irrevocable and unqualified, in writing.
  2. §2518(b)(2) — Timely. The writing must be received by the transferor (or the transferor's legal representative or the holder of legal title to the property) no later than the date nine months after the later of (i) the date the transfer creating the interest was made, or (ii) the day the disclaimant reaches age 21.
  3. §2518(b)(3) — No acceptance of benefits. The disclaimant must not have accepted the interest or any of its benefits.
  4. §2518(b)(4) — Passage without direction. As a result of the refusal, the interest must pass, without any direction on the part of the disclaimant, to either (a) the decedent's spouse, or (b) a person other than the disclaimant.

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2 elaborates each requirement with substantial specificity, and Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3 walks the partial-disclaimer mechanic. The four-part test is the whole federal-tax standard; state law adds procedural machinery (typically the Uniform Disclaimer of Property Interests Act, UDPIA) but does not modify the substantive federal requirements.[4]

Why §2518 exists

Congress enacted the modern IRC §2518 in the Tax Reform Act of 1976 as part of a package that unified the federal estate and gift tax regimes. Before 1976, the federal tax treatment of a disclaimer varied dramatically by state — because state law defined whether a disclaimer was retroactive, whether it operated as a transfer by the disclaimant, and whether the disclaimant had ever taken title. Congress replaced that patchwork with a uniform federal standard in §2518, so that a beneficiary in Kansas and a beneficiary in Louisiana would face the same federal transfer-tax consequences from an identical disclaimer.[5] The four-part test was designed to be strict — the drafters intentionally imposed the nine-month deadline and the no-benefits rule as bright-line requirements that would not admit case-by-case exceptions, precisely to prevent the disclaimer mechanism from becoming a post-hoc tax-planning tool used at leisure whenever a beneficiary later regretted an acceptance.

Why §2518 matters more post-SECURE than pre-SECURE

Two structural changes since 2019 have raised the stakes on §2518 planning for inherited-IRA fact patterns. First, the SECURE Act 10-year rule under IRC §401(a)(9)(H) collapsed the payout period for non-eligible-designated-beneficiaries from a life-expectancy stretch (typically 25-40 years) down to a maximum ten years, meaning the post-death tax choice matters much more urgently than it did pre-SECURE.[6] Second, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 (Pub. L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) permanently raised the unified estate and gift tax exemption to $15 million per person for 2026 decedents and gifts (indexed thereafter),[7] which reshuffles the exemption-utilization arithmetic that determines whether a disclaimer accomplishes its planning purpose. Every post-mortem review of a decedent-IRA-plus-surviving-spouse fact pattern in 2026 should include an explicit §2518 analysis, walked against a modeled comparison of (a) accept and spousal-rollover, (b) disclaim in full into a QTIP or credit-shelter trust, and (c) partial disclaimer at some optimized fraction.

The nine-month deadline under §2518(b)(2)

The nine-month deadline is the single most-often-blown requirement of §2518. The clock starts on the decedent's date of death — the same date on the death certificate — and expires nine calendar months later. The deadline is not extendable by administrative practice, by custodian delay, by ignorance of the account, or by illness of the disclaimant. The only recognized extensions are those available under IRC §7508 (extensions for taxpayers serving in a combat zone) and IRC §7508A (extensions during a Presidentially declared disaster), neither of which typically applies to a domestic-death inherited-IRA fact pattern.[4]

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(c)(1) is specific on the counting mechanic. Nine months means nine calendar months, counted from the day after the transfer date. A decedent dying June 30 has a disclaimer deadline of March 31 of the following year (calculated as June 30 + 9 months = March 30, but adjusted per Reg. §25.2518-2(c)(1)'s counting-from-following-day mechanic to March 31 in most cases; practitioners should confirm the specific mechanic against the fact pattern's dates). A decedent dying February 15 in a non-leap year has a disclaimer deadline of November 15. The Treasury Regulations at §25.2518-2(c)(1) and (c)(2) walk the counting mechanic in detail, including the rule that if the ninth-month anniversary date does not exist in the target month (for example, February 30), the deadline moves to the last day of the target month.

When is the deadline triggered on an inherited IRA?

For an inherited IRA, the operative transfer for §2518 purposes is the account owner's death — not the custodian's later processing of a beneficiary-designation form and not the beneficiary's later notice of the account. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(c)(3) provides that the transfer creating the interest is the transfer that would be recognized for federal-tax purposes as effecting the passage of the interest. For an IRA payable to a named beneficiary at the owner's death, that transfer is the death itself. The beneficiary's later paperwork with the custodian is administrative processing, not a transfer.

The custodian-delay trap

A recurring failure mode: the surviving spouse learns of the IRA on day 60, the custodian's paperwork takes eight weeks to process, and by the time the surviving spouse is deciding whether to disclaim, only 30 days remain before the nine-month deadline. The nine-month clock has been running the whole time, and the custodian's processing delay does not extend it. The safe practice is to open a §2518 file the moment any death notice is received on any client account and to identify all beneficiaries and residuary-trust beneficiaries within the first 30 days. A disclaimer coordinator (typically the estate-planning attorney) should tickler the file at the six-month mark to escalate any pending disclaimer decision.

Post-mortem changes to the deadline

Two narrow situations extend or modify the deadline. First, a minor disclaimant has until nine months after reaching age 21 to disclaim (regardless of when the decedent died) — this is the §2518(b)(2)(B) rule. Second, a disclaimant who acquires an interest by a taxable transfer other than a bequest (for example, a beneficiary of a lifetime gift) has the nine-month clock start on the date of the taxable transfer, which is typically the date the gift was completed. Both extensions are narrow and rarely applicable in an inherited-IRA-with-adult-spouse fact pattern.

The no-acceptance-of-benefits rule under §2518(b)(3)

The no-benefits rule is the second most-often-blown requirement, and it is the one that catches sophisticated practitioners as often as unsophisticated ones. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(1) is broad: "A qualified disclaimer cannot be made with respect to an interest in property if the disclaimant has accepted the interest or any of its benefits, expressly or impliedly, prior to making the disclaimer." The regulation goes on to enumerate examples of acceptance: using the property, accepting dividends or interest or rent, directing others to act with respect to the property, and pledging the property as security. The examples are illustrative, not exhaustive — any exercise of dominion or control over the interest is acceptance.[4]

Applied to an inherited IRA

For an inherited IRA, the following actions constitute acceptance of benefits with respect to the disclaimed portion and void the disclaimer as to that portion:

  • Taking any distribution from the account, even a de minimis distribution and even one that is immediately re-contributed to another retirement account.
  • Rolling over the account into a spousal-treated-as-own IRA under IRC §408(d)(3), which is an affirmative election to treat the IRA as her own.
  • Directing the custodian's investment lineup, executing trades, or rebalancing the portfolio.
  • Changing the beneficiary designation on the account.
  • Consolidating the account into another IRA she owns.
  • Using the account as security or collateral for any loan.

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(2) provides one important safe harbor: mere receipt of legal title to the property is not acceptance if it occurs by operation of law and the disclaimant does not exercise any dominion or control over the property. A surviving spouse who is named beneficiary is not deemed to have accepted the interest merely by being named — she has accepted only if she takes some affirmative action with respect to the account. Practitioners commonly tell surviving spouses: "Freeze the account. Do nothing until we complete the disclaimer analysis. No distributions, no trades, no beneficiary changes, no rollovers. The account can sit exactly as it is until the disclaimer is executed."

The RMD trap in the year of death

If the decedent died in the second half of the year and had not yet taken his required minimum distribution (RMD) for the year, the surviving spouse (as beneficiary) is required to take the year-of-death RMD by December 31 of the year of death per IRC §401(a)(9)(B)(i). But the year-of-death RMD is by definition an acceptance of benefits with respect to any portion the surviving spouse would otherwise disclaim. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(4) Example 4 walks the mechanic: the surviving spouse may satisfy the year-of-death RMD by having the custodian pay the RMD to the successor beneficiary who would take under the disclaimer, or by disclaiming a specific dollar amount that includes the RMD, so long as the surviving spouse does not personally receive the RMD. If the surviving spouse personally receives the year-of-death RMD, she has accepted benefits and the disclaimer as to that portion fails. Coordinate the RMD with the disclaimer decision before the year-end deadline.

Partial acceptance

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(1) is clear that acceptance of one interest in property does not preclude disclaimer of a different, separable interest. A surviving spouse who accepts a $500,000 distribution from a $6 million IRA has accepted benefits with respect to that $500,000 but can still disclaim the remaining $5.5 million provided the remaining $5.5 million is a separable interest under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3. This is a common fact pattern where the surviving spouse takes an early distribution before consulting counsel — the disclaimer can still be salvaged for the untouched portion. But it requires careful documentation of the distribution amount, the date, and the specific portion of the account that was accepted.

The unqualified-refusal requirement under §2518(b)(1)

The refusal must be irrevocable and unqualified. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(b)(1) requires that the disclaimer be a "written refusal" that is "irrevocable and unqualified." The two adjectives operate together: irrevocable means the disclaimant cannot later change her mind and retract the disclaimer; unqualified means the disclaimer cannot be conditional on any future event or on any specific outcome.

Practitioners occasionally draft disclaimers that read: "I disclaim the property provided that it passes to my sister." That is a conditional disclaimer and fails the unqualified-refusal test. The correct language is: "I irrevocably and unconditionally refuse to accept the interest identified below." The successor beneficiary is determined by the governing instrument (the beneficiary designation, the will, the trust instrument) or by state intestacy law — not by the disclaimant's specification.

Partial disclaimers are permitted

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3 permits partial disclaimers, provided the disclaimed portion is one of four specific types of severable interest:

  1. A specific undivided fraction of the property (e.g., one-third).
  2. A specific pecuniary amount (e.g., exactly $2,000,000).
  3. A specific asset (e.g., 100 shares of XYZ stock, or a specific parcel of real estate).
  4. A specific pecuniary or residuary bequest under a will.

The disclaimed portion must be a discrete, separable interest — the disclaimant cannot disclaim a partial interest that is not severable under the governing instrument. For an inherited IRA, the disclaimant can disclaim exactly one-quarter of the account balance (a specific fraction), or exactly $1,500,000 (a specific pecuniary amount), or specific assets held in the account (a specific asset — though this is unusual for a brokerage IRA and more common for a self-directed IRA holding real estate or an operating business).[8]

The one-third-into-QTIP fact pattern

A common post-mortem partial disclaimer: the surviving spouse inherits a $6 million IRA outright as named beneficiary. She wants to accept the amount that will optimally use her own future exemption while leaving the balance in a QTIP trust that names their children as remainder beneficiaries. She calculates that accepting $4 million outright (rolling into her own IRA) plus disclaiming $2 million into the QTIP trust (which qualifies for a QTIP election on the decedent's Form 706) will optimally use both spouses' $15 million lifetime exemptions. She executes a partial disclaimer under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3(a)(1)(ii)(B) specifying exactly $2,000,000 as the disclaimed pecuniary amount. The disclaimed $2 million passes into the QTIP trust per the beneficiary designation's contingent-beneficiary provisions. The disclaimer is qualified provided the $2 million portion has not received any distributions, has not been rolled over, and has not been subject to any exercise of dominion by the surviving spouse.

The passage-without-direction requirement under §2518(b)(4)

The disclaimed interest must pass, without any direction by the disclaimant, either (a) to the decedent's spouse or (b) to a person other than the disclaimant. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(e) elaborates. The disclaimant may not have any power to direct where the disclaimed interest goes — no general power of appointment, no limited power of appointment, no ability to influence the successor beneficiary. If the disclaimant retains any such power over the disclaimed property, the disclaimer fails.[4]

The "person other than the disclaimant" rule

The rule that the disclaimed interest must pass to a "person other than the disclaimant" is strict for non-spouse disclaimants but has a critical exception for the surviving spouse (§2518(c)(3), discussed below). For a non-spouse disclaimant — say, an adult child who inherits an IRA and wants to disclaim in favor of her own children — the disclaimed interest must pass to someone other than the disclaimant. If the successor beneficiary named in the IRA beneficiary designation is a trust in which the disclaimant is also a beneficiary, or if the disclaimant would take under state intestacy after the disclaimer, the disclaimer may fail the "person other than the disclaimant" test.

The specific mechanic for a non-spouse disclaimant is to trace the passage of the disclaimed interest under the governing instrument's contingent-beneficiary provisions (the IRA beneficiary designation's per-stirpes or per-capita hierarchy) or under the state's intestacy statute if no contingent beneficiary is named. If the traced passage brings the property back to the disclaimant in any capacity, the disclaimer fails. Practitioners should confirm the passage before executing the disclaimer by reviewing the beneficiary designation, the will, any trust instruments, and the state intestacy statute.

The identifiable-successor requirement

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(e)(2) implicitly requires that the successor beneficiary be identifiable from the governing instrument or state law without any direction from the disclaimant. If the disclaimant retains any discretion — even discretion over the timing of the distribution to the successor, or discretion over how the successor's interest is administered — the passage-without-direction test can fail. The safe practice is to structure the disclaimer so that the successor beneficiary is fully determined by the governing instrument at the moment of the disclaimer, with no residual discretion in the disclaimant.

The §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse special rule

IRC §2518(c)(3) contains the single most powerful exception to the passage-without-direction rule, and it is the specific reason that §2518 planning is so valuable for surviving spouses inheriting IRAs. The subsection provides: "Paragraph (4) of subsection (b) shall not apply to a transfer to a spouse of the decedent." In plain English, when the disclaimant is the decedent's surviving spouse, the disclaimed interest may pass to a trust in which she is a beneficiary — without violating the passage-without-direction requirement — provided the property passes in a form that qualifies for the marital deduction under IRC §2056.[9]

This exception unlocks the classic post-mortem planning move. The surviving spouse is the named beneficiary of the decedent's IRA. Under the beneficiary designation's contingent-beneficiary provision, if the surviving spouse disclaims, the IRA passes into a QTIP marital trust in which she is the life-income beneficiary and the couple's children are the remainder beneficiaries. The surviving spouse disclaims the direct IRA interest. The IRA passes into the QTIP trust. The surviving spouse continues to receive lifetime income from the QTIP trust (which she also would have received from the outright IRA). No §2518 defect: the passage-without-direction rule is waived under §2518(c)(3) because the disclaimant is the surviving spouse and the disclaimed property passes into a QTIP-qualifying trust. The decedent's executor makes a QTIP election on Form 706 for the disclaimed property, preserving the marital deduction.[10]

Why the §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception matters

The §2518(c)(3) exception is the specific reason that a QTIP-into-disclaimer trust is the workhorse of post-mortem estate planning for surviving spouses. Without the exception, a surviving spouse disclaiming into a trust in which she retains a life-income interest would fail the §2518(b)(4) passage-without-direction test — because the disclaimed property, through the QTIP trust, is still benefiting her. The exception carves out exactly that fact pattern, letting the surviving spouse achieve two goals at once: (1) declining outright ownership (with all of its exemption-utilization, creditor-exposure, and remainderman-coordination consequences) and (2) retaining lifetime income (with all of its living-expense and quality-of-life benefits). No other statutory provision does this. The §2518(c)(3) exception is why disclaimer-into-QTIP is a first-line consideration for every surviving-spouse inherited-IRA fact pattern.

Structural requirements for the disclaimer-into-QTIP mechanic

For the disclaimer-into-QTIP mechanic to work, three structural requirements must be met:

  1. The beneficiary designation's contingent-beneficiary provision must name the QTIP trust. If the primary beneficiary is the surviving spouse and the contingent beneficiary is the couple's children directly (not a QTIP trust), a disclaimer by the surviving spouse would pass the IRA outright to the children — bypassing the QTIP structure entirely. The estate-planning attorney must ensure that the IRA beneficiary designation has, as its contingent-beneficiary provision, a properly-drafted QTIP trust with a life-income interest for the surviving spouse.
  2. The QTIP trust must be a "see-through" trust under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4. To be treated as a designated beneficiary under the SECURE Act 10-year rule (and to receive the "greater of" trust-accounting-income treatment under Rev. Rul. 2000-2), the QTIP trust must satisfy the see-through requirements: (a) valid under state law, (b) irrevocable at the account owner's death, (c) identifiable beneficiaries from trust instrument, and (d) proper documentation delivered to the plan administrator by October 31 of the year following death. A trust that fails see-through will be treated as a non-designated beneficiary, forcing accelerated distribution under the five-year rule instead of the ten-year rule.[11]
  3. The executor must make a valid QTIP election on Form 706. The QTIP election is made on Schedule M of Form 706 and preserves the marital deduction for the disclaimed property. Without the election, the disclaimed IRA is included in the decedent's estate at full value, consuming his exemption and potentially triggering estate tax if the exemption is insufficient. The QTIP election is an executor-level decision, not a spouse-level decision, and must be filed with the timely-filed Form 706 (nine months after death, or fifteen months with a six-month extension under §6081).

The failed-disclaimer §2519 trap

A failed disclaimer is not treated as a nullity. It is recharacterized under federal transfer-tax law as either a §2511 gift (if the disclaimant was an outright beneficiary) or a §2519 disposition (if the disclaimant was a life-income beneficiary of a QTIP trust). Both recharacterizations can produce seven-figure tax exposure.[12]

The §2511 gift recharacterization

If the disclaimant was an outright beneficiary (say, an adult child who inherited an IRA and purported to disclaim in favor of her own children), the failed disclaimer is a §2511 gift from the disclaimant to whoever ultimately receives the property. The gift is measured at the fair market value of the property at the date of the failed disclaimer (typically the date of the attempted execution, though the exact valuation date depends on the fact pattern). The gift is reported on the disclaimant's Form 709 for the calendar year of the failed disclaimer. It uses the disclaimant's lifetime exemption first (currently $15 million per person for 2026), and excess is taxed at 40 percent. For a $3 million inherited IRA where the disclaimant has already used $12 million of her lifetime exemption, the excess $2 million above the $3 million remaining exemption is taxed at zero (because $3M < $3M remaining), but if she had already used $14 million of her exemption, the excess $2 million above the remaining $1 million is taxed at 40 percent — a $400,000 out-of-pocket tax cost.

The §2519 disposition recharacterization

If the disclaimant was a life-income beneficiary of a QTIP trust and purported to disclaim her income interest, the failed disclaimer is a §2519 disposition of her qualifying-income-interest. Under Rev. Rul. 98-8 and IRC §2519(a), the §2519 disposition triggers a constructive transfer of the entire underlying property (net of the actuarial income-interest value). For a $5 million QTIP trust with a 72-year-old surviving spouse, the §2519 constructive transfer is approximately $3.3 million (the trust corpus less the actuarial income-interest value of about $1.7 million). That constructive transfer uses lifetime exemption first, with excess at 40 percent. For a surviving spouse who had already used most of her exemption on prior gifts, the tax cost can reach $1 million to $2 million.[13]

The failed-disclaimer recharacterization is worse than never attempting

A common post-hoc reaction to a discovered failed disclaimer: "Well, we tried; at least the disclaimer wasn't executed successfully, so nothing bad happened." That reaction is wrong. Under federal transfer-tax law, the failed disclaimer is affirmatively recharacterized as either a §2511 gift or a §2519 disposition — either of which is a taxable event. The disclaimant is materially worse off than she would have been if she had never attempted the disclaimer. This is why §2518 disclaimer execution is not a task to delegate to unfamiliar counsel or to attempt with a form. Every disclaimer with material tax exposure — say, over $500,000 of underlying property value — should be reviewed by an estate-planning attorney with substantial §2518 experience before execution. The review fee is trivial next to the potential recharacterization exposure.

The state-law UDPIA overlay

The Uniform Disclaimer of Property Interests Act (UDPIA), promulgated by the Uniform Law Commission in 1999 (updated 2010), provides the state-law procedural machinery that a disclaimant uses to execute the federal-tax disclaimer. UDPIA has been adopted in some form in roughly 30 states, including Alabama, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Georgia, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, New Mexico, North Dakota, Oregon, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, and West Virginia. States that have not adopted UDPIA typically have their own state-law disclaimer statutes that provide substantially similar procedural machinery.[14]

What UDPIA does and does not do

UDPIA does three things: (1) provides a state-law framework for a disclaimer to be recognized under state law as effective; (2) specifies the writing, filing, and delivery requirements at the state-law level; and (3) determines the state-law effect of the disclaimer on the passage of the property (typically, the disclaimant is treated as if she predeceased the decedent for state-law purposes). UDPIA does not extend the federal nine-month deadline. UDPIA does not modify the federal no-benefits rule. UDPIA does not modify the federal passage-without-direction requirement. A disclaimer that is valid under UDPIA state law but delivered on day 275 after the decedent's death is a valid state-law refusal — and a §2511 gift or §2519 disposition for federal tax purposes.

State-law delivery requirements

UDPIA typically requires the disclaimer to be delivered to the personal representative of the decedent's estate (for testamentary interests) or the trustee of the relevant trust (for trust interests). For an inherited IRA, UDPIA typically requires delivery to the IRA custodian. Some states additionally require the disclaimer to be recorded in the probate court records if it affects real estate (this is a state-by-state variation). The federal §2518 delivery requirement (delivery to the "transferor, the transferor's legal representative, or the holder of the legal title to the property") is generally satisfied by delivery to the IRA custodian, provided the custodian has legal title to the account assets under the terms of the account agreement. The prudent practice is to deliver the disclaimer to multiple recipients (custodian, executor, successor beneficiary) to eliminate any question about delivery satisfaction.[14]

Six recurring §2518 failure modes in inherited-IRA practice

The following mistakes account for the vast majority of §2518 disclaimer failures that reach practitioner attention. Each is preventable with early engagement of §2518-experienced counsel.

Mistake 1 — Missing the nine-month deadline because the beneficiary or the custodian delayed. The nine-month clock starts on the decedent's date of death and runs regardless of when the beneficiary learns of the account or when the custodian completes paperwork. Prevention: open a §2518 file on every death notice received on a client account within the first 30 days; identify all beneficiaries and residuary-trust beneficiaries; tickler the file at the six-month mark for escalation of any pending disclaimer decision.

Mistake 2 — Taking a distribution from the inherited IRA before executing the disclaimer. Any distribution is acceptance of benefits with respect to the disclaimed portion and voids the disclaimer as to that portion. Prevention: freeze the account immediately upon death notice; no distributions, no rollovers, no trades, no beneficiary changes until the disclaimer is executed. If a year-of-death RMD is required and would exceed the disclaimant's intended acceptance amount, coordinate the RMD through the successor beneficiary or by disclaiming a specific pecuniary amount that includes the RMD.

Mistake 3 — Rolling over the inherited IRA into a spousal-treated-as-own IRA before completing the disclaimer analysis. The spousal rollover under IRC §408(d)(3) is an affirmative election to treat the IRA as the surviving spouse's own account and is unambiguous acceptance of benefits. The rollover cannot be undone; once executed, the disclaimer window is closed as to any portion the surviving spouse might otherwise have disclaimed. Prevention: no rollover until the §2518 analysis is complete and the surviving spouse has made an informed decision about the exact amount to accept versus disclaim.

Mistake 4 — Executing a disclaimer that fails the passage-without-direction rule. If the successor beneficiary is a trust in which the disclaimant is also a beneficiary (in a non-spousal fact pattern) or if state intestacy would return the property to the disclaimant, the disclaimer fails §2518(b)(4). Prevention: trace the passage of the disclaimed interest under the beneficiary designation, the will, the trust instruments, and state intestacy law before executing the disclaimer. Confirm that the property flows to a person or entity other than the disclaimant (or, for a surviving spouse, to a QTIP-qualifying trust).

Mistake 5 — Drafting a conditional disclaimer instead of an unqualified refusal. Language such as "I disclaim provided the property passes to X" or "I disclaim conditioned on Y" fails the unqualified-refusal test under §2518(b)(1). Prevention: use standard §2518 disclaimer language ("I irrevocably and unconditionally refuse to accept the interest identified below") and never include any conditional or hortatory language.

Mistake 6 — Failing to make the QTIP election on the decedent's Form 706 for a disclaimer-into-QTIP fact pattern. The §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception lets the surviving spouse disclaim into a QTIP-qualifying trust without violating the passage-without-direction rule, but the marital deduction is only available if the executor makes a valid QTIP election on Form 706 Schedule M for the disclaimed property. Failure to make the QTIP election means the disclaimed property is fully included in the decedent's estate, consuming his exemption and potentially triggering estate tax. Prevention: the estate-planning attorney should coordinate with the estate's accountant to ensure the QTIP election is made on the timely-filed Form 706 for all property passing into the disclaimer-QTIP trust.

Worked case study: $6 million inherited IRA

Consider Marcus and Sophia, a first-marriage couple in their 60s with two adult children (Jordan and Priya). Marcus (age 68) dies unexpectedly in May 2026, survived by Sophia (age 65). Marcus's largest asset is a $6,000,000 traditional IRA held at Fidelity. The IRA beneficiary designation names Sophia as the primary beneficiary, with a contingent-beneficiary provision naming the "Marcus Family Testamentary QTIP Trust f/b/o Sophia" as the successor if Sophia predeceases him or disclaims. Marcus's will contains a properly-drafted QTIP trust with a life-income interest for Sophia and remainder to Jordan and Priya in equal shares. The estate is otherwise modest ($800,000 house, $400,000 taxable brokerage, $150,000 in Marcus's individual bank accounts) — well under the $15 million OBBBA exemption.

The three planning alternatives

Sophia's post-mortem planning question is: should she (A) accept the IRA outright and spousal-rollover, (B) disclaim in full into the QTIP trust, or (C) disclaim in part into the QTIP trust with the balance accepted outright?

Recommended alternative for this fact pattern

For Marcus and Sophia's fact pattern — first-marriage couple, aligned remainderman interests (both children of both spouses), modest supplemental estate, no state estate-tax exposure (they live in Texas), and Sophia in good health at age 65 — the (A) spousal-rollover alternative is recommended. The primary reasons: (1) Sophia's own life-expectancy distribution period (roughly 22 years starting at age 73 when RMDs begin under IRC §401(a)(9)(C)) is materially longer than the 10-year rule that would apply to a QTIP-trust-held IRA, allowing significantly greater tax-deferred growth; (2) the aligned remainderman interests (Jordan and Priya are Sophia's own children) eliminate the second-marriage remainderman-coordination benefit of a QTIP trust; (3) Sophia's $15 million exemption at her own later death is well above the projected estate value even after growth, so the exemption-utilization argument for a QTIP election is not material.

The (B) full-disclaimer-into-QTIP alternative would be preferred if any of the following facts changed: (i) Marcus was Sophia's second spouse and the remainderman were his children from a first marriage (creditor and coordination risk), (ii) Sophia had potential creditor exposure (professional liability, ongoing litigation, guarantor of business debts) that the QTIP trust's spendthrift protection would meaningfully address, (iii) Sophia's own estate at projected death was already at or above the $15 million exemption so accepting the IRA outright would push her over the exemption at her death, or (iv) Sophia was concerned about her own capacity to manage a large IRA and preferred trustee administration.

The (C) partial-disclaimer alternative is fact-specific — typically used when Sophia wants to accept enough to satisfy her income and liquidity needs (say, $2M outright to cover next-decade cash requirements and provide investment flexibility) while disclaiming the balance into the QTIP trust for creditor and remainderman coordination purposes.

Executing the disclaimer if (B) or (C) is chosen

Sophia's disclaimer deadline is nine months after Marcus's May 2026 death — early February 2027. Before that deadline, the estate-planning attorney must:

  1. Confirm the QTIP trust in Marcus's will satisfies see-through requirements under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4 and the SECURE Act 10-year rule requirements.
  2. Confirm the IRA custodian's paperwork accepts the disclaimer and will honor the contingent-beneficiary provision.
  3. Confirm that no distributions, rollovers, or other acceptance actions have been taken on the disclaimed portion during the nine-month window.
  4. If a year-of-death 2026 RMD is required, coordinate the RMD payment to the successor beneficiary (the QTIP trust) rather than to Sophia personally.
  5. Draft the disclaimer using standard §2518 language, specifying the exact dollar amount (for a partial disclaimer) or the percentage of the account (for a fractional partial disclaimer) or "all of my interest" (for a full disclaimer).
  6. Execute the disclaimer, deliver copies to Fidelity (as custodian), Marcus's executor, and the QTIP trustee.
  7. Coordinate with the estate's accountant to ensure the QTIP election is made on Marcus's Form 706 Schedule M for the disclaimed property.
  8. Retain the executed disclaimer and delivery-confirmation records for the statute of limitations period plus a reasonable margin.

Pre-disclaimer action checklist

The 8-item pre-§2518-disclaimer checklist for an inherited-IRA beneficiary

Complete each item before executing any §2518 disclaimer with material tax exposure. Retain the completed checklist and the estate-planning attorney's contemporaneous work notes for the statute of limitations plus a reasonable margin.

  1. Identify the exact date the nine-month clock started. This is the decedent's date of death per the death certificate. Compute the nine-month deadline and tickler it at both the six-month and eight-month marks. If any minor beneficiaries are in the fact pattern, compute the alternative nine-months-after-age-21 deadline for each of them.
  2. Freeze the inherited account. No distributions, no rollovers, no trades, no beneficiary designation changes on any portion the disclaimant may want to disclaim. The account can sit exactly as it is at the moment of the decedent's death until the disclaimer decision is made and executed. If a year-of-death RMD is required, coordinate the RMD payment through the successor beneficiary or through a specific pecuniary disclaimer that includes the RMD.
  3. Trace the passage of the disclaimed interest. Review the beneficiary designation's contingent-beneficiary provision, the decedent's will, any relevant trust instruments, and the state intestacy statute to determine exactly who receives the disclaimed property. Confirm that the passage satisfies §2518(b)(4) — either to the decedent's spouse or to a person other than the disclaimant. For a surviving-spouse disclaimant, confirm the passage is to a QTIP-qualifying trust.
  4. Model the disclaimer against alternatives. For a surviving-spouse fact pattern, compare (A) accept + spousal-rollover, (B) full disclaimer into QTIP trust, and (C) partial disclaimer at optimized fraction. For a non-spouse fact pattern, compare (A) accept outright, (B) disclaim in favor of successor beneficiaries, and (C) partial disclaimer at optimized fraction. Compute expected 10-year and 30-year tax burden, exemption utilization, and creditor exposure for each alternative.
  5. Confirm the QTIP trust satisfies see-through requirements. For a disclaimer-into-QTIP fact pattern, confirm the QTIP trust satisfies Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4 see-through requirements: valid under state law, irrevocable at the account owner's death, identifiable beneficiaries, and documentation delivery to the plan administrator by October 31 of the year following death. Coordinate with the trustee to ensure the October 31 deadline is met.
  6. Coordinate the QTIP election on Marcus's Form 706. The §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception preserves the marital deduction only if the executor makes a valid QTIP election on Form 706 Schedule M. Coordinate with the estate's accountant to ensure the QTIP election covers all property passing into the disclaimer-QTIP trust.
  7. Draft the disclaimer using standard §2518 language. Use "irrevocably and unconditionally refuse to accept" language. Specify the disclaimed property with precision — for a partial disclaimer, specify the exact fraction, exact pecuniary amount, exact asset, or exact bequest under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3. No conditional or hortatory language. Sign before a notary. Deliver copies to the custodian, the executor, the successor beneficiary, and (for real estate) the probate court.
  8. Document the beneficiary's informed consent. Prepare a written memorandum, signed by the disclaimant, acknowledging the amount of the disclaimed property, the tax consequences of the disclaimer versus the alternatives, the identification of the successor beneficiary, and the disclaimant's understanding that the disclaimer is irrevocable. The memorandum becomes the record if the disclaimant or her heirs later challenge the disclaimer decision.

Frequently asked questions about IRC §2518 and inherited IRAs

What is IRC §2518 and why does it matter for a QTIP-held inherited IRA?

IRC §2518 defines a qualified disclaimer as an irrevocable and unqualified refusal to accept an interest in property, made in writing, delivered within nine months of the transfer creating the interest, before any acceptance of benefits, and resulting in the disclaimed interest passing without direction by the disclaimant. When the four-part test is met, federal transfer-tax law treats the disclaimant as if she never received the interest — no gift-tax event under IRC §2511, no §2519 disposition, and no §2044 estate-tax inclusion in her later estate. For a surviving spouse considering whether to accept, disclaim in part, or disclaim in full her interest in a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA, §2518 is the single most important post-mortem planning lever.

What is the nine-month deadline under §2518(b)(2)?

The disclaimer must be received by the transferor (or transferor's legal representative or the holder of legal title to the property) no later than nine months after the later of (i) the date the transfer creating the interest was made or (ii) the day the disclaimant reaches age 21. For an inherited IRA, the nine-month clock starts on the decedent's date of death and is not extendable absent a Presidentially declared disaster under IRC §7508A. No facts-and-circumstances excuse (illness, ignorance of the account, custodian delay) will save a late disclaimer.

What does 'no acceptance of benefits' mean under §2518(b)(3)?

Under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(1), the disclaimant must not have accepted the interest or any of its benefits before the disclaimer, expressly or impliedly. For an inherited IRA, taking any distribution — even a de minimis distribution and even one that is immediately re-contributed — is acceptance of benefits with respect to the disclaimed portion and voids the disclaimer as to that portion. Freeze all activity on the account (no distributions, no rollovers, no beneficiary changes, no investment direction) until the disclaimer is executed. Merely being named as beneficiary is not acceptance; taking a discretionary act on the account is.

What does 'unqualified refusal' mean under §2518(b)(1)?

The refusal must be irrevocable and unqualified — a clean, unconditional statement declining to accept the interest. A conditional refusal ("I disclaim provided the property passes to X") is not a qualified disclaimer. A partial refusal is permitted under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3, but the disclaimed portion must be a discrete, severable interest (specific fraction, specific pecuniary amount, specific asset, specific bequest). The disclaimant may not attempt to direct where the disclaimed interest passes; she may only refuse it.

What is the passage-without-direction requirement under §2518(b)(4)?

The disclaimed interest must pass, without any direction by the disclaimant, either (a) to the decedent's spouse or (b) to a person other than the disclaimant. If the disclaimant retains any power to direct where the disclaimed interest goes — including a general or limited power of appointment, or an ability to influence the successor beneficiary — the disclaimer fails. For an inherited IRA fact pattern, the passage is typically satisfied by the custodian's default beneficiary hierarchy or by state intestacy.

What is the §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse special rule?

IRC §2518(c)(3) contains a critical exception for the decedent's surviving spouse: she may disclaim an interest AND still receive a benefit from the disclaimed property, provided the property passes to a trust in which she has a life income interest that qualifies for the marital deduction. In practice, this lets the surviving spouse disclaim a direct IRA interest into a QTIP trust where she remains the income beneficiary — without violating the passage-without-direction rule — and the disclaimed property still qualifies for a QTIP election on the decedent's Form 706. This is the single most powerful post-mortem planning tool for a surviving spouse who inherits an IRA outright.

What happens if the disclaimer fails?

A failed disclaimer is not treated as a nullity — it is recharacterized under federal transfer-tax law. If the disclaimant was an outright beneficiary, the failed disclaimer is a §2511 gift of the interest to whoever ultimately receives the property. If the disclaimant was a life-income beneficiary of a QTIP trust, the failed disclaimer is a §2519 disposition of her qualifying-income-interest, triggering a constructive transfer of the entire underlying property under Rev. Rul. 98-8. Either recharacterization can produce seven-figure gift-tax exposure. The disclaimant is materially worse off than she would have been if she had never attempted the disclaimer.

How does the Uniform Disclaimer of Property Interests Act interact with §2518?

UDPIA provides the state-law procedural machinery a disclaimant uses to execute the federal-tax disclaimer. UDPIA requires a written disclaimer, filing or delivery to the personal representative or trustee, and typically has no state-law deadline. But UDPIA does not extend the federal nine-month deadline, does not modify the no-benefits rule, and does not modify the passage-without-direction requirement. A disclaimer valid under UDPIA state law but delivered on day 275 after death is a valid state-law refusal — and a §2511 gift or §2519 disposition for federal tax purposes. Practitioners must satisfy both state-law UDPIA requirements and federal §2518 requirements.

Can a disclaimer be partial?

Yes. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3 permits partial disclaimers, provided the disclaimed portion is a separate interest, a specific undivided fraction of the property, a specific pecuniary amount, or a specific asset. A surviving spouse inheriting a $6 million IRA can disclaim exactly one-third ($2 million) into a QTIP trust and accept the remaining two-thirds outright — provided the partial disclaimer is executed within nine months, satisfies the no-benefits rule as to the disclaimed portion, and the disclaimed portion passes into the trust without direction by her.

How does the SECURE Act 10-year rule interact with disclaimer planning?

The SECURE Act 10-year rule under IRC §401(a)(9)(H) makes disclaimer planning more valuable for inherited-IRA fact patterns. When the surviving spouse accepts outright and spousal-rolls, she can stretch distributions over her own life expectancy. When she disclaims into a QTIP trust using §2518(c)(3), the trust is a see-through trust subject to the 10-year rule, distributions flow through the trust under Rev. Rul. 2000-2 mechanics, and the surviving spouse retains her life-income interest without the direct-ownership complications. The tradeoff is fact-specific and requires modeling before the nine-month deadline expires.
