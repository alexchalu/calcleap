IRC §2035 Three-Year Rule in 2026: The §2036/§2037/§2038/§2042 Retained-Interest Lookback, the §2035(b) Gift-Tax Gross-Up, the ILIT Deathbed Trap, and §6166/§303/§2032A Coordination

§2035 is the section that pulls the last three years of a decedent's transfer-tax planning back onto the Form 706. It does not reach every gift, and it does not reach any gift the way lay planners fear — since 1981 its scope has been narrowed to retained-interest releases under §§2036/2037/2038/2042 and to the gift-tax gross-up under §2035(b). But where it applies, it applies without mercy: a $10 million ILIT-owned death benefit disappears in the fifth year and reappears in the second if the timing is wrong. This is the 2026 walkthrough of every operative subsection, every coordination hook to §6166, §303, and §2032A, and every trap that keeps repeating in the Tax Court reporters.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 12, 2026 21 min read How we research

Every lawyer, CPA, and financial planner working an estate near the $15 million §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount permanently reset by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, §70106, July 2025) eventually meets the same question: if the decedent gifted this property last year, is it in the gross estate?[1] The answer routes through IRC §2035 — the three-year lookback rule — and the answer is almost never, but the exceptions are the ones that matter.[2]

§2035 has two independent operative parts, and confusing them is the entry point to nearly every §2035 planning mistake. §2035(a) is the retained-interest lookback: if the decedent released, within three years of death, an interest in property that would have been included in the gross estate under §2036 (retained life estate), §2037 (transfers taking effect at death), §2038 (revocable transfers), or §2042 (life insurance owned by the insured), the property is pulled back at date-of-death fair market value.[2] §2035(b) is the gift-tax gross-up: any gift tax paid by the decedent or the decedent's spouse on gifts made during the three-year period ending on the date of death is added to the gross estate.[2] The two rules move on different tracks, applying to different transactions, for different reasons.

This guide walks §2035 from the statute forward: the historical arc that reduced the section from a general three-year rule to today's narrower version, the §2035(a) hooks into each of §§2036/2037/2038/2042, the ILIT deathbed trap that dominates the case law, the §2035(b) gift-tax gross-up mechanic, the §2035(c) coordination with §303 stock redemption + §6166 installment estate tax + §2032A special-use valuation, the §2035(d) bona fide sale exception, the §2035(e) revocable-trust attribution rule, and three worked case studies. Every dollar figure is the 2026 figure under Rev. Proc. 2025-32.[3] When you're ready to size the numbers, our estate-tax calculator, life-insurance calculator, and income-tax calculator handle the arithmetic side.

The historical arc — from 1976 broad rule to 1981 narrowing

The current §2035 is the third generation of the statute. Under the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, §2035 was the "contemplation of death" rule: gifts made "in contemplation of death" were pulled into the gross estate, with a rebuttable presumption of such contemplation for any gift made within three years of death.[4] The Tax Reform Act of 1976 replaced the contemplation-of-death test with a bright-line three-year rule: every gift made within three years of death was pulled into the gross estate, regardless of the donor's state of mind.[5]

The 1976 version was widely criticized as overbroad — it swept in ordinary birthday-check gifts, tuition payments, and any transfer at all — and Congress narrowed it dramatically five years later. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (ERTA) §424 rewrote §2035 to reach only lifetime transfers that would have been included in the gross estate under §§2036, 2037, 2038, or 2042 if the transferred interest had been retained.[6] In other words, if the transfer was a "retained-power" or "retained-interest" transfer that could have been made bulletproof only by living another three years, §2035 pulls it back. Straight outright gifts — cash, stock, real estate to a child, a §2503(b) annual-exclusion payment — are outside §2035(a) entirely. This is the operative rule in 2026.

The 1981 narrowing preserved the gift-tax gross-up. Since 1976, §2035(b) has separately required inclusion of any gift tax paid on gifts made within three years of death. That rule survives because it addresses a different problem — the tax-inclusive/tax-exclusive base arbitrage — that the narrowing of §2035(a) did not touch.

§2035(a) — the three-year retained-interest lookback

The current text of §2035(a) provides that if the decedent (1) made a transfer of an interest in property, by trust or otherwise, during the three-year period ending on the date of death, or (2) relinquished a power with respect to property that would have been included in the gross estate under §§2036, 2037, 2038, or 2042 if it had been retained by the decedent, then the value of the property (or the interest therein) is included in the gross estate.[2]

The four hooked sections identify the categories of "retained interest" or "retained power" transfers §2035(a) reaches. The relevant elements from each:

The pattern is clear: §2035(a) pulls back property that would have been in the gross estate but for a last-three-years release. It does not pull back property that had a clean transfer with no retained power at any point in the decedent's ownership history.[7]

The three-word summary

Released. Retained. Insured. If the decedent released a power (§2036/§2037/§2038) or an ownership incident in a policy (§2042) within three years of death, §2035(a) pulls it back. Outright gifts of anything else — cash, real estate, appreciated stock — are outside §2035(a).

§2036 hook — retained life estate, retained income

IRC §2036(a) pulls into the gross estate the value of property the decedent transferred if the decedent retained (1) possession or enjoyment of the property, or the right to income from it, for life or for a period not ascertainable without reference to death, or (2) the right, either alone or in conjunction with another person, to designate who shall possess or enjoy the property or its income.[8]

The §2035(a) hook to §2036 is triggered when the decedent, within three years of death, relinquishes such a retained interest. A common fact pattern: a decedent transfers a home to a Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT) reserving a 10-year rent-free occupancy interest under Treas. Reg. §25.2702-5(c). If the decedent survives the retained term, §2036 does not apply and the property is out of the gross estate. If the decedent instead surrenders the retained interest early — say, moves out before the term ends and formally releases the occupancy right — §2035(a) applies to the release if the decedent dies within three years. The remainder property is pulled back into the gross estate at date-of-death fair market value under §2036 principles.

The same rule applies to Grantor Retained Annuity Trusts (GRATs) under §2702, Grantor Retained Unitrusts (GRUTs), and any other structure with a retained income or possession interest. The Estate of Powell v. Commissioner, 148 T.C. 392 (2017), addressed a §2036 attack on a family limited partnership and reinforced that a retained right to designate distributions can be enough to invoke §2036.[9] The §2035(a) three-year lookback runs from any last-three-years release of that right.

§2037 hook — transfers taking effect at death

IRC §2037 pulls into the gross estate the value of property the decedent transferred if (1) possession or enjoyment of the property can be obtained by any person only by surviving the decedent, and (2) the decedent retained a reversionary interest immediately before death with a value exceeding 5% of the value of the transferred property.[10] §2037 is narrow and technical; its use is almost entirely limited to fact patterns involving reversion-conditioned trust interests.

The §2035(a) hook to §2037 catches a decedent who releases the reversionary interest — or restructures the trust to eliminate the survival condition — within three years of death. If the release happened outside the three-year window, §2035(a) does not reach it; the trust simply escapes both §2037 and §2035. If the release happened within three years, the property is pulled back as if §2037 had been in force at death.

§2038 hook — revocable transfers and retained powers

IRC §2038 pulls into the gross estate the value of property the decedent transferred if the decedent retained, at the date of death, the power (alone or in conjunction with any other person) to alter, amend, revoke, or terminate the transfer.[11] The classic §2038 case is a revocable living trust: because the grantor can revoke at any time, the trust corpus is fully includible in the gross estate under §2038(a).

The §2035(a) hook to §2038 catches a decedent who releases the power to revoke, alter, or amend within three years of death. Two scenarios recur:

  • Revocable trust converted to irrevocable within three years. A decedent revokes the revocability provision of an existing revocable trust, converting the trust to irrevocable status. If the decedent dies within three years of that release, §2035(a) pulls the corpus back into the gross estate under §2038 principles.
  • Retained power of substitution released. An irrevocable grantor trust drafted under IRC §675(4)(C) with a retained power of substitution — the grantor's right to swap trust assets for assets of equivalent value in a non-fiduciary capacity — is arguably a §2038 power. The Treasury has not litigated the point aggressively, but conservative practice treats a release of §675(4) substitution as a §2035(a)-eligible event. Practitioners often preserve the substitution power lifelong precisely to keep §2035 out of play.[12]

§2042 hook — the ILIT deathbed trap

IRC §2042 pulls into the gross estate the value of life insurance proceeds receivable (1) by the executor of the decedent's estate, or (2) by all other beneficiaries where the decedent held "incidents of ownership" in the policy at the time of death.[13] "Incidents of ownership" is broadly defined in Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) and includes any right to change the beneficiary, cancel or surrender the policy, borrow against it, assign it, or revoke an assignment.

The §2035(a) hook to §2042 — commonly called the "ILIT deathbed trap" — is the most litigated single application of §2035. The typical fact pattern: an insured individual owns a large existing whole-life or universal-life policy in their own name, decides to move the death benefit outside the estate for §2042 planning purposes, and transfers ownership of the policy to a newly established Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT). If the insured survives three years from the date of transfer, §2035(a)(2) does not apply and the death benefit stays outside the gross estate. If the insured dies within three years, the full policy proceeds are pulled back into the gross estate at date-of-death value.[14]

The economic consequence is severe. A $5 million term policy transferred to an ILIT 30 months before death produces $5 million of gross-estate inclusion. Assuming the decedent's estate is already above the $15 million exclusion, that translates to $2 million of federal estate tax at the 40% top rate under §2001. The entire estate-planning benefit of the ILIT is destroyed by the three-year timing failure.

The standard drafting fix — original-issue ILIT ownership

The best-practice response to the §2035(a)(2) trap is to have the ILIT purchase the policy directly at inception. The ILIT is named as the applicant, owner, and beneficiary from the policy's issue date; the insured never owns the policy at any point in time. Because there is no policy owned by the insured to transfer, §2035(a)(2) can never apply. Existing-policy transfers to ILITs — often necessary when the insured has already accumulated coverage — carry the three-year timing risk that no drafting fix can eliminate; only survival past the three-year mark cures it.

Sale-to-ILIT as an alternative

Where an existing policy exists and the insured's health suggests a real risk of dying within three years, some practitioners execute a sale of the policy to the ILIT for adequate consideration rather than a gift. Because §2035(d) excepts bona fide sales for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth, a properly documented sale to the ILIT — at a price equal to the interpolated terminal reserve plus unearned premium (IRS Announcement 2005-3) or a professional valuation — takes the transfer out of §2035(a) entirely.[15]

The tradeoff is that the sale must comply with the §101(a)(2) "transfer for value" rule to preserve income-tax-free treatment of the eventual death benefit. Sale to an ILIT that is a grantor trust of the insured (or the insured's spouse) falls within the §101(a)(2)(B) transfer-to-insured exception because the insured is deemed to own the trust for income-tax purposes under Rev. Rul. 2007-13.[16] This is the standard structure for sale-to-ILIT execution: an existing insurance policy is sold at fair market value to the ILIT, which is designed as a grantor trust of the insured, and the death benefit is received tax-free under §101(a) with no §2035(a)(2) exposure.

§2035(b) — the gift-tax gross-up rule

§2035(b) is a separate operative rule that does not depend on any §§2036/2037/2038/2042 hook. It provides that the amount of the decedent's gross estate is increased by the amount of any tax paid under Chapter 12 (gift tax) by the decedent or the decedent's spouse on any gift made by the decedent or the spouse during the three-year period ending on the date of the decedent's death.[2]

The economic logic: Chapter 12 gift tax is tax-exclusive — the donor pays the tax out of assets that themselves are not part of the taxable base. If the donor gifts $10 million and pays $4 million of gift tax at the 40% rate, the tax base was $10 million and the total wealth transferred out of the donor's estate was $14 million (the $10 million gift plus the $4 million paid to Treasury). Chapter 11 estate tax is tax-inclusive — the tax is computed on the total dollar amount, including what will be used to pay the tax. If the decedent instead dies holding $14 million, the estate tax at 40% is $5.6 million and only $8.4 million passes to the beneficiaries.

The tax-exclusive/tax-inclusive gap is enormous — nearly $1.6 million on a $14 million base at the 40% rate — and Congress views the gift-tax base as the preferred one because it accelerates federal revenue. §2035(b) eliminates the gap for last-three-years deathbed gifts. If the same $10 million gift is made 18 months before death, the $4 million of gift tax paid is added to the gross estate under §2035(b), producing a total gross estate of $18 million (the $14 million net wealth transferred plus the $4 million grossed up). The estate tax computation then produces the same total federal tax burden whether the wealth was transferred by gift or by devise — the arbitrage is closed for the last three years.[17]

The math — where §2035(b) actually bites

The §2035(b) gross-up applies only to gift tax paid. It does not apply to gifts that consumed §2010(c) exemption without actually paying gift tax. A decedent who gifts $10 million in 2026 while still holding significant §2010(c) exemption consumes exemption but pays no cash gift tax; §2035(b) adds zero to the gross estate. Only after the §2010(c) exemption is fully exhausted does §2502 gift tax become payable, and only that paid tax gets grossed up.[1]

The consequence: under the OBBBA-permanent $15 million (2026) §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount, §2035(b) affects only decedents whose lifetime taxable gifts have exceeded $15 million per person ($30 million for a married couple using §2513 gift-splitting) — the top-of-market wealth cohort. For most estates, the §2035(b) gross-up is a footnote rather than a live risk.

Interaction with §2523 and §2503(b)

  • §2523(a) unlimited spousal marital deduction. A transfer between citizen spouses that is fully deducted under §2523(a) generates no taxable gift, requires no Form 709 tax payment, and produces no §2035(b) gross-up. Deathbed inter-spousal rebalancing is fully outside §2035.[18]
  • §2503(b) $19,000 annual-exclusion gifts. An annual-exclusion gift produces no taxable gift and no gift-tax payment for the excluded portion; §2035(b) does not gross up the exclusion. Systematic annual-exclusion gifting in the last three years of life is efficient and safe.
  • §2523(i)(2) $194,000 enlarged exclusion for noncitizen-spouse gifts. Same treatment as §2503(b) — no tax paid, no §2035(b) gross-up. Above the $194,000 threshold, the excess is a taxable gift consuming §2010(c) exemption; only the actually-paid gift tax above the exemption gets grossed up.[18]

§2035(c) — coordination with §303, §6166, and §2032A

§2035(c) is a coordination rule that reaches beyond the pure gross-estate computation to specific business-succession relief provisions. The section has three distinct components.

§2035(c)(1) — property included under §2035(a) counts for §303/§6166/§2032A

For purposes of the §303 stock-redemption test (redemption of closely-held stock treated as a §303 dividend equivalent), the §6166 installment-payment election (up to fourteen annual installments of estate tax attributable to a closely-held business), and the §2032A special-use valuation election (up to a $1,420,000 reduction for 2026 special-use farm/business real property under Rev. Proc. 2025-32), property pulled into the gross estate under §2035(a) is treated as includible in the gross estate — meaning it counts toward the qualification tests just like any other gross-estate asset.[19] This lets the estate leverage last-three-years transfers that got yanked back to help meet the qualification thresholds.

§2035(c)(2) — gifts within three years count for the §6166 35% test denominator

§6166 requires the value of an interest in a closely-held business to exceed 35% of the adjusted gross estate for the estate to qualify for installment payment.[20] §2035(c)(2) provides that, for purposes of the 35% test only, gifts made by the decedent within three years of death — including outright gifts of business stock that are outside §2035(a) entirely — are added back into the denominator (adjusted gross estate) of the 35% test.

The purpose is anti-manipulation: without §2035(c)(2), a wealthy decedent could gift $10 million of non-business assets in the last three years to shrink the adjusted gross estate denominator, artificially inflating the closely-held-business interest's fraction above the 35% qualifying threshold. §2035(c)(2) requires that those gifts be added back for the 35% computation only. If the decedent has $8 million of closely-held business stock and gifts $5 million of publicly traded stock in the year before death, leaving an adjusted gross estate of $10 million, the naive 35% test gives $8M ÷ $10M = 80% — easy qualification. With §2035(c)(2), the $5 million gift is added back into the denominator: $8M ÷ ($10M + $5M) = 53% — still qualifying, but on the correct base.

§2035(c)(3) — the §2032A exception for §2503(b) annual-exclusion gifts

§2035(c)(3) creates a de minimis exception for the §2032A qualification test: gifts that qualify for §2503(b) annual-exclusion treatment (up to $19,000 per donee in 2026) are excluded from the §2035(c)(2) add-back for §2032A purposes.[21] The §6166 add-back has no such exception — every gift within three years counts toward the 35% denominator regardless of §2503(b) coverage.

§2035(d) — the bona fide sale exception

§2035(d) provides a blanket exception to both §2035(a) and §2035(b) for any transfer that was a "bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth."[2] The exception operates as follows:

  • The transfer is not §2035(a)-eligible. Because a sale is not a "transfer of an interest in property" in the §2035(a) sense — the decedent received consideration equal to the fair market value of the interest transferred — §2035(a) does not apply. This is the doctrinal basis for the sale-to-ILIT technique described above.
  • The transfer is not §2035(b)-eligible. Because a sale does not generate a taxable gift under §2512(b) (which measures the gift as the excess of fair market value over consideration received), no gift tax is paid; §2035(b) has nothing to gross up.
  • The exception is fact-intensive. The transaction must be genuinely arm's-length. Sales between family members are scrutinized under the "intra-family transaction" doctrine — the Treasury regularly challenges them as disguised gifts if the consideration is materially below fair market value.[22]

The practical significance: §2035(d) is the reason sophisticated planners use installment sales to intentionally defective grantor trusts (IDGTs), sale-to-ILIT, and other for-value structures rather than pure gifts when the decedent's health suggests three-year risk. The economic result is essentially the same as a gift — the trust ends up with the property, the decedent ends up with an installment note or a policy premium check — but the §2035 exposure is eliminated because the transaction is a sale.

§2035(e) — revocable-trust attribution

§2035(e) provides that, for purposes of §2035, any transfer from a decedent's revocable trust that is treated as owned by the decedent under IRC §676 (grantor trust rules) is treated as if the transfer had been made directly by the decedent.[2] This closes the potential loophole where a decedent could use the revocable trust as a two-step laundering pipeline — trust distributes to itself as trustee, then to a third party — to escape §2035(a) or §2035(b).

The practical mechanic: if a revocable trust makes a $500,000 gift to a child in year 1 of the three-year lookback window, the transfer is attributed to the grantor as if the grantor had made the gift directly. Any gift tax paid on it (typically none if within §2503(b) exclusions, or drawing from §2010(c) exemption otherwise) is subject to §2035(b) gross-up. If the trust surrenders a retained power on trust corpus within three years of the grantor's death, §2035(a) still applies as if the grantor had personally released the power.

§2035(e) reaches only revocable-trust transfers, not irrevocable-grantor-trust transfers. A transfer from an intentionally defective grantor trust (IDGT) that the decedent does not own for §676 purposes — where the grantor's income-tax ownership arises from a different grantor-trust trigger such as §675(4) power of substitution — is not swept in by §2035(e). This is the technical foundation for the "estate freeze" doctrine that treats installment-sale-to-IDGT transactions as complete transfers of the underlying property to the trust: the IDGT is not the grantor's revocable trust for §2035(e) purposes, so a further trust distribution to a child is not attributed to the grantor.

Coordination with §2010(c) DSUE portability

§2010(c)(4) portability lets a surviving spouse inherit the deceased spouse's unused §2010(c) exemption ("DSUE" amount) if the deceased spouse's estate files Form 706 and elects portability.[23] The interaction with §2035 is subtle.

Because §2035(a) inclusions add gross-estate value at date-of-death fair market value, they increase the taxable estate and potentially consume some of the decedent's §2010(c) exemption. This reduces the DSUE amount that ports to the surviving spouse. A decedent who left a $10 million estate and had made an existing-policy transfer to an ILIT 30 months before death, dying with $8 million in the estate plus the $5 million of pulled-back life insurance proceeds, has a $13 million gross estate. The estate uses $13 million of §2010(c) exemption; DSUE ports to the surviving spouse in the amount of $15M − $13M = $2M, versus the $7M DSUE that would have ported without the §2035(a)(2) inclusion.

The §2035(b) gross-up has the same effect: gift tax paid within three years is added to the taxable estate, consuming §2010(c) exemption and reducing DSUE by the same amount. For high-net-worth couples where DSUE preservation matters, §2035 timing is not merely a first-spouse problem; it materially affects the second spouse's estate-tax posture as well.

State-level treatment

State estate taxes in the 12 states (Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington) plus the District of Columbia generally conform their gross-estate definitions to federal §2031, incorporating §2035 by reference or by parallel state statute.[24] The state exclusion amounts (ranging from Oregon's $1 million to Connecticut's $13.99 million in 2026) create fact patterns where §2035 has state-level consequence even when federal §2010(c) fully covers the estate.

The most common state-level §2035 issue: an Oregon or Massachusetts decedent with a $4 million estate transfers an existing $2 million life insurance policy to an ILIT 24 months before death. Federal §2010(c) covers the estate with room to spare. State exclusion is $1 million (Oregon) or $2 million (Massachusetts). The $2 million policy proceeds pulled back under §2035(a)(2) push the state-taxable estate substantially above the state exemption, generating state estate tax at rates up to 16%. Practitioners in the state-estate-tax states routinely execute existing-policy sales to ILITs (invoking §2035(d)) rather than gifts, precisely to avoid the state-level three-year exposure.

Connecticut is also the only state with a standalone gift tax (Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-640).[25] Connecticut has its own three-year lookback under Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-391 that generally conforms to federal §2035; a Connecticut decedent whose federal §2035 inclusions push the gross estate above the state's $13.99 million 2026 exemption pays state estate tax accordingly. Worked case studies

Case 1: The ILIT deathbed trap — existing $8M policy transfer

Robert (68) is a Massachusetts resident with a $22 million estate composed of $14 million in liquid marketable securities, a $6 million Boston brownstone, and a $2 million existing whole-life insurance policy on his own life (naming his adult daughter Jennifer as primary beneficiary). Robert's advisor recommends transferring the policy to a newly established ILIT with Jennifer as trustee and equal remainder shares to Jennifer and her siblings. The policy has a $2 million cash value, an interpolated terminal reserve of $1.95 million, and an $8 million net death benefit.

Robert transfers ownership of the policy to the ILIT on March 15, 2024, as an outright gift. Robert dies on October 20, 2026 — approximately 31 months later.

The §2035(a)(2) analysis:

  • Retained-interest hook. The policy would have been in the gross estate under §2042 had Robert retained ownership (Robert held all incidents of ownership before the transfer). §2035(a)(2) applies to the release.
  • Timing. Death within three years of transfer (31 months < 36 months). §2035(a)(2) pulls the policy back.
  • Inclusion amount. §2042 fair market value at death = $8 million net death benefit (payable to the ILIT). The entire $8 million is added to Robert's gross estate.
  • Bona fide sale exception. Not applicable — the transfer was a gift, not a sale. Robert did not receive consideration equal to the $2 million cash value or interpolated terminal reserve.

Federal consequence: Robert's gross estate is $22M + $8M − $22M (the $22M already counts the policy at its pre-transfer value in the taxable-gifts adjustment; on the correct accounting, the pull-back adds the $8M death benefit less any adjustment for previously reported gifts). Assuming the original 2024 gift was reported on Form 709 as a $2M taxable gift consuming Robert's §2010(c) exemption, and now §2035(a)(2) adds back the $8M death benefit, the net gross-estate increase is $6M ($8M policy proceeds pulled in less $2M already accounted for). Robert's total gross estate is $22M + $6M = $28M. The §2010(c) $15M exemption covers the first $15M, leaving $13M of taxable estate. Federal estate tax at 40% under §2001: approximately $5.2 million.

Compare to the counterfactual: if Robert had survived to April 2027 (past the three-year mark), §2035(a)(2) would not apply. The $8M policy proceeds would be paid to the ILIT tax-free (subject to Crummey withdrawal rights on annual premium contributions). Robert's gross estate would be $20M (excluding the policy); §2010(c) covers $15M; taxable estate is $5M; federal estate tax at 40% is $2M. The three-year timing failure cost the family approximately $3.2 million of federal estate tax.

Massachusetts adds a state-level layer: Massachusetts estate tax on $28M is roughly $2.9M under the state's graduated schedule (max 16%). The state consequence of the three-year miss is another $850,000 above what would have been owed on the $20M state gross estate. Total avoidable transfer-tax cost: approximately $4 million.

Case 2: The gift-tax gross-up on a deathbed $20M gift

Diana (79) is a Florida resident with a $50 million estate. In February 2025, Diana executes a $20 million taxable gift to her three adult children, having already exhausted her §2010(c) lifetime exemption ($15M 2025 baseline, applied fully to earlier gifts). The 2025 taxable gift generates §2502 gift tax at the 40% top rate: $20M × 40% = $8 million paid to Treasury out of Diana's cash reserves on April 15, 2026 (Form 709 filing date).

Diana dies on November 12, 2026 — approximately 21 months after the taxable gift and 7 months after the gift tax was paid.

The §2035(b) analysis:

  • Gift tax paid within three years of death. The $8 million gift tax was paid on April 15, 2026, less than three years before Diana's November 12, 2026 death. §2035(b) applies.
  • Gross-up amount. The full $8 million paid to Treasury is added to Diana's gross estate under §2035(b).

Diana's gross estate at death: $50M − $20M (the gift, no longer in the estate) − $8M (the gift tax paid, no longer in the estate) + $8M (§2035(b) gross-up) = $30M. §2010(c) exemption is fully exhausted (used pre-2025); the entire $30M is taxable estate. Federal estate tax at 40% = $12 million.

Compare to the counterfactual where Diana simply held the $20M until death (no gift, no gift tax, no §2035(b)): Diana's gross estate is $50M; §2010(c) exhausted; taxable estate $50M; federal estate tax at 40% = $20M. But Diana's estate has only $22M in liquid assets (having paid the $8M gift tax and made the $20M gift) — same as under the actual pattern. Under the counterfactual, all $50M is retained and $20M of tax is due; under the actual pattern, $30M is retained (after the completed gift) and $12M of tax is due. The gift saved $8M of federal estate tax — but §2035(b) grossed up the $8M paid to Treasury, so the net saving is zero.

The economic point: §2035(b) makes deathbed gift-tax payment tax-neutral relative to holding the property until death. Only survival past three years captures the tax-exclusive/tax-inclusive arbitrage. A donor with adequate life expectancy who makes a $20M taxable gift and survives 40 months permanently transfers $8M of gift tax to Treasury but escapes $8M of §2035(b) gross-up — pocketing the full $8M of tax savings. Beneath three years, the arbitrage is neutral.

Case 3: The §6166 35% test — pre-death gifts on business-succession qualification

Marcus (76) is the founder of a closely-held manufacturing business worth $18 million. His other assets total $12 million, for a total gross estate of $30 million if Marcus dies without further planning. Under §6166, Marcus's estate can elect to pay the estate tax attributable to the business in up to fourteen annual installments — a critical liquidity relief — only if the business interest exceeds 35% of the adjusted gross estate. Marcus's naive fraction: $18M ÷ $30M = 60% — comfortably qualifying.

Marcus's advisor suggests aggressive lifetime gifting of the non-business assets to boost the fraction. Over the 32 months before his death, Marcus systematically gifts $10 million of publicly traded stock to his children (using §2010(c) exemption). Marcus dies on August 4, 2026.

The naive fraction at death: business interest $18M ÷ non-business assets $2M + business $18M = $18M ÷ $20M = 90%. Marcus's advisor believes the §6166 election is even more clearly available than before.

The §2035(c)(2) analysis corrects the arithmetic:

  • Gifts within three years of death. The $10M in gifts made during the 32 months before death are added back into the denominator of the 35% test under §2035(c)(2), even though they are not pulled into the gross estate under §2035(a) (they were outright gifts of publicly traded stock, not retained-interest transfers).
  • §2503(b) annual-exclusion carve-out. Approximately $228,000 of the gifts ($19,000 × 3 children × 4 years) fall within the §2503(b) annual exclusion. These annual-exclusion amounts do NOT get added back for §6166 purposes — §2035(c)(3) provides that exception only for §2032A, not §6166, so on the strict reading the entire $10M is added back for §6166 test purposes. Practitioners' interpretation varies here; the conservative posture assumes full add-back.
  • Corrected fraction. $18M ÷ ($20M + $10M) = $18M ÷ $30M = 60%. Still qualifying, but on the correct base.

Marcus's estate qualifies for §6166 whether or not the last-three-years gifts are added back. But had Marcus's business interest been smaller — say, $10 million rather than $18 million — the pre-gift fraction would have been $10M ÷ $30M = 33%, disqualifying under the strict 35% test; the pre-gift-add-back "manipulated" fraction would have been $10M ÷ $20M = 50%, appearing to qualify; the §2035(c)(2)-corrected fraction reverts to $10M ÷ $30M = 33%, correctly disqualifying. §2035(c)(2) prevents the manipulation.

Six recurring mistakes in §2035 planning

  1. Transferring an existing life insurance policy to an ILIT and hoping to survive three years. The standard ILIT deathbed trap. The correct move is either to have the ILIT purchase the policy new at inception (no §2035(a)(2) exposure ever) or to execute a bona fide sale of the existing policy to the ILIT for adequate consideration under §2035(d), preserving the §101(a)(2)(B) transfer-to-insured exception through grantor-trust structure. A pure gift transfer is malpractice-adjacent for a client with any real three-year mortality concern.
  2. Releasing a retained §675(4)(C) power of substitution too early. Grantor-trust status under §675(4)(C) is often preserved by retaining the substitution power lifelong. Some practitioners release the power to eliminate grantor-trust income-tax reporting burden. If the release happens within three years of death, §2035(a) may pull the trust corpus back into the gross estate under §2038 principles. The Treasury has not aggressively litigated this position; the safer practice is to preserve the substitution power lifelong.
  3. Assuming §2035(b) grosses up all lifetime gifts, not just gift tax paid. A common misconception. §2035(b) adds only the actual dollar amount of Chapter 12 gift tax paid on gifts within three years of death — not the value of the gifts themselves, and not the exemption consumed. A decedent whose gifts remained fully within §2010(c) exemption pays no gift tax and generates no §2035(b) gross-up regardless of the dollar amount gifted in the three-year window.
  4. Applying §2035(a) to outright cash or stock gifts. Since ERTA 1981, §2035(a) reaches only §§2036/2037/2038/2042 releases. An outright gift of $500,000 in publicly traded stock to a child within three years of death is entirely outside §2035(a). It counts against §2010(c) exemption if it exceeds the §2503(b) annual exclusion, but it does not come back into the gross estate.
  5. Missing the §6166 35% test add-back. §2035(c)(2) requires that all gifts made within three years of death be added back into the adjusted gross estate denominator of the §6166 35% test — including outright gifts that are outside §2035(a). Practitioners running a manipulation calculation to boost the closely-held-business fraction above 35% by shrinking the denominator via deathbed gifts of non-business assets get the arithmetic wrong and file a §6166 election that IRS Appeals will reject.
  6. Ignoring the state-level exposure. §2035 pull-backs affect state gross-estate composition in the 12 state-estate-tax jurisdictions plus D.C. An estate that is fully covered federally by §2010(c) can still owe substantial state estate tax on §2035(a) inclusions. The state-level exposure is often the practically-binding constraint on planning; sale-to-ILIT with §2035(d) protection is worth its administrative cost precisely because of state-level pull-back risk.

Action checklist — before making any transfer that could invoke §2035

  1. Identify the transfer's §2035(a) hook. Is the transfer a release of a §2036 retained life estate, §2037 reversionary interest, §2038 retained power, or §2042 policy-ownership incident? If yes, §2035(a) is in play and three-year mortality risk drives everything.
  2. Assess three-year mortality realistically. Consult with the client's physician on life-expectancy assumptions. Underwriting scores from life-settlement viatical-market brokers can provide a defensible actuarial baseline. If actuarial mortality within three years exceeds ~10%, the §2035 exposure is not theoretical — plan around it.
  3. Prefer original-issue ILIT ownership for new life insurance. If life insurance is part of the plan, have the ILIT purchase the policy directly at inception rather than transferring an existing policy. This eliminates §2035(a)(2) exposure completely.
  4. Consider sale-to-ILIT for existing policies. Where an existing owner-insured policy must be moved, execute a bona fide sale to the ILIT at interpolated terminal reserve plus unearned premium (IRS Announcement 2005-3), rather than a gift. Structure the ILIT as a grantor trust under §675(4) to preserve §101(a)(2)(B) transfer-to-insured exception on the death benefit.
  5. Preserve grantor-trust triggers lifelong. Do not release retained powers of substitution, retained interests, or other grantor-trust triggers unless the client's actuarial life expectancy is materially greater than three years. Release-then-die-within-three-years is one of the most litigated §2035 patterns.
  6. Model §2035(b) gross-up for decedents above §2010(c) exemption. If lifetime taxable gifts already exceed $15M (2026) and the client contemplates further taxable gifts near death, compute the §2035(b) gross-up as part of the projection. The gross-up is tax-neutral relative to holding the property, but the timing and liquidity of the gift-tax payment matter for cash-flow planning.
  7. Model §2035(c)(2) add-back for §6166 qualification. If the estate expects to elect §6166 installment payment of business-attributable estate tax, run the 35% test with all last-three-years gifts added back into the adjusted gross estate denominator. Do not rely on the post-gift naive fraction.
  8. Coordinate with state-level exposure. Check the state estate-tax rules in every jurisdiction where the decedent is domiciled or holds real property. State exclusion amounts of $1 million–$4 million (Oregon, Massachusetts, Illinois, New York) create §2035 exposure on estates well below the $15M federal threshold. State-level §2035(d) sale-to-ILIT structures are often materially more valuable than the federal-only planning suggests.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2035 do in 2026?

IRC §2035 is the three-year lookback rule in Chapter 11 of the Internal Revenue Code. It has two independent operative parts. §2035(a) pulls the value of certain lifetime transfers back into the decedent's gross estate if the decedent released or transferred, within three years of death, an interest in property that would have caused inclusion under §§2036 (retained life estate), 2037 (transfers taking effect at death), 2038 (revocable transfers), or 2042 (life insurance on the decedent's life). §2035(b) adds to the gross estate the amount of any gift tax paid on gifts made by the decedent or the decedent's spouse during the three-year period ending on the date of death, closing the deathbed gift-tax-payment arbitrage.

Does §2035(a) reach outright cash gifts made within three years of death in 2026?

No. Since the 1981 Economic Recovery Tax Act narrowed the section, §2035(a) reaches only lifetime transfers that would have triggered inclusion under §2036, §2037, §2038, or §2042 if the transferred interest had been retained. An outright cash gift to a child, a §2503(b) $19,000 annual-exclusion gift, a §2503(e) direct-pay tuition or medical payment, and a §2523(a) unlimited-marital-deduction transfer to a citizen spouse are all outside §2035(a). The gift tax paid on any Chapter 12 gift within three years of death, however, is separately included under §2035(b) as the gross-up.

What is the ILIT deathbed trap and how does §2035(a)(2) create it?

The ILIT (Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust) deathbed trap is the estate-planning problem that arises when a policy owner transfers an existing life insurance policy to an ILIT within three years of death. §2035(a)(2) treats the transfer as a §2042 release: because the insured died within three years and the policy would have been in the gross estate under §2042 if it had still been owned, §2035(a) pulls the full death benefit back into the gross estate. The standard practitioner response is to have the ILIT purchase the policy directly on the insured's life at inception — the ILIT is the applicant, owner, and beneficiary from day one — so there is never a policy owned by the insured to transfer, and §2035(a)(2) cannot reach it.

How does §2035(b) — the gift-tax gross-up — actually work?

§2035(b) adds to the decedent's gross estate the amount of gift tax paid by the decedent or the decedent's spouse on any gift made by either spouse during the three-year period ending on the date of the decedent's death. The rule exists because Chapter 12 gift tax is tax-exclusive — the donor pays the tax out of assets that themselves are not part of the taxable base — while Chapter 11 estate tax is tax-inclusive. Without §2035(b), a deathbed gift program that paid gift tax at 40% would move wealth downstream more efficiently than dying with the property in the gross estate at the same 40% rate. §2035(b) neutralizes that arbitrage for the last three years of life.

Does §2035(a) reach a §2523(a) unlimited marital-deduction transfer to a citizen spouse?

No. A §2523(a) transfer between citizen spouses is not a transfer of a retained interest and does not, standing alone, produce inclusion under §§2036/2037/2038/2042 if it had been retained; it is an outright transfer. §2035(a) has no application. §2035(b) is also inoperative on the transfer itself because a §2523(a) transfer is fully deducted from the donor's taxable gifts and no gift tax is paid on it. Spouses can therefore rebalance appreciated property between themselves at any time, including on the deathbed of the sicker spouse, without triggering §2035 — subject to the separate §1014(e) one-year lookback rule that limits the basis step-up when the sicker spouse predeceases and devises the property back to the donor spouse within one year.

How does §2035 coordinate with §6166 closely-held-business installment estate-tax payments?

IRC §6166 lets the estate of a decedent whose interest in a closely-held business exceeds 35% of the adjusted gross estate pay the portion of estate tax attributable to that interest in up to fourteen annual installments. §2035(c)(1)(A) treats property pulled into the gross estate under §2035(a) as includible for purposes of the §6166 35%-test denominator. §2035(c)(2) goes further: for the §6166 35%-test only, all gifts made by the decedent within three years of death — including outright gifts of business stock that would not otherwise be pulled back under §2035(a) — are added back into the denominator of the 35% test. This prevents a decedent from deliberately gifting non-business assets in the last three years to inflate the business interest's fraction of the gross estate above 35% and qualify for installment payment when the interest would not otherwise have qualified.

What is the §2035(d) bona fide sale exception?

§2035(d) excepts from both §2035(a) and §2035(b) any transfer that was a bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth. If the decedent sold a life insurance policy to an ILIT for its full fair market value (typically the interpolated terminal reserve plus unearned premium), the transaction is a sale, not a gift, and §2035(a)(2) does not apply — even if the decedent dies within three years. The exception has to be executed correctly: the ILIT must actually pay the consideration, the price must be adequate, and the sale must be documented as an arm's-length transaction. The §101(a)(2) transfer-for-value rule is a separate concern for the income-tax character of the eventual death benefit, and the §101(a)(2)(B) transfer-to-insured exception is the standard drafting move to preserve the tax-free receipt.

Does §2035 apply to transfers out of a decedent's revocable living trust?

Yes, under §2035(e). Because a revocable trust is a grantor trust under §676 while the grantor is alive, any transfer from the revocable trust is treated for §2035 purposes as if the transfer had been made directly by the grantor. This closes the loophole that would otherwise let a decedent use a revocable trust as a two-step laundering pipeline — trust distributes to itself as trustee, then to third party — to escape §2035(a) or §2035(b). The transfer is attributed to the grantor, and the three-year lookback runs from the date the trust made the actual transfer to the ultimate donee, not from any earlier trust-to-trust step.

Does the §2503(b) $19,000 annual-exclusion gift trigger §2035(b) gross-up if paid within three years of death?

No, because no gift tax is paid on a §2503(b) annual-exclusion gift that is fully within the $19,000 (2026) per-donee exclusion. §2035(b) grosses up the amount of gift tax actually paid; it does not gross up gifts that were tax-free at the time of the transfer. This makes annual-exclusion gifting an efficient deathbed strategy: a decedent with ten grandchildren who has capacity to gift $190,000 in 2026 removes $190,000 from the gross estate without triggering §2035(a) (no retained interest) and without triggering §2035(b) (no gift tax paid). Over three years — the maximum §2035 lookback — a systematic gifting program with a spouse using §2513 gift-splitting can move $190,000 × 2 × 3 = $1,140,000 per ten donees free of any three-year lookback risk.

How does §2035 interact with the $15 million §2010(c) basic exclusion amount under the OBBBA?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 2025) §70106 permanently set the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 for estates of decedents dying and gifts made after December 31, 2025, indexed for inflation. §2035 operates independently of §2010(c). Property pulled into the gross estate under §2035(a) is added at date-of-death fair market value; the gift-tax gross-up under §2035(b) adds the actual dollar amount of gift tax paid. Once the gross estate is computed with those additions, the §2010(c) unified credit — worth $15,000,000 in 2026 — offsets the resulting tentative tax. For an estate well below the $15,000,000 threshold, §2035's technical inclusion has no cash-tax consequence. For an estate close to or above the threshold, §2035 can turn what looked like a tax-free deathbed transfer into a taxable amount at the 40% top rate under §2001.
