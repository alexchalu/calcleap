IRC §2042 Life Insurance Incidents of Ownership in 2026: The §2042(1) Proceeds-Payable-to-Executor Rule, §2042(2) Incidents Doctrine, Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) Enumerated Powers, and the ILIT Three-Year Trap

§2042 is the section that decides whether a life-insurance policy stays out of the gross estate or drops into it at full face value. Unlike §§2036–2038 — which turn on economic benefit retained during life — §2042 turns on abstract powers held at the exact moment of death. A single overlooked incident of ownership pulls the entire policy face amount into the estate at the 40% top rate. This is the 2026 walkthrough of §2042(1) proceeds-to-executor, §2042(2) incidents of ownership, the Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) enumerated powers, the 5% reversionary-interest rule, the ILIT §2035(a) three-year trap, and the Estate of Levy trustee-power doctrine that governs every irrevocable-life-insurance-trust in the country.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 14, 2026 22 min read How we research

Every estate-planner who has ever sized a term or whole-life policy on a wealthy client eventually meets the same load-bearing question: will the policy proceeds fall inside the gross estate or outside it?[1] A $10 million policy that lands inside the estate at the 40% top rate under §2001 consumes $4 million of §2010(c) exemption on top of the taxable-estate math; a $10 million policy that lands outside pays through to beneficiaries in full. The answer routes through IRC §2042 — the life-insurance section of Chapter 11 — and the answer is that §2042 catches more policies, in more configurations, than most policyholders realize.[2]

§2042 has two independent operative triggers. §2042(1) pulls proceeds into the gross estate whenever the proceeds are receivable by or for the benefit of the estate, regardless of who held policy powers before death.[3] §2042(2) pulls proceeds when the decedent possessed at death any "incident of ownership" over the policy, exercisable alone or in conjunction with any other person. The two triggers operate independently — either is sufficient by itself to cause full face-value inclusion. Unlike the retained-interest sections (§§2036–2038), §2042 does not require any actual economic benefit received during life; abstract, unused, unexercised powers held at the exact moment of death are enough.

This guide walks §2042 from the statute forward: the historical arc from the Revenue Act of 1918 through the 1954 codification, the operative mechanics of subsections (1) and (2), the Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) enumerated list of incidents, the 5% reversionary-interest threshold, the trustee-power doctrine from Estate of Levy through Rev. Rul. 84-179, ILIT design at inception, the §2035(a) three-year trap on transfers of existing policies, group term life insurance under §79 and Rev. Rul. 84-130, split-dollar arrangements under the Treas. Reg. §1.61-22 economic-benefit and loan regimes, community-property state complications after Estate of Cavenaugh, buy-sell agreement policy structuring after Connelly v. United States, and three worked case studies. Every dollar figure is a 2026 figure under Rev. The 1918 → 1942 → 1954 arc — a century of tightening

The current §2042 is the fifth generation of the statute. The Revenue Act of 1918, Pub. L. 65-254, §402(f), first imposed federal estate tax on the proceeds of life-insurance policies to the extent that proceeds in excess of $40,000 were "receivable by all other beneficiaries" — an early, narrow rule that focused on the aggregate face amount rather than on ownership structure.[5] The Revenue Act of 1926, §302(g), removed the $40,000 threshold and tied inclusion instead to premium payment: any proceeds attributable to premiums paid by the decedent were included, on a proportional basis.[6] The premium-payment test proved administratively difficult and produced litigation for the next two decades.

The Revenue Act of 1942, Pub. L. 77-753, §404, restructured the section into essentially its modern form, replacing the premium-payment test with the incidents-of-ownership test that persists today.[7] The 1942 amendment made the test at-death rather than during-life, meaning that a policy transferred years before death with no subsequent involvement escaped §2042 (subject to the §2035 three-year lookback added later). The Internal Revenue Code of 1954 codified the rule as §2042 with the two-part structure of §2042(1) (proceeds receivable by the executor) and §2042(2) (incidents of ownership) that has been retained essentially unchanged ever since.[3]

Congress has not amended the operative text of §2042 in seventy years. The doctrinal evolution has come entirely through Treasury regulations — notably Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1, which enumerates specific incidents — and through case law working out the boundaries of the incidents-of-ownership concept in specific fact patterns: the Estate of Levy trustee-power line, the community-property Cavenaugh line, the group-life-assignment Rev. Rul. 84-130 line, and the buy-sell valuation Connelly line all fill in what the statutory text leaves abstract.

§2042(1) — proceeds receivable by or for the benefit of the estate

§2042(1) provides that the value of the gross estate shall include the value of all property to the extent of the amount receivable by the executor as insurance under policies on the life of the decedent.[3] Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(b) clarifies that "receivable by the executor" means receivable by the estate itself — either directly, as when the policy names the estate as beneficiary, or indirectly, as when the proceeds are subject to a legal obligation of the estate.[8]

Three fact patterns trigger §2042(1):

Fact patternMechanismAmount included
Estate named as direct beneficiaryPolicy owner or trustee designates "my estate" or "the executor of my will" as beneficiaryFull face amount of proceeds
Proceeds subject to an estate obligationPolicy names a creditor, or proceeds are contractually pledged to satisfy estate debtsAmount required to discharge the obligation
State-law reversion when named beneficiary predeceasesAll named beneficiaries predecease insured with no contingent beneficiary; proceeds default to insured's estate under policy termsFull face amount of proceeds

The most common §2042(1) mistake is the third pattern: a policyholder names a spouse as beneficiary, does not designate contingent beneficiaries, and the spouse predeceases. When the policyholder later dies, the proceeds default to the estate under the policy's boilerplate — pulling the entire face amount into the gross estate as §2042(1) proceeds even if the policyholder held no incidents of ownership at death (unlikely, but possible in cross-owned or trust-owned policies where record ownership was transferred). Contingent beneficiary designation — one primary, at least two contingents, and a final default to a trust rather than the estate — closes this pathway entirely.

The default-to-estate trap

Most life-insurance policy applications include a boilerplate provision that proceeds "default to the estate of the insured" if no named beneficiary survives. This provision is invisible in ordinary planning but activates whenever the primary beneficiary predeceases and no contingents are named. Every policy — regardless of ownership structure — should have at least two named contingents plus a final default to a testamentary or inter vivos trust rather than the estate.

§2042(2) — incidents of ownership possessed at death

§2042(2) provides that the value of the gross estate shall include the amount receivable by all other beneficiaries as insurance under policies on the life of the decedent with respect to which the decedent possessed at his death any of the incidents of ownership, exercisable either alone or in conjunction with any other person.[3] The section reaches proceeds paid to beneficiaries other than the estate whenever the decedent held incidents at the moment of death.

Three features of §2042(2) are load-bearing:

  • "Any" is any. A single retained incident of ownership causes full face-value inclusion. There is no proportional inclusion where the decedent held one incident out of many. Rev. Rul. 84-179 explicitly rejects any fractional-inclusion approach.[9]
  • "Exercisable alone or in conjunction with any other person" is broad. A power exercisable only jointly with another person — including a beneficiary of the policy — still counts as an incident. The Supreme Court's O'Malley principle in the §2036(a)(2) context applies here by analogy.[10]
  • "Possessed at his death" is time-fixed. An incident released more than three years before death escapes §2042; an incident released within three years is pulled back under §2035(a). The three-year window is the load-bearing planning constraint.

What the regulation enumerates

Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) provides the classic list of incidents of ownership, though the regulation is explicitly non-exclusive:[11]

Case law and Revenue Rulings have expanded the enumerated list to include: the power to change dividend options on a participating whole-life policy (Estate of Lumpkin v. Commissioner, 474 F.2d 1092 (5th Cir. 1973)); the power to convert group term to individual whole-life at retirement (Rev. Rul. 84-130); the power to veto a change of ownership held by another party (contested across circuits); the power to remove and replace a trustee combined with the power to name oneself successor trustee (Rev. Rul. 79-353, though partially superseded); and the power to substitute policy assets of equivalent value (Rev. Rul. 2011-28, in the grantor-trust context, distinguishing §675(4)(C) power to substitute from §2042 incidents).[12]

What is not an incident

Not every policy-related power is an incident. Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) and case law exclude:

  • The mere power to pay premiums. Paying premiums on a policy owned by another party is not an incident. The insured spouse who pays premiums on a policy owned by an ILIT does not thereby acquire an incident, though the premium payment may constitute a taxable gift under Chapter 12 that is offset by §2503(b) annual-exclusion Crummey rights.
  • An inchoate expectancy under state law. A beneficiary's inchoate interest in a policy — the expectation of receiving proceeds without any legal right to affect them — is not an incident.
  • A trustee's power exercisable only for the benefit of third parties, without any personal interest. A truly independent trustee holding standard trustee powers over a policy owned by an ILIT does not attribute those powers to the trust's grantor or to any beneficiary. But if the insured is the trustee, Estate of Levy attributes the trustee powers to the insured personally.
  • A remainder interest with an actuarial value below the 5% threshold. §2042(2) treats a reversionary interest as an incident only where the interest's actuarial value immediately before death exceeded 5% of the value of the policy.

The 5% reversionary interest rule

§2042(2) provides that a reversionary interest in the policy or its proceeds — whether arising by the express terms of the policy or other instrument or by operation of law — is treated as an incident of ownership if the value of the reversionary interest immediately before the decedent's death exceeded 5% of the value of the policy.[3] The rule parallels §2037's 5% reversion rule for retained-remainder transfers of property other than life insurance.

A reversionary interest is any possibility that the policy or proceeds may (1) return to the decedent, (2) return to the decedent's estate, or (3) become subject to a power of disposition by the decedent. Actuarial valuation uses the §7520 rate in effect on the relevant date and mortality tables published under Rev. Rul. 2022-04 (Table 2010CM).[13]

The 5% rule matters most in trust designs where the settlor is a contingent remainder beneficiary. Consider an ILIT with the following distribution scheme: "During the insured's life, income to spouse; at insured's death, proceeds to trustee to distribute among children in trustee's discretion; if all children predecease the insured, to insured's siblings; if all siblings predecease, to insured." That final reverter — "if all siblings predecease, to insured" — is a reversionary interest. Whether it exceeds 5% actuarially depends on the number of siblings, the age difference between the insured and the siblings, and the §7520 rate.

Practical ILIT drafting eliminates any pathway of return-to-settlor entirely. The final default clause names a charitable organization or a distant family branch rather than the settlor. The Treasury has consistently applied the 5% rule against settlors who kept even remote-contingent reversionary interests in ILITs; the drafting cost of a clean charitable-default clause is negligible against the §2042(2) full-inclusion risk.

The Estate of Levy trustee-power doctrine

The most fact-specific §2042 doctrine is the treatment of trustee powers held by the insured. Estate of Levy v. Commissioner, 70 T.C. 873 (1978), addressed the fact pattern where the insured served as trustee of a trust that owned a policy on the insured's life; the trust instrument gave the trustee standard powers to change the beneficiary, borrow against policy cash value, and surrender the policy.[14] The Tax Court held that those trustee powers were incidents of ownership under §2042(2), attributable to the insured-trustee's personal gross estate at death, notwithstanding that the trustee held the powers in a fiduciary capacity for the benefit of third-party trust beneficiaries.

The reasoning: §2042(2) asks whether the decedent possessed at death any incident of ownership; the statute does not distinguish between incidents held in an individual capacity and incidents held in a fiduciary capacity. A trustee who can change beneficiaries has the power to change beneficiaries, whether or not that power is subject to fiduciary duty to trust beneficiaries. The fiduciary constraint may make the exercise wrongful, but the possession is real.

Rev. Rul. 84-179 confirmed and extended Estate of Levy.[9] The Ruling held that trustee powers over a policy on the trustee's own life are incidents of ownership regardless of whether the trustee could benefit personally from the exercise. The Ruling further distinguished trustee-power situations from cases where the decedent held a mere fiduciary role — e.g., a corporate officer who signed policy paperwork on behalf of the corporation — from cases where the decedent held actual policy control powers. The trustee-power rule reaches the actual policy powers, not the routine administrative acts.

The Estate of Levy fix

The insured should never serve as trustee of an ILIT that owns policies on the insured's life. Where family control preferences demand insured involvement, the alternatives are: (1) trust protector role with limited amendment powers not amounting to incidents; (2) investment adviser role over trust assets other than the policy; (3) trustee role over a separate trust that does not hold the policy; (4) power to remove and replace the trustee with an independent successor (a power that itself must not amount to an incident under Rev. Rul. 95-58's independent-successor requirement). Every ILIT trustee should be either an independent institutional trustee or an unrelated professional individual with no beneficial interest in the trust.

Irrevocable Life Insurance Trusts — inception design vs transfer-of-existing

The ILIT is the structural response to §2042. An independent trustee applies for and owns the policy from inception, pays premiums from trust funds (typically funded by the insured's annual gifts to the trust), and directs proceeds at the insured's death to trust beneficiaries. The insured never holds any incident of ownership at any point. §2042 has nothing to attach to; the full policy proceeds pass to beneficiaries outside the gross estate.

Two structural variants govern how the ILIT acquires the policy:

Inception-owned (the safe path)

The insured funds the ILIT with cash. The independent trustee applies for the policy in the trust's name, is named as owner and beneficiary on the application, and pays the first premium. The insured is the measuring life and executes the medical exam and application authorization, but is not the applicant, owner, or beneficiary. The trust owns the policy from the first day of coverage. §2042(2) attaches nothing because the insured never possessed any incident.

The §2035(a) three-year lookback does not apply to an inception-owned policy because there is no transfer of an existing interest. The insured's gift of cash to the ILIT to fund premiums is a Chapter 12 taxable gift — offset by §2503(b) annual-exclusion Crummey rights up to the $19,000-per-donee 2026 amount — but the underlying policy proceeds are outside §2042 from day one.[15] See our §2503(b) annual gift-tax exclusion deep-dive for the Crummey-power mechanics.

Transfer-of-existing (the trap path)

The insured owns an existing policy — typically a whole-life policy issued years earlier — and transfers it to the ILIT. §2042(2) at the transfer moment says the insured no longer possesses any incident. But §2035(a) provides that any transfer of an interest in property within three years of death that would have been included under §2042 had the interest not been transferred is pulled back into the gross estate at the full policy face amount.[16]

A policyholder who transfers a $5 million policy to an ILIT and dies 30 months later has the entire $5 million pulled back into the gross estate exactly as if the transfer had never occurred. The three-year clock runs from the date of assignment, not from any earlier date. And the §2035(a) inclusion is at full face value — not the transfer-date cash surrender value that determined the taxable gift on assignment. See our §2035 three-year rule deep-dive for the operational mechanics of the lookback across §§2036/2037/2038/2042.

The three-year rule has no bona fide sale exception in the §2035(a)/§2042 context comparable to §2036(c) for retained-interest transfers. §2035(d) preserves the bona fide sale exception across §§2035–2038, but sale of a life-insurance policy to the ILIT for its full fair market value is itself subject to the transfer-for-value rule of §101(a)(2), which can eliminate the income-tax exclusion of the death benefit under §101(a)(1) unless the sale falls within a §101(a)(2) exception.[17] The classic exception for sales to the insured or to a partnership or corporation in which the insured is a partner or shareholder is often available but requires precise structuring.

The three-year mortality trap

For any high-face-amount policy, the transfer-of-existing pathway carries meaningful §2035(a) exposure over the three-year window. Life-expectancy actuarials matter: a 55-year-old male in good health has roughly a 2% probability of dying within three years, while a 75-year-old male in average health has roughly a 15%+ probability. High-face-amount transfers of existing policies by older or health-compromised insureds should be underwritten against mortality tables — and where the exposure is material, the alternatives are (1) a new inception-owned policy in the ILIT, (2) a sale of the existing policy for fair market value under a §101(a)(2) exception, or (3) waiting out the three-year window under continued ownership.

Crummey powers and premium funding

The classic ILIT is funded by the insured's annual gifts of cash equal to the annual premium. Each gift is a Chapter 12 taxable gift. Crummey v. Commissioner, 397 F.2d 82 (9th Cir. 1968), established that beneficiaries' short-term withdrawal rights (typically 30 days) over each gift qualify the gift as a "present interest" for §2503(b) purposes, allowing the annual exclusion — $19,000 per donee in 2026 per Rev. Proc. 2025-32 — to shelter the premium gift.[18]

The mechanics: the insured contributes cash to the ILIT. The trustee sends a written notice to each beneficiary — spouse, children, sometimes grandchildren — informing them of the contribution and their withdrawal right. The withdrawal window is typically 30 days. Beneficiaries do not exercise the withdrawal (by design); at the end of the window, the withdrawal right lapses and the trustee applies the funds to the annual premium.

The lapse of a Crummey power is itself a taxable event under §2514(e) — a beneficiary who lets a withdrawal right of more than the greater of $5,000 or 5% of the trust corpus lapse is treated as making a taxable transfer of the excess.[19] The "hanging power" and "5-or-5" drafting techniques manage this risk. For premium levels above the $19,000 annual exclusion per donee, additional beneficiaries (children, grandchildren, contingent remaindermen) can each hold a proportional Crummey right, expanding the aggregate annual-exclusion shelter.

Group term life insurance and Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(3)

Employer-provided group term life insurance under IRC §79 is a common overlooked §2042 exposure. §79 permits an employer to provide up to $50,000 of group term coverage per employee income-tax-free; amounts above $50,000 produce imputed income to the employee at Table I rates published by the IRS.[20] But §79's income-tax treatment does not affect the estate-tax analysis: any group life proceeds paid at the employee's death are subject to §2042 to the extent the employee possessed incidents of ownership.

Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(3) confirms that a group life policy has the same incidents-of-ownership analysis as an individual policy.[21] The typical group life insured possesses several incidents: the ability to designate the beneficiary, the ability to terminate coverage by leaving employment, the ability to convert the group policy to individual whole-life at retirement (a right conferred by most group-life master contracts), and the ability to assign the policy to a third party. Each is an incident under §2042(2).

Rev. Rul. 84-130 confirmed the assignment approach: an employee can assign all incidents of ownership in a group life policy to a third-party owner (typically an ILIT) by execution of an irrevocable assignment. The assignment must be effective under state law — most states permit irrevocable assignment of group life proceeds — and must transfer all incidents, not just the beneficiary-designation power.[22] After effective assignment, the §2042 attribution stops at the moment of assignment, subject to the §2035(a) three-year lookback if the employee dies within three years.

An unassigned group-life policy at death produces §2042(2) inclusion of the full face amount. For an employee with $2 million of employer-provided group life who has not executed an assignment, that is a $2 million addition to the gross estate, with $800,000 of federal estate tax at the 40% top rate under §2001 if the estate is above the §2010(c) exemption threshold. The fix is a modestly-drafted ILIT that accepts the assignment of group life proceeds from every eligible employee-insured in the family plan.

Split-dollar arrangements

Split-dollar life insurance is a private arrangement in which two parties — typically an employer and an employee, or a family member and an ILIT — jointly fund a policy on one insured, with the death benefit and cash value split between them per the split-dollar agreement. Treas. Reg. §1.61-22 (finalized 2003) established two exclusive regimes for split-dollar income-tax treatment: the economic-benefit regime and the loan regime.[23]

The §2042 analysis of a split-dollar policy depends on which party holds the incidents of ownership. In an employer-owned/employee-insured economic-benefit arrangement, the employer typically holds all incidents; §2042 does not attribute those incidents to the employee-insured. But if the split-dollar agreement gives the employee-insured any incident — the power to change the beneficiary on the employee's portion of the death benefit is the most common — that incident triggers §2042(2) as to the employee's economic-benefit share of proceeds.

In a family split-dollar arrangement — commonly used to fund large-face-amount policies with the parent-insured providing premium loans to an ILIT that owns the policy — the ILIT is the record owner and holds all incidents. The insured-parent has no §2042 exposure at death provided the loan documentation is clean (the parent's rights are those of a creditor, not those of a policy owner). The loan is repaid at the insured's death from the death benefit or from other trust assets, and only the outstanding loan balance is included in the insured-parent's gross estate as an asset (the loan receivable).

Community property state complications

The nine community-property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin) create additional §2042 exposure that non-community-property planners routinely overlook. Property acquired during marriage with community funds — including a life insurance policy purchased with community funds during marriage — is presumed to be community property regardless of which spouse's name is on the title or the policy.

Estate of Cavenaugh v. Commissioner, 100 T.C. 407 (1993), addressed a Texas policy purchased with community funds during marriage, with the non-insured spouse (wife) named as owner and beneficiary. The insured husband died first. The Tax Court held that under Texas community-property law, the husband held a one-half community interest in the policy — which constituted an incident of ownership for §2042(2) purposes — regardless of the wife's record title. The 50% inclusion of the proceeds in the husband's gross estate was upheld.[24]

The community-property fix is a written transmutation agreement (called by different names in different states — "postnuptial agreement," "separate property agreement," "transmutation") executed before the policy is purchased. The agreement transmutes the funds used to pay premiums from community property to the non-insured spouse's separate property. When the policy is purchased with the transmuted-separate funds, it becomes wholly the non-insured spouse's separate property, and the insured spouse holds no community interest. State-law formalities vary — California requires strict compliance with Cal. Fam. Code §852; Texas requires signed writing with agreement to the transmutation.

Buy-sell agreement policies after Connelly v. United States

Business-purchase (buy-sell) agreements are commonly funded with life insurance on each owner's life. Two structures dominate:

Cross-purchase

Each owner personally owns a policy on the life of every other owner. Owner A holds a policy on Owner B; Owner B holds a policy on Owner A; and so on. When Owner A dies, Owner B receives the policy proceeds and uses them to purchase A's business interest from A's estate. §2042 does not attribute A's death-benefit proceeds to A's gross estate because A owned no policy on A's own life — A owned only policies on B, C, D. Those policies on B, C, D are §2033 assets of A's estate at their cash surrender values (which are included), but the death benefit proceeds paid to A's estate on A's death are zero because A's death does not trigger a payout on B/C/D policies.

The classic cross-purchase problem is the number-of-shareholders arithmetic. With n shareholders, the cross-purchase requires n×(n−1) policies. Two shareholders → 2 policies. Five shareholders → 20 policies. Ten shareholders → 90 policies. The number of policies plus the differing premiums (older shareholders need higher premiums) make cross-purchase administratively burdensome above three or four shareholders.

Redemption (entity-purchase)

The corporation itself owns a policy on each shareholder's life. When a shareholder dies, the corporation receives the proceeds and uses them to redeem the deceased shareholder's stock from the estate. §2042 does not directly apply — the corporation is not the decedent. But the corporate ownership of the death-benefit proceeds affects the §2031 valuation of the deceased shareholder's stock under Rev. Rul. 59-60.[25]

Connelly v. United States, 602 U.S. 257 (2024), the Supreme Court's landmark valuation decision on redemption-style buy-sell life insurance, dramatically altered the calculus.[26] The taxpayer argued that the corporation's obligation to redeem the deceased shareholder's stock should offset the value of the insurance proceeds received to fund the redemption — leaving corporate value roughly unchanged. The Supreme Court unanimously rejected the offset argument: under standard corporate valuation principles, life-insurance proceeds received by a corporation are corporate assets that inflate the value of every share pro rata; the corporation's contractual obligation to buy back specific shares is not a liability that reduces per-share value. The valuation increase can be substantial. In Connelly itself, a $3.5 million insurance policy on Michael Connelly's life produced an $890,000 increase in the §2031 valuation of his stock — an increase that would not have occurred under a cross-purchase structure.

The Connelly redraft

After Connelly, most practitioners with two-to-four-owner buy-sell arrangements now use cross-purchase rather than redemption. For larger ownership groups, a trusteed cross-purchase — where a single "purchase trust" owns all the policies and coordinates the transaction — reduces the number-of-policies burden while preserving the Connelly-avoidance benefit. Existing redemption arrangements should be reviewed for Connelly exposure and restructured where the exposure materially affects the deceased shareholder's estate-tax posture.

Interaction with the §2035(a) three-year lookback

§2035(a) pulls back into the gross estate the value of property transferred within three years of death that would have been included under §2042 had the transfer not occurred.[16] The interaction between §2042 and §2035(a) matters most in three fact patterns:

  • Transfer of an existing policy to an ILIT within three years of death. The classic ILIT trap. Full face-amount inclusion under §2035(a). No bona fide sale exception without §101(a)(2) transfer-for-value compliance.
  • Assignment of group term life within three years of death. Rev. Rul. 84-130 confirms that group life assignments are §2035(a)-eligible transfers. Full face-amount inclusion if the assignor-employee dies within three years of the assignment date.
  • Trust modification that releases an incident of ownership within three years of death. Where a trustee-power incident under Estate of Levy is released — e.g., the insured resigns as trustee and an independent successor is named — the release starts a new three-year clock. §2035(a) pulls back if death occurs within three years of the resignation, at full face value.

Our §2035 three-year-rule deep-dive covers the lookback mechanic across all four hooked sections (§§2036/2037/2038/2042) in operational depth, including the §2035(d) bona fide sale exception and its narrow application in the life-insurance context.

DSUE portability and the surviving-spouse effect

§2010(c)(4) portability, made permanent by OBBBA §70106, permits a surviving spouse to inherit the deceased spouse's unused §2010(c) exemption (the DSUE amount) if the first spouse's estate files Form 706 and elects portability.[1] A §2042 inclusion at the first death consumes exemption dollar-for-dollar and reduces the DSUE that ports to the surviving spouse. See our §2010(c) DSUE portability election deep-dive for the operational mechanics.

Consider a $10 million first-death estate with $2 million of unassigned group life proceeds pulled back under §2042(2). Absent the group life inclusion, the gross estate would have been $8 million and DSUE porting to the surviving spouse would have been $15M − $8M = $7M. With the §2042 inclusion, the gross estate is $10 million and DSUE porting is $15M − $10M = $5M — a $2 million reduction that mirrors the group-life inclusion dollar-for-dollar. For a family with the surviving spouse projected to have a $18 million taxable estate at second death, the $2M DSUE port reduction translates to $800,000 of additional federal estate tax at the 40% top rate under §2001.

State-level treatment

The 12 state-estate-tax jurisdictions plus the District of Columbia — Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington — generally conform their gross-estate definitions to federal §2031, incorporating §2042 by reference or by parallel state statute.[27] Our state estate-tax field guide covers state-by-state mechanics; the §2042 exposure is particularly acute in states with low state-exclusion amounts (Oregon at $1 million; Massachusetts at $2 million; Rhode Island at $1.9 million in 2026) where a §2042 inclusion that has no federal cash-tax effect (because the federal $15M exemption absorbs the taxable estate) can still generate substantial state estate tax on the policy proceeds.

A representative fact pattern: an Oregon resident dies with a $500,000 estate composed of $200,000 in liquid assets, a $300,000 residence, and $2 million of unassigned employer-provided group life. Federal gross estate: $2.5 million. Federal §2010(c) exemption fully covers the taxable estate — zero federal estate tax. Oregon estate tax on $2.5 million above the $1 million state exemption at Oregon's graduated schedule (max 16%) produces roughly $180,000 of state estate tax. A modest ILIT assignment of the group life more than three years before death would have avoided the entire state tax liability.

Worked case studies

Case 1: The unassigned group life failure

Elizabeth (58) is a Massachusetts resident, senior executive at a Boston law firm. Her estate consists of $4 million in liquid assets, a $3 million Cambridge home, and $5 million of employer-provided group life insurance (a supplemental policy on top of the basic $50,000 group coverage). She has never executed an assignment of the group life policy — her plan documents show her spouse Robert as beneficiary, no contingent.

Elizabeth dies unexpectedly at age 58. Robert survives.

The §2042 analysis:

  • Section 2042(2) trigger. Elizabeth possessed at death the classic group-life incidents of ownership: the power to change the beneficiary, the power to terminate coverage by resignation, the right to convert the group policy to individual coverage under the master contract's conversion provisions, and (under Massachusetts law) the right to assign the policy to a third party. Each is an incident under Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2)-(3).
  • Inclusion amount. Full $5 million face amount of the group life policy under §2042(2).

Federal consequence: Elizabeth's gross estate is $4M + $3M + $5M = $12M. Federal §2010(c) exemption ($15M in 2026) fully covers the taxable estate — zero federal estate tax. Robert receives the $5M proceeds as beneficiary and can port Elizabeth's DSUE (approximately $3M — the excess of the $15M exemption over the $12M gross estate). But the DSUE port is $3M smaller than it would have been had the group life been outside the estate: without the §2042 inclusion, gross estate would have been $7M and DSUE port would have been $8M.

Massachusetts consequence: Massachusetts estate tax on $12M above the $2M state exemption at the state's graduated schedule (max 16%) produces roughly $1.24M of state estate tax. Without the §2042 inclusion, Massachusetts gross estate would have been $7M and Massachusetts state tax would have been approximately $560,000 on the $5M-above-exemption base. The unassigned group life cost the family approximately $680,000 in Massachusetts state estate tax alone. And on Robert's death, the $3M DSUE port reduction produces approximately $1.2M of additional federal estate tax at the 40% top rate — a total federal-plus-state cost of roughly $1.88M attributable to the failure to assign the group life policy at any point during Elizabeth's lifetime.

Case 2: The three-year ILIT trap

Marcus (72) is a New York resident with a $28 million estate composed of $15 million in a diversified investment portfolio, an $8 million Manhattan condominium, and a $5 million whole-life policy on his own life issued twenty years ago (Marcus is the owner, insured, and premium payor; his wife Diane is the beneficiary). Marcus's advisor recommends transferring the policy to an ILIT to remove it from the gross estate.

Marcus executes the assignment on March 15, 2024. The cash surrender value of the policy on that date is $1.8 million; Marcus reports the assignment as a taxable gift of $1.8 million on Form 709 for tax year 2024, using §2010(c) exemption. Marcus dies on July 22, 2026 — 28 months after the assignment, inside the §2035(a) three-year window.

The §2042 / §2035(a) analysis:

  • §2042(2) at death (moment-of-death test). Marcus held no incidents at death — the ILIT trustee held all incidents. Direct §2042 attribution: zero.
  • §2035(a) three-year lookback. The March 15, 2024 assignment was a transfer of an interest that would have been included under §2042 had Marcus retained it. The transfer occurred within three years of Marcus's July 22, 2026 death. §2035(a) pulls back the property that would have been included under §2042 had Marcus retained the interest — the full policy proceeds. The inclusion amount is the death-benefit face amount, $5 million, not the $1.8 million cash surrender value that was used for the taxable-gift measure on the 2024 assignment.
  • Adjustment for prior gift. The $1.8 million taxable gift reported in 2024 is added back to the calculation via the adjusted-taxable-gifts mechanic in Form 706, but the §2010(c) exemption used on the gift is restored. Net effect: full $5M in gross estate, gift-consumed exemption restored, net exemption impact tracks with the underlying inclusion.

Federal consequence: Marcus's gross estate is $15M + $8M + $5M = $28M. Federal §2010(c) exemption absorbs $15M; taxable estate is $13M; federal estate tax at 40% is approximately $5.2M. If Marcus had lived past March 15, 2027 (three years after assignment), the $5M policy would have escaped §2042 entirely, exemption use would have been limited to the $1.8M gift consumption, and net federal estate tax would have been approximately $3.2M — a savings of $2M relative to the actual outcome. The three-year mortality risk destroyed most of the planned savings.

The counterfactual with better structure: had Marcus's advisor set up the ILIT with an inception-owned new policy (rather than transferring the existing policy), the §2035(a) three-year lookback would not have applied — there is no transfer of an interest when the trust applies for a new policy at inception. Marcus could have surrendered the old policy for its $1.8M cash surrender value, contributed the surrendered proceeds to the ILIT under the annual-exclusion Crummey-power arrangement, and had the trustee apply for a new $5M policy on Marcus's life. Under the new-policy structure, the $5M face amount would have been outside the estate on day one, and Marcus's three-year mortality risk would have been eliminated.

Case 3: The Estate of Levy trustee-power inclusion

David (66) is a Texas resident with an $18 million estate. Twelve years ago, David established an ILIT to hold a $6 million second-to-die survivorship policy insuring David and his wife Sarah. The trust instrument names David as trustee — a common but doctrinally dangerous choice. The trust instrument gives the trustee standard powers, including the powers to change beneficiaries, to surrender the policy for cash surrender value, and to obtain policy loans against cash value.

David dies before Sarah. Sarah survives; the second-to-die policy pays no death benefit at David's death but has a $2.1 million cash surrender value.

The §2042 analysis:

  • David's trustee powers. As trustee, David held the powers to change beneficiaries, surrender the policy for its cash surrender value, and take policy loans. Under Estate of Levy v. Commissioner, 70 T.C. 873 (1978), and Rev. Rul. 84-179, those trustee powers are incidents of ownership attributable to David personally, notwithstanding that David held them in fiduciary capacity.[14]
  • Second-to-die complication. The policy does not pay a death benefit at David's death because Sarah is still alive. But §2042(2) attributes the policy — not the death benefit — to the insured with incidents. The Treasury measures the includible amount as the policy's fair market value at the insured's date of death.[28] For a second-to-die policy, that is the interpolated terminal reserve plus unearned premium (per Rev. Rul. 76-490), which for this policy at David's date of death is approximately $2.3 million — slightly higher than cash surrender value because the interpolated terminal reserve reflects the actuarial value of the future death benefit to the survivor.

Federal consequence: David's gross estate is $18M (non-policy assets) + $2.3M (policy value under §2042(2) via Estate of Levy) = $20.3M. Federal §2010(c) exemption absorbs $15M; taxable estate is $5.3M; federal estate tax at 40% is approximately $2.12M. If David had named an independent trustee from inception — his brother, an institutional trustee, or an unrelated professional trustee — the §2042(2) attribution would have been zero and David's federal estate tax would have been zero (the $18M gross estate would have exceeded the $15M exemption by $3M, producing $1.2M of federal tax rather than $2.12M). The trustee-selection mistake cost $920,000 of federal estate tax on David's death, plus the additional impact on Sarah's DSUE port at second death.

Six recurring mistakes in §2042 planning

  1. Retaining ownership of a policy "for beneficiary flexibility." The most common §2042(2) trap. Every retained power — beneficiary designation, cash-value borrowing, surrender, assignment, dividend election, settlement option — is an incident of ownership. A single retained incident produces full face-value inclusion at the 40% top rate. If flexibility matters, house the policy in an ILIT with a trust protector who holds limited amendment powers not amounting to incidents; the insured never retains ownership directly.
  2. Transferring an existing policy to an ILIT and dying within three years. The §2035(a) three-year lookback undoes the transfer entirely — the full face-amount policy is pulled back into the gross estate as if the transfer never occurred. The structurally safe path for large policies is an inception-owned new policy applied for by the ILIT trustee at issuance; the insured is the measuring life but never the applicant, owner, or beneficiary. Where the client has an existing policy of substantial value, surrender or sale of the old policy plus a new-policy application in the ILIT is often preferable to a direct assignment.
  3. Naming the insured as trustee of an ILIT. Estate of Levy v. Commissioner, 70 T.C. 873 (1978), and Rev. Rul. 84-179 attribute the trustee's substantive powers over the policy — beneficiary change, surrender, borrowing — to the insured-trustee personally as incidents of ownership. The trustee should be an independent institutional trustee or an unrelated professional individual with no beneficial interest in the trust. Where family control preferences demand insured involvement, the insured can serve as trust protector with limited amendment powers, as investment adviser over non-policy trust assets, or with a power to remove and replace the trustee subject to the independent-successor requirement of Rev. Rul. 95-58.
  4. Failing to assign group term life insurance. An unassigned $2M-$5M employer-provided group life policy is a common overlooked §2042(2) exposure. The fix is a modestly-drafted ILIT that accepts an irrevocable assignment of all incidents of ownership per Rev. Rul. 84-130. The assignment must be filed with the group life plan administrator and must transfer all incidents (not just the beneficiary-designation power). Once effective, §2042 stops attributing at the moment of assignment, subject to the §2035(a) three-year window.
  5. Community property state ownership without a transmutation agreement. In the nine community-property states, a policy purchased with community funds is presumed to be community property regardless of record title, producing 50% inclusion in the insured spouse's gross estate under Estate of Cavenaugh. The fix is a written transmutation agreement executed before policy purchase, complying with state-specific formalities (e.g., Cal. Fam. Code §852 for California), transmuting the premium-funding source to the non-insured spouse's separate property. The agreement should predate the policy application, not follow it.
  6. Redemption-style buy-sell after Connelly. The Supreme Court's 2024 unanimous holding in Connelly v. United States rejected the redemption-liability offset argument, producing material §2031 valuation increases for shareholders of closely-held corporations funded by redemption-style buy-sell life insurance. For most two-to-four-owner arrangements, cross-purchase is preferable; for larger ownership groups, a trusteed cross-purchase reduces the number-of-policies burden while preserving the Connelly avoidance. Existing redemption arrangements should be reviewed and restructured where the valuation exposure is material.

Action checklist — before executing any life-insurance-based estate plan

  1. Inventory every policy on the client's life. Individual whole life, universal life, term, group term, split-dollar, executive bonus, buy-sell — a full policy roster with issue date, face amount, current cash surrender value, owner, insured, beneficiary, and premium payor. Any policy that shows the insured as owner or as holder of any incident triggers §2042(2) exposure.
  2. For every insured-owned policy, assess the assignment vs new-inception decision. For older insureds or those with material health concerns, mortality tables and life-expectancy actuarials matter: a 55-year-old male has roughly 2% three-year mortality; a 75-year-old male has roughly 15%+ three-year mortality. Where the three-year §2035(a) exposure is material, prefer new-inception-owned policies in an ILIT over assignment of existing policies.
  3. Assign every group term life policy above §79's $50,000 income-tax-free threshold. An irrevocable assignment of all incidents to an ILIT — complying with the group-plan administrator's assignment procedures and with Rev. Rul. 84-130 — removes the group-life proceeds from §2042 at the moment of assignment. The three-year §2035(a) window still applies but starts from the assignment date, so early planning is essential.
  4. Name an independent trustee for every ILIT. Never name the insured as trustee. Institutional trustees are the safest choice; unrelated professional individuals are acceptable. Family members can serve as trustee only if they hold no incidents of ownership on any policy on their own life (i.e., cannot be trustee of an ILIT insuring their own life) and are otherwise independent of the settlor's control.
  5. Eliminate every reversionary interest in the ILIT distribution scheme. The final default-taker under the ILIT — after primary, contingent, and remote-contingent beneficiaries have all failed — should be a charitable organization or a distant family branch, never the settlor. The 5% reversionary interest rule under §2042(2) can pull the entire policy back into the gross estate if the actuarial value of a remote reverter exceeds the threshold.
  6. In community-property states, execute a transmutation agreement before policy purchase. The agreement must comply with state-specific formalities and must clearly transmute the premium-funding source to the non-insured spouse's separate property. Where the policy was purchased before the transmutation was in place, remedial transmutation and gift restructuring may be possible but is fact-specific and complex.
  7. Structure buy-sell arrangements as cross-purchase where possible. Post-Connelly, redemption-style arrangements produce material §2031 valuation increases for the deceased shareholder's stock. Cross-purchase avoids that outcome. For larger ownership groups where the n×(n−1) policy count is administratively burdensome, a trusteed cross-purchase preserves the Connelly avoidance while reducing the number of policies.
  8. Coordinate with §2010(c)(4) DSUE portability. A §2042 inclusion at the first spouse's death consumes exemption and reduces the DSUE that ports to the surviving spouse. Model the second-death consequence explicitly: a $2M §2042 inclusion at first death that has no first-death federal tax effect (because the exemption still absorbs the estate) can nevertheless cost $800,000 of federal tax at second death via reduced DSUE. State estate-tax modeling matters especially in Oregon, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Minnesota, where state exemptions are materially below the federal $15M threshold.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2042 do in 2026?

IRC §2042 pulls the full proceeds of a life-insurance policy into the decedent's gross estate under either of two independent triggers. §2042(1) applies whenever the proceeds are receivable by or for the benefit of the estate — even if no one alive at the moment of death held any policy powers. §2042(2) applies whenever the decedent possessed at death any incident of ownership over the policy, exercisable alone or in conjunction with any other person. Either trigger produces full inclusion of the face amount (plus any post-death interest accrued before payment). The section makes life-insurance-planning uniquely difficult because it does not turn on economic benefit received during life the way §§2036–2038 do; it turns on abstract powers held at the moment of death, some of which the average policyholder does not realize exist.

What is an "incident of ownership" under Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2)?

Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(2) enumerates the classic incidents: the power to change the beneficiary, the power to surrender or cancel the policy, the power to assign the policy or revoke a prior assignment, the power to pledge the policy for a loan, the power to obtain a policy loan from the insurer, and the power to determine the timing or manner of benefit payments (elect settlement options). The regulation is not exclusive — courts have added the power to change dividend options, the power to convert group term to whole life, and (in some circuits) the power to veto a change of ownership. The consistent principle is that any power to affect the economic benefit of the policy — regardless of whether the decedent could obtain the benefit personally — is an incident of ownership. A single retained incident causes full §2042(2) inclusion; there is no proportional or fractional inclusion for one incident out of many.

What is the ILIT three-year trap under §2035(a) and §2042?

An Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT) is the standard structural response to §2042: an independent trustee applies for and owns the policy from inception, so the insured never holds any incident of ownership. The three-year trap arises when the insured owns an existing policy and transfers it into the ILIT. §2042(2) at the transfer moment says the insured no longer has any incident of ownership. But §2035(a) provides that any transfer of an interest in property within three years of death that would have been included under §2042 had it not been released is pulled back into the gross estate at the full policy face amount. So a policyholder who transfers a $5 million policy to an ILIT and dies 30 months later has the entire $5 million pulled back into the gross estate exactly as if the transfer had never occurred. The only structurally safe path for high-face-amount policies is to have the trustee apply for the policy at inception, so no transfer ever happens and §2035(a) has nothing to hook onto.

Does a policy owned by the insured's spouse escape §2042?

Yes, if the spouse is the applicant, owner, premium payor, and beneficiary designator from inception and the insured holds no incident of ownership at any point. Cross-ownership — spouse A applies for and owns a policy on spouse B's life — sits entirely outside §2042 because the insured (spouse B) never held any incident. The complication is community property: in the nine community-property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), a policy purchased with community funds is presumed to be community property regardless of which spouse's name is on the policy. Under Estate of Cavenaugh v. Commissioner, 100 T.C. 407 (1993), and later cases, the insured spouse is treated as holding a community half-interest that constitutes an incident of ownership for §2042(2) purposes. Rev. Rul. 2011-28 and Estate of Cavenaugh's holding produce a 50% inclusion of the proceeds even where record-title is in the non-insured spouse alone. The community-property fix is a documented separate-property agreement transmuting the policy or its funding source to the non-insured spouse's separate estate before the policy issues.

What is the 5% reversionary interest rule under §2042(2)?

§2042(2) treats as an incident of ownership any reversionary interest the decedent had in the policy or its proceeds, whether arising by the express terms of the policy or trust or by operation of law, if the value of the reversionary interest immediately before death exceeded 5% of the value of the policy. A reversionary interest is any possibility that the policy or proceeds may return to the decedent or the decedent's estate, or be subject to a power of disposition by the decedent. The 5% test is measured actuarially against the §7520 rate in effect on the date of the transfer or on death (depending on context) using standard mortality tables. The rule matters most in trust designs where the settlor is a contingent remainder beneficiary of the ILIT — e.g., "proceeds to spouse for life, remainder to children, but if all children predecease the insured then back to the insured." The remote-contingent-remainder pathway can nevertheless exceed 5% actuarially in specific fact patterns, so ILIT drafters typically eliminate any pathway of return-to-settlor entirely.

Can a trustee of an ILIT be treated as holding incidents of ownership under Estate of Levy?

Yes, if the insured serves as trustee and the trust instrument gives the trustee substantive powers over the policy. Estate of Levy v. Commissioner, 70 T.C. 873 (1978), held that trustee powers to change beneficiary designations, borrow against policy cash value, surrender the policy, or assign it are incidents of ownership under §2042(2) even where the trustee holds those powers in a fiduciary capacity for the benefit of third parties. Rev. Rul. 84-179 clarified that a trustee's fiduciary duty running to trust beneficiaries does not defeat §2042 attribution — the incidents are attributed to the decedent-trustee's personal estate. The practical rule: the insured should never serve as trustee of an ILIT that owns policies on the insured's life. Where family or control preferences demand insured involvement, the insured can serve as an investment adviser, a trust protector with limited powers not amounting to incidents, or as trustee of a separate trust that does not hold the policy.

How does §2042 apply to group term life insurance provided by an employer?

Group term life insurance under IRC §79 provided by an employer is fully within §2042 at the employee-insured's death unless the employee has assigned all incidents of ownership to a third party or trust. Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(3) confirms that the ability to designate the beneficiary, the ability to terminate coverage by leaving employment, the ability to convert the group policy to individual whole life at retirement, and the ability to assign the policy are all incidents of ownership for §2042(2) purposes. An employee with $2 million of employer-provided group life who has not executed an assignment adds $2 million to the gross estate at death. Rev. Rul. 84-130 confirmed the assignment approach: an irrevocable assignment of all incidents of ownership to a third-party owner (typically an ILIT) removes the group life proceeds from §2042 at the moment of assignment, subject to the §2035(a) three-year lookback if the assignor dies within three years.

Do buy-sell agreement policies trigger §2042 for the insured shareholder?

It depends on the buy-sell structure. In a cross-purchase arrangement, each shareholder owns a policy on the life of every other shareholder — the insured never owns a policy on their own life, so §2042 has nothing to attach to. In a redemption (entity-purchase) arrangement, the corporation owns policies on each shareholder's life; §2042 does not directly apply because the corporation is not the decedent, but the value of the shareholder's corporate stock is inflated by the insurance proceeds received at death, which can affect §2031 stock valuation under Rev. Rul. 59-60 and Estate of Blount v. Commissioner, 428 F.3d 1338 (11th Cir. 2005). The Supreme Court went further in Connelly v. United States, 602 U.S. 257 (2024), holding that life insurance proceeds received by a corporation for a shareholder-redemption obligation are not offset by the redemption liability in valuing the decedent's shares — a significant valuation increase. Practitioners now overwhelmingly prefer cross-purchase over redemption for buy-sell life-insurance funding, subject to the number-of-shareholders limitation.

How does §2042 interact with the $15 million §2010(c) basic exclusion in 2026?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, §70106, July 2025) permanently set the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 per person for decedents dying and gifts made after December 31, 2025, indexed for inflation. §2042 operates independently of §2010(c); it determines what enters the gross estate. Life-insurance proceeds pulled into the gross estate under §2042 are included at full face amount plus any post-death interest — never at any discount. For estates well below the $15M exemption, §2042 inclusions may add nothing to net federal estate tax if the exemption still absorbs the total. For estates near or above the threshold, §2042 inclusion of a large policy — a $10 million buy-sell policy, a $5 million key-person policy, or a $3 million individual policy on a wealthy insured — can transform a mostly-covered estate into one materially exposed to the 40% top rate under §2001. §2042 also drives the §2010(c)(4) DSUE portability calculus: proceeds pulled back at first death consume exemption and reduce the DSUE that ports to the surviving spouse.

What are the six most common §2042 planning mistakes in 2026?

The six recurring mistakes are: (1) Retaining ownership of a policy "for beneficiary flexibility" — every retained power is an incident. (2) Transferring an existing policy to an ILIT and dying within three years — §2035(a) undoes the transfer entirely; the fix is inception-owned policies. (3) Naming the insured as trustee of an ILIT — Estate of Levy imputes trustee powers as personal §2042 incidents. (4) Failing to assign group term life insurance — an unassigned $2M employer policy adds $2M to the gross estate. (5) Community property state ownership without a transmutation agreement — Cavenaugh produces 50% inclusion despite record title in the non-insured spouse. (6) Redemption-style buy-sell after Connelly v. United States — the corporation-owned policy inflates §2031 stock value without offset for the redemption liability. Each mistake is preventable with structural planning that keeps the insured entirely disentangled from ownership, powers, and community-property funding sources.

Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §101(a)(2) — Transfer-for-value rule: life-insurance proceeds paid at death are generally excluded from income under §101(a)(1), but a policy transferred for valuable consideration is subject to the transfer-for-value rule that eliminates the exclusion, subject to enumerated exceptions (transfers to the insured, to a partner of the insured, or to a partnership or corporation in which the insured is a partner or shareholder). law.cornell.edu (26 USC §101)
  18. Crummey v. Commissioner, 397 F.2d 82 (9th Cir. 1968) — Ninth Circuit held that beneficiaries' short-term withdrawal rights over trust contributions qualify the contributions as present interests for §2503(b) annual-exclusion purposes; the foundational authority for ILIT premium-funding under withdrawal-right notices. scholar.google.com (Crummey)
  19. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §2514(e) — Lapse-of-power rule: the lapse of a general power of appointment (including a Crummey withdrawal right) is treated as a release, but only to the extent the property subject to the power exceeded the greater of $5,000 or 5% of the aggregate value of the property out of which the power could have been satisfied; the "5-or-5" drafting anchor for Crummey-power ILITs. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §2514)
  20. Internal Revenue Code, 26 U.S.C. §79 — Group term life insurance: employer-provided coverage up to $50,000 is income-tax-free; amounts above $50,000 produce imputed income to the employee at Table I rates. §79's income-tax treatment does not affect §2042 estate-tax analysis. law.cornell.edu (26 USC §79)
  21. Treas. Reg. §20.2042-1(c)(3) — Confirms that group life policies have the same incidents-of-ownership analysis as individual policies; addresses the "master contract" convertibility power under §79 as an incident of ownership. ecfr.gov (26 CFR §20.2042-1(c)(3))
  22. Rev. Rul. 84-130, 1984-2 C.B. 194 — Confirmed that an employee can assign all incidents of ownership in a group term life policy to a third-party owner (typically an ILIT), removing the group life proceeds from §2042 at the moment of assignment subject to the §2035(a) three-year lookback. irs.gov (Rev. Rul. 84-130)
  23. Treas. Reg. §1.61-22 (finalized 2003) — Established the two exclusive regimes for split-dollar life insurance: the economic-benefit regime (Treas. Reg. §1.61-22(d)) for endorsement arrangements and the loan regime (Treas. Reg. §1.7872-15) for collateral-assignment arrangements. The §2042 attribution turns on which party holds the incidents under the split-dollar agreement. ecfr.gov (26 CFR §1.61-22)
  24. Estate of Cavenaugh v. Commissioner, 100 T.C. 407 (1993) — Tax Court held that under Texas community-property law, an insured spouse holds a one-half community interest in a policy purchased with community funds during marriage even where the non-insured spouse is named as record owner; the community-half constitutes an incident of ownership under §2042(2) producing 50% inclusion. scholar.google.com (Cavenaugh)
  25. Rev. Rul. 59-60, 1959-1 C.B. 237 — The foundational IRS ruling on valuation of closely-held stock for federal estate-tax purposes; enumerates eight factors including earning capacity, book value, dividend-paying capacity, goodwill, sales of the stock, and comparable-company prices. Applied by Estate of Blount and Connelly to redemption-style buy-sell life-insurance situations. irs.gov (Rev. Rul. 59-60)
  26. Connelly v. United States, 602 U.S. 257 (2024) — Supreme Court unanimous decision (Justice Thomas) rejecting the redemption-liability offset argument in valuing shares of a closely-held corporation that received life-insurance proceeds to fund a shareholder-redemption obligation; the operative valuation authority for redemption-style buy-sell arrangements. supremecourt.gov (Connelly)
  27. Instructions for Form 706, United States Estate (and Generation-Skipping Transfer) Tax Return — Schedule D reporting for insurance on the decedent's life; state-return conformity is generally by statutory cross-reference to the federal §2031 gross-estate composition. irs.gov (Form 706 Instructions)
  28. Rev. Rul. 76-490, 1976-2 C.B. 300 — Governs valuation of a life-insurance policy on the insured's life where the insured predeceases another life (as in second-to-die policies); the includible amount for the earlier-death insured is the interpolated terminal reserve plus unearned premium, reflecting the actuarial value of the future death benefit to the survivor. irs.gov (Rev. Rul. 76-490)
