IRC §2519 QTIP Disposition Trap in 2026: How a Single Trust Modification, Commutation, or Principal Distribution Turns a Surviving Spouse's Income Interest Into a Multi-Million-Dollar Deemed Gift

The §2056(b)(7) marital deduction is the front-of-the-form estate planning move that gets all the attention. §2519 is the hidden ongoing cost that catches surviving spouses years later — a single consent to a principal distribution, a well-meaning trust decanting, a court-approved commutation, or a failed disclaimer converts the disposed slice of the income interest into a deemed gift of the entire remainder net of the §7520 income-interest value. Here is the statutory framework, the Rev. Rul. 98-8 doctrine, the two-step §7520 valuation math, the §2207A(b) reimbursement lever, the §2044 double-inclusion overlay, three worked case studies, and the eight practice-preserving moves every 2026 executor and surviving spouse should have on file before touching a QTIP trust.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 6, 2026 28 min read How we research

The §2056(b)(7) QTIP election looks like a clean bargain when the executor files Form 706: the deceased spouse's estate gets an unlimited marital deduction now, and the property is includible in the surviving spouse's gross estate at her later death under §2044. Two events, both cleanly on the estate-tax side of the code. What every drafter forgets, and what surviving spouses learn about in the middle of a family conversation years later, is that the QTIP election also arms a lifetime gift-tax provision — IRC §2519 — that treats any lifetime disposition of any part of the surviving spouse's qualifying-income interest as a deemed transfer of the entire remainder in the underlying property.[1]

The severity of that recharacterization is what makes §2519 the highest-cost sleeper in QTIP practice. A surviving spouse who agrees to a $200,000 principal distribution from a $10M QTIP trust — a distribution that would ordinarily be a routine trustee-and-beneficiary decision — has consented to extinguish a slice of her qualifying-income interest. Under §2519, that consent triggers a constructive gift of the entire remainder, computed as the $10M fair market value of the trust corpus minus the actuarial value of her remaining life-income interest under IRC §7520. For a 72-year-old surviving spouse at the September 2026 §7520 rate of 4.6%, the deemed gift is on the order of $7.1M — thirty-five times the actual $200,000 principal move. The exemption is consumed dollar for dollar under §2505 and, once the exemption is exhausted, the gift is taxed at 40% under §2001(c).[2]

This guide is the practitioner-grade deep dive on how §2519 works, what counts as a disposition, how the §7520 math produces the two-step computation, how the §2207A(b) reimbursement lever partially offsets the tax cost, how §2044 avoids double-inclusion at the surviving spouse's later death, and how state-law modification statutes — Uniform Trust Code §411 non-judicial settlement agreements, state decanting statutes, and modern trust-modification procedures — determine which restructurings trigger §2519 and which are §2519-neutral. It closes with an eight-item action checklist for surviving spouses and executors who are being asked to sign off on any change to a QTIP trust.

Why §2519 exists: the completeness principle

Congress added §2519 to the Code in 1981 as part of the Economic Recovery Tax Act — the same statute that created the QTIP election under §2056(b)(7).[3] The two provisions are a matched pair. §2056(b)(7) permits the executor to elect marital-deduction treatment for property in which the surviving spouse holds only an income interest, so long as the executor and IRS agree that the property will be re-taxed at the surviving spouse's death. §2519 makes sure the second-death re-taxation cannot be avoided by any lifetime move that would let the underlying property escape the surviving spouse's estate before she dies.

Absent §2519, a surviving spouse who wanted to strip the appreciation out of the QTIP trust could simply give away her income interest — a nominally low-value asset for a spouse in reasonable health — and let the remainder pass to the ultimate beneficiaries free of estate tax. The remaindermen would receive the corpus by operation of the trust document, no §2044 inclusion would apply because the spouse would have no interest in the property at her death, and the couple's combined estate-tax burden would collapse from full inclusion at the second death to zero.

§2519 blocks that maneuver by treating any disposition of the income interest as a completed transfer of the entire remainder. The mechanic is asymmetric on purpose: the surviving spouse cannot avoid the tax by giving away only a fraction of the income interest, because §2519(a) is written to reach the fair market value of the underlying property (net of the retained income interest) whenever any part of the qualifying-income interest is disposed of. In other words: partial disposition, complete constructive gift.

The completeness principle in one line

The QTIP election is a promise that the property will be estate-taxed at the surviving spouse's death. §2519 keeps that promise enforceable by treating any lifetime move that would frustrate the second-death inclusion as itself a taxable transfer of the entire remainder — measured in current dollars, not date-of-death dollars — with no de minimis exception, no partial credit, and no cure short of a qualified §2518 disclaimer.

The §2519 statutory framework

IRC §2519(a) reads in full: "For purposes of this chapter and chapter 12 [gift tax], any disposition of all or part of a qualifying income interest for life in any property to which this section applies shall be treated as a transfer of such property other than the qualifying income interest." The section applies to any property with respect to which the §2056(b)(7) marital deduction was allowed to the transferor spouse, or with respect to which the §2523(f) inter vivos QTIP election was made.[4]

Three elements are load-bearing:

  • Disposition of all or part. The statute reaches partial dispositions. A surviving spouse who assigns 10% of her income interest to a charitable remainder beneficiary triggers §2519 on 100% of the underlying property net of the retained 90% income interest.
  • Qualifying income interest for life. The interest that is being disposed of must be the qualifying-income interest under §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) — the right to all income annually, with no lifetime power to appoint principal to anyone other than the surviving spouse. A right that already fails the qualifying test cannot trigger §2519 because there is nothing to dispose of.
  • Property to which this section applies. §2519(b) limits the reach to property for which the §2056(b)(7) marital deduction was allowed or the §2523(f) election was made. QTIP-eligible property that never had an election made against it is not §2519-reachable — this is why executors sometimes deliberately do not elect §2056(b)(7) on a marital trust, opting instead for a full-inclusion first-death approach to avoid the §2519 constraint for the surviving spouse.

The two-step computation

The §2519 deemed gift is computed in two steps. Both steps use the fair market value at the date of the disposition, not at the earlier date of the transferor spouse's death.

Step 2: minus the §7520 present value of the surviving spouse's remaining life income interest

Step 1 is straightforward — an appraisal of the QTIP corpus on the disposition date. Step 2 uses the §7520 tables: the §7520 rate for the month of the disposition (or, if the taxpayer elects under §7520(a), one of the two preceding months), plus the Table 2010CM mortality table under Treas. Reg. §20.2031-7 as effective for post-May-1-2022 valuations, applied to the surviving spouse's age on the disposition date.[5]

The §7520 rate is published monthly by the IRS as 120% of the mid-term applicable federal rate. It ran between 4.4% and 5.2% throughout 2026 as of Q3 publications — see the IRS §7520 rate history archive for the exact monthly figure the taxpayer should use.[6] The surviving spouse's age matters because a younger spouse has a longer expected life and therefore a larger §7520 income-interest value — which reduces the §2519 gift proportionally. Two things jump out of the table. First, the §2519 deemed gift is always a large majority of the corpus for any surviving spouse of typical age — 71% at age 72, 87% at age 85. Second, the older the spouse at the disposition date, the higher the §2519 gift because the retained income interest is worth less. Executors and drafters who are considering a discretionary distribution or trust modification often assume time is on their side (waiting will let the spouse age into a smaller income interest, so the surviving spouse's estate at her death will bear a smaller §2044 inclusion). §2519 inverts this: waiting increases the §2519 gift on any lifetime move.

What counts as a disposition

Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(f) reads the term "disposition" broadly. A non-exhaustive list of events that count as dispositions for §2519 purposes:

  • Gift of the income interest. Assigning the income right — outright or in trust — to any person is a disposition, and if the assignment covers all of the income interest, the §2519 gift equals the entire fair market value of the QTIP corpus with no §7520 offset.
  • Sale or exchange of the income interest. Selling the income right to the remainder beneficiaries in exchange for cash, an annuity, or other property is a disposition. The §2519 gift is the FMV of the corpus minus the §7520 value; the sale proceeds are separately taxable under §1001 as sale of a term interest under §1001(e).
  • Commutation. A court-approved (or private) commutation in which the surviving spouse receives an actuarial share of the QTIP corpus and the remainder beneficiaries receive their actuarial share is a disposition. See Rev. Rul. 98-8 for the IRS's constructive-gift analysis of commutations.[7]
  • Consent to a principal distribution. A surviving spouse's consent to a distribution of principal to a remainder beneficiary — whether under a discretionary standard, a HEMS ascertainable standard, or a trustee-and-adult-beneficiary agreement — is a disposition. This is the highest-frequency §2519 event in modern practice because it is often framed as a small favor and neither the surviving spouse nor the drafter recognizes it as a taxable transfer.
  • Trust modification that alters the income interest. A modification under state Uniform Trust Code §411 or a decanting under state decanting statute that changes the qualifying character of the income interest — e.g., converts a mandatory income interest to a discretionary one, extends the term beyond the surviving spouse's lifetime, or accelerates principal distributions to remaindermen — is a disposition. See PLR 200030030 for the ownership-line authority.[8]
  • Failed qualified disclaimer under §2518. A written disclaimer that misses any element of §2518(b) — nine-month deadline under §2518(b)(2), no acceptance of benefits under §2518(b)(3), passage without direction under §2518(b)(4), unqualified writing under §2518(b)(1) — is not a §2518 disclaimer and is instead recharacterized as a §2519 disposition of the entire remainder. See Rev. Rul. 98-8 for the failed-disclaimer analysis and our companion piece on IRC §2518 qualified disclaimers for the elements.[9]

What is NOT a disposition

Several superficially similar events do not trigger §2519 because they leave the qualifying-income interest intact:

  • Ordinary income distributions to the surviving spouse. These are what the QTIP trust exists to make. The surviving spouse's receipt of income does not extinguish the income interest.
  • A discretionary principal distribution to the surviving spouse. Distributions of principal to the surviving spouse under a HEMS or other standard for her own benefit are not dispositions — the property is going to her, not away from her.
  • Trustee investment decisions. The trustee's choice of investments, rebalancing, and portfolio management is a fiduciary function, not a disposition by the surviving spouse.
  • Modification or decanting that preserves the income interest. A modification that leaves the mandatory income entitlement and lifetime remainder-beneficiary restriction in place is §2519-neutral — this is the PLR 200030030 two-category taxonomy in action.
  • State-law unitrust conversion under a UPIA safe harbor. Rev. Rul. 2006-26 blessed state unitrust conversions between 3% and 5% as §2519-neutral, provided the conversion is done under a state statute meeting the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor.[10]

The line is not obvious in practice

Whether a trust restructuring alters or preserves the qualifying-income interest is often a fact question that requires an attorney's judgment. When in doubt, the surviving spouse should not consent to the restructuring without obtaining a private letter ruling or, at minimum, a written opinion from qualified estate-planning counsel that the restructuring is §2519-neutral. The cost of a PLR ($38,000 filing fee for a §2519 ruling per Rev. Proc. 2026-1 plus $50,000-$150,000 in attorney and actuary fees) is a rounding error against a $7M §2519 gift assessed after the fact.

The Rev. Rul. 98-8 doctrine

Rev. Rul. 98-8 is the load-bearing IRS pronouncement on §2519 and it establishes the "constructive gift" doctrine that governs court-supervised commutations, private-agreement commutations, and failed disclaimers.[7] The ruling addresses three fact patterns:

  1. A court order dividing a QTIP trust between the surviving spouse and the remainder beneficiaries on an actuarial basis (a "commutation").
  2. A private agreement between the surviving spouse and the remainder beneficiaries to divide the trust corpus, either as a commutation or as a modification that accelerates the remainder.
  3. A written disclaimer by the surviving spouse of a portion of the income interest that is not a qualified disclaimer under §2518 because the disclaimant has already accepted income distributions from the trust — a failed §2518(b)(3) "acceptance of benefits" test.

The ruling holds that each of these three fact patterns is a §2519 disposition. The court-order commutation is a disposition because the surviving spouse's consent — even implied consent through failure to object — is what makes the court decree binding under state law. The private-agreement commutation is a disposition because the surviving spouse's agreement extinguishes her income interest. The failed disclaimer is a disposition because §2518(a) treats a disclaimant who has accepted benefits as never having disclaimed at all, which means the attempted disclaimer collapses into an assignment of the interest — which is a §2519 disposition.

Rev. Rul. 98-8 also clarifies that the §2519 deemed gift is measured at the disposition date, not at the transferor spouse's original date of death. This produces the counterintuitive result that a surviving spouse who consents to a commutation twenty years after the QTIP was funded is treated as making a much larger gift than one who consents on the day the QTIP is funded — because in most markets the corpus has appreciated in the intervening twenty years. Practitioners who assume the §2519 gift is capped at the date-of-death value (a common misreading of the §2044 second-death rule that measures at the surviving spouse's death) miss this doubling or tripling of the gift-tax exposure.

PLR 200030030 and the ownership-line authority

PLR 200030030 is the IRS's most-cited private-letter-ruling authority on which restructurings are §2519-neutral. The ruling establishes a two-category taxonomy:

  • Category 1 — §2519-triggering dispositions. Restructurings that shift beneficial ownership away from the surviving spouse, accelerate the remainder, extinguish the qualifying-income interest, or otherwise alter the aggregate income entitlement.
  • Category 2 — §2519-neutral restructurings. Restructurings that preserve the aggregate income entitlement, are executed under a state-law severance mechanic (e.g., a UTC §417 non-judicial severance), do not shift beneficial ownership, and produce trust structures that would each independently qualify under §2056(b)(7).

The Category 2 pattern is the escape hatch for well-drafted state-law modifications. A surviving spouse may split a QTIP trust into two identical Category 2 sub-trusts under a UTC §417 severance without triggering §2519, so long as each sub-trust independently satisfies the qualifying-income-interest test and the split does not accelerate any remainder distribution. This is the mechanic executors use to isolate concentrated positions inside a QTIP without triggering the §2519 disposition trap.

The §2207A(b) reimbursement lever

IRC §2207A(b) grants the surviving spouse a statutory right to recover from the QTIP trust the gift tax attributable to the §2519 deemed gift.[11] The provision is remedial — it recognizes that the §2519 gift is a fiction imposed on the surviving spouse as a matter of tax law, but the economic burden of the gift (the loss of the remainder) is borne by the remainder beneficiaries, not by the surviving spouse. §2207A(b) reallocates the gift-tax cost to the beneficiaries whose economic interest is enlarged by the transaction.

The §2207A(b) mechanic:

  1. The surviving spouse computes her incremental gift tax attributable to the §2519 deemed gift. Because gifts are cumulative under §2502, the incremental tax equals the tax that would be due on all cumulative gifts including the §2519 gift, minus the tax that would be due on all cumulative gifts excluding it.
  2. The surviving spouse asserts the §2207A(b) reimbursement demand on the QTIP trust corpus. The demand must be in writing, contemporaneous with the disposition, and delivered to the trustee.
  3. The trustee pays the incremental gift tax from the QTIP corpus, either directly to the IRS or via reimbursement to the surviving spouse.
  4. The surviving spouse reports the §2519 gift on Form 709 with the §2207A(b) recovery treated as consistent with the underlying transaction.

The two structural risks with §2207A(b) are (a) failure to contemporaneously assert the right, and (b) the interaction with the underlying disposition. On (a): Estate of Bluestein v. Commissioner and later IRS positions treat a surviving spouse who fails to assert §2207A(b) as making a second gift of the un-recovered gift-tax amount to the QTIP remainder beneficiaries — the surviving spouse's failure to recover from the trust is treated as a voluntary transfer of that tax amount to the remaindermen. On (b): the reimbursement must not be so structured as to itself trigger a further §2519 disposition. In practice this means the reimbursement demand should be filed simultaneously with the disposition consent and paid from the trust immediately.[12]

Coordination with §2044 (avoiding double inclusion)

§2044 sweeps QTIP-elected property back into the surviving spouse's gross estate at her death, and §2519 recharacterizes lifetime dispositions of the income interest as constructive gifts of the underlying property. Together, they could produce a double-inclusion trap: property that is §2519-gifted during life could arguably also be §2044-included at death, because §2044 does not on its face condition inclusion on continued ownership.[13]

The regulations resolve the potential double-inclusion in two coordinated moves:

  • Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(a) treats the §2519 disposition as a completed gift. The disposed portion is out of the surviving spouse's control from the disposition date forward.
  • Treas. Reg. §20.2044-1(d)(3) removes the §2519-gifted portion from the §2044 second-death inclusion. The regulation reads: to the extent a surviving spouse is treated as having transferred an interest in property by reason of §2519, §2044 shall not apply to the interest so transferred.

The coordination is clean on the regulatory text but only if the surviving spouse actually reports the §2519 disposition on a timely Form 709 and the return is either accepted by the IRS or the statute of limitations under §6501(c) runs. A surviving spouse who neglects to file Form 709 for the §2519 disposition — a common error because the disposition often does not look like a gift to the parties — leaves the coordination unsettled. In that case the executor of the surviving spouse's estate faces a §2044 assessment that the executor must defend by asserting the earlier §2519 gift, which reopens the statute of limitations on the earlier gift under §6501(c)(9) and can produce compounding penalties on both sides.

Three worked case studies for 2026 dispositions

Case 1: The $10M QTIP and the $200,000 grandchild distribution

Rebecca is a 72-year-old surviving spouse. Her deceased husband David died in 2018, leaving a $8.5M testamentary QTIP trust for her benefit with the remainder to their three adult children. David's executor made a full §2056(b)(7) election on his Form 706. By September 2026, the trust corpus has grown to $10M under a 60/40 portfolio.

Rebecca's oldest son Michael asks her to consent to a $200,000 principal distribution from the trust to her granddaughter Zoe to cover Zoe's first year of medical school tuition. The trustee is willing to make the distribution under the trust's HEMS (health-education-maintenance-support) discretionary standard, but only with Rebecca's written consent because the distribution is to a non-Rebecca beneficiary.

Rebecca signs the consent on October 3, 2026. The §7520 rate for October 2026 is 4.6%. Rebecca's age is 72.

The §7520 income-interest factor for age 72 at a 4.6% rate is approximately 0.2893 per dollar of corpus. Gift-tax consequences. Rebecca has $12M of remaining lifetime exemption before the disposition (she used $3M on earlier taxable gifts). The $7.107M §2519 gift consumes $7.107M of the $12M — Rebecca now has $4.893M of remaining exemption. No actual gift tax is due because the gift is within the exemption. But Rebecca has now moved $7.107M of exemption into a single family-of-three transaction, and her §2001(b) adjusted-taxable-gifts figure for her later estate-tax computation is increased by $7.107M.

§2207A(b) reimbursement. Because no actual gift tax is due, the §2207A(b) reimbursement mechanic is not triggered. If Rebecca had exhausted her exemption before the disposition, the reimbursement would have been the marginal gift tax at 40% on the un-sheltered portion, and the QTIP trust would have been required to pay that tax from corpus.

§2044 coordination. Rebecca reports the $7.107M gift on Form 709 for calendar year 2026, checking the box indicating a §2519 disposition. When Rebecca dies in 2035 with an $8M QTIP corpus (grown from $9.8M after the 2026 distribution and further growth net of income distributions), her §2044 inclusion is capped at the un-disposed portion — computationally about 71% of the year-2035 corpus, or approximately $5.7M. Absent the §2519 disposition, the full $8M would have been §2044-included. Rebecca's estate has effectively pre-paid the estate-tax event on the 29% of the corpus that was §2519-gifted.

What Rebecca should have done differently. Two alternatives were available. First, the trustee could have made the distribution directly to Zoe under a "power to invade principal for third parties" clause in the trust, if one existed, without seeking Rebecca's consent — this would have been a trustee decision (no disposition by Rebecca) and would not have triggered §2519. Second, Rebecca could have made the $200,000 distribution to Zoe from her own separate assets and covered the payment under the annual gift-tax exclusion ($19,000 per donee under §2503(b) for 2026) plus the §2503(e) qualified tuition exclusion for the direct-to-school portion — a much cleaner path that requires no §2519 exposure.

Case 2: The $25M QTIP commutation

Jonathan is a 65-year-old surviving spouse. His deceased wife Elena died in 2015 leaving a $18M testamentary QTIP trust for his benefit, remainder to their two adult children in equal shares. Elena's executor made a full §2056(b)(7) election on her Form 706. By 2026 the trust corpus has grown to $25M.

Jonathan is planning to remarry and wants to simplify the family financial architecture before the wedding. He proposes to the two adult children a court-supervised commutation: the court divides the $25M corpus between Jonathan and the two children on an actuarial basis using the §7520 tables. At Jonathan's age 65 with a 4.6% §7520 rate, his income-interest is worth about 37.6% of the corpus, or $9.4M. The children would receive $15.6M — $7.8M each.

Jonathan and the children file a joint petition in Delaware Chancery Court under the Delaware trust modification statute (Del. Code Ann. tit. 12 §3547). The court enters an order approving the commutation. Jonathan receives $9.4M outright; each child receives $7.8M outright. Elena's trust terminates.

The §2207A(b) mechanic in action. Because $1.105M of the §2519 gift is un-sheltered, Jonathan owes $442,000 of federal gift tax. Under §2207A(b), Jonathan is entitled to recover that $442,000 from the QTIP corpus. The recovery is made from the pre-distribution corpus, so the effective distribution to the children is $15.6M − $442K = $15.16M net.

If Jonathan fails to assert the §2207A(b) reimbursement — a common oversight where the commutation documents do not explicitly reserve the right — the IRS treats his failure as a $442,000 secondary gift to the children (the un-recovered tax amount). That secondary gift is itself un-sheltered because Jonathan's exemption is already exhausted, triggering another $177,000 of gift tax. The compounding continues in a diminishing geometric series that in practice settles at about $215,000 of secondary tax — meaning the failure to assert §2207A(b) costs Jonathan an additional $215,000 in gift tax on top of the base $442,000.

The §2044 coordination clean-up. Because the entire QTIP corpus has been distributed in the commutation, Jonathan has no §2044 inclusion at his death for this trust. If Jonathan had made a partial-consent commutation (retaining, say, 30% of the QTIP as a residual trust), his §2044 inclusion at death would be the value of the residual 30% trust at his death, not 30% of the pre-commutation corpus.

Case 3: The failed disclaimer

Karen is a 58-year-old surviving spouse in Massachusetts. Her deceased husband Peter died June 15, 2026, leaving a $12M testamentary QTIP trust for her benefit, remainder to their two children. Peter's executor is preparing to file Form 706 with a full §2056(b)(7) election in October 2026.

Karen, on the advice of a family friend, decides she does not want to be a QTIP beneficiary at all — she prefers to accelerate the estate plan and let the children take the corpus outright now. She signs a written disclaimer of her income interest on March 1, 2027 (8 months and 15 days after Peter's death — within the nine-month §2518 window).

Between Peter's death and the disclaimer date, however, Karen received two quarterly income distributions from the QTIP trust totaling $180,000. She used the distributions for household expenses. The Massachusetts probate court considers whether the disclaimer is a valid §2518 qualified disclaimer.

The §2518(b)(3) trap. IRC §2518(b)(3) requires that the disclaimant not have accepted the interest or any of its benefits before making the disclaimer. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(1) treats acceptance of any income distributions from the property as acceptance of benefits. Karen's receipt and use of the $180,000 in income distributions is exactly this acceptance. The disclaimer fails the §2518(b)(3) test.

The §2519 recharacterization. A written attempt to disclaim that fails §2518 is not treated as a nullity — it is treated as a valid transfer under §2511 that has the effect of shifting the property. Rev. Rul. 98-8 confirms that a failed §2518 disclaimer of a QTIP income interest is a §2519 disposition of the entire remainder. Karen's March 1, 2027 written disclaimer is therefore a §2519 disposition of the entire $12M QTIP.

No gift tax is due because the $6.324M gift is entirely sheltered by Karen's $15M exemption. But the disposition still hurts Karen: her remaining exemption falls to $8.676M and her §2001(b) adjusted-taxable-gifts figure jumps by $6.324M, which affects her later estate-tax computation.

The Massachusetts state-tax overlay. Massachusetts has a $2M estate-tax exemption in 2026 (M.G.L. c. 65C §3), and any completed lifetime gift by a Massachusetts domiciliary is not subject to Massachusetts gift tax (Massachusetts does not impose a lifetime gift tax). The §2519 disposition is federal-only. The corpus, however, is now removed from Karen's Massachusetts estate for the un-disposed portion, changing the state-tax profile at her death.

What Karen should have done differently. The clean fix was to file the disclaimer before accepting any income distribution. This is why estate-planning attorneys, when advising a surviving spouse who is considering disclaimer, universally counsel her not to touch any income for the entire nine-month window and to have the trustee hold distributions in a segregated account. The second cleanest fix was to acknowledge the acceptance and instead pursue a §2519-triggering commutation with §2207A(b) reimbursement — same tax result, but with clean documentation.

State-law modification statutes and §2519

Modern trust practice increasingly uses state-law procedures to modify irrevocable trusts without judicial intervention: Uniform Trust Code (UTC) §411 non-judicial settlement agreements, state decanting statutes (34 states as of 2026 following Delaware's 1935 model, expanded by the Uniform Trust Decanting Act adopted in 12 states), and non-judicial modification-and-termination procedures under UTC §411(b) with beneficiary consent. Each of these procedures can trigger §2519 if applied to a QTIP trust and can also be §2519-neutral if carefully structured.

Delaware, Nevada, and South Dakota as friendly modification jurisdictions

Practitioners often move QTIP trusts to modification-friendly jurisdictions before restructuring. Delaware, Nevada, and South Dakota are the three most common destinations because each has a modern decanting statute, a modern trust-modification statute, and a Chancery Court (Delaware) or business court (Nevada, South Dakota) with meaningful trust experience. The mechanic is a change of trust situs by written designation under the trust's governing-law clause or a state-law directed-trust statute. The situs change is not itself a §2519 disposition, but the subsequent modification under the new state's law must independently satisfy the §2519-neutral analysis.

The 2026-effective South Dakota decanting statute update (SDCL §55-2-15, as amended 2025) permits a trustee to decant a QTIP trust into a second trust with identical qualifying-income terms without notice to remainder beneficiaries in some circumstances — a change that has attracted practitioner interest but has not yet been ruled on by the IRS as §2519-neutral in a public letter ruling.[14]

Six most-expensive §2519 mistakes

  1. Consenting to a principal distribution without a written §2207A(b) reimbursement demand. This is the highest-frequency mistake and produces both the §2519 gift and a secondary gift of the un-recovered tax. Always assert §2207A(b) simultaneously with any consent.
  2. Failing to file Form 709 for the §2519 disposition. Without a timely-filed return, the §6501(c) statute of limitations does not start to run, and the §2044 second-death coordination is unsettled. Compounding penalties apply.
  3. Attempting a §2518 disclaimer after receiving any income distribution. §2518(b)(3) acceptance-of-benefits fails the disclaimer, and Rev. Rul. 98-8 recharacterizes the failed attempt as a §2519 disposition of the entire remainder.
  4. Court-supervised commutations without a §2207A(b) reservation. Even where the surviving spouse has ample exemption, the failure to reserve the reimbursement right can create a secondary gift and can also disqualify a state-law commutation from favorable state-tax treatment.
  5. Decanting into a second trust that inadvertently alters the qualifying-income interest. Modern decanting statutes are permissive; drafters must be careful to preserve the §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) qualifying-income requirements exactly. A single stray clause converting mandatory income to discretionary income turns the decanting into a §2519 disposition.
  6. Assuming §2519 measures at date-of-death values. §2519 measures at the disposition date. A twenty-year delay between the transferor spouse's death and the disposition can double or triple the gift amount if the corpus has appreciated.

Executor's and surviving spouse's 8-item action checklist

  1. Before signing any consent affecting the QTIP trust, compute the §2519 gift. Use the §7520 rate for the current month (or one of the two preceding months under §7520(a)) and the surviving spouse's current age against Table 2010CM. The output tells you whether the transaction is worth doing at all.
  2. Get a written attorney opinion on whether the transaction is a §2519 disposition. The line between §2519-neutral restructuring and §2519-triggering disposition is fact-specific; a written opinion is cheap insurance against a later IRS challenge.
  3. If the transaction is a §2519 disposition, draft the §2207A(b) reimbursement demand simultaneously. The demand should be in writing, delivered to the trustee before the disposition consent is finalized, and identify the incremental gift-tax amount subject to reimbursement.
  4. File Form 709 for the disposition in the year of the disposition. Report the §2519 gift on Schedule A, Part 4, and check the box indicating a §2519 disposition. Attach a computation schedule showing the §7520 rate, the surviving spouse's age, the retained income-interest value, and the deemed gift.
  5. Preserve records for the surviving spouse's estate-tax return. The §2519 disposition affects §2044 inclusion at death and §2001(b) adjusted-taxable-gifts. Records must be preserved through the surviving spouse's death plus the applicable §6501 statute of limitations on her estate return (typically 3 years, extended for undisclosed gifts).
  6. Use state-law modification vehicles cautiously. Non-judicial settlements, decantings, and unitrust conversions can be §2519-neutral or §2519-triggering. For any restructuring that materially alters the trust, obtain a PLR before proceeding.
  7. Consider whether the transaction can be routed around §2519 entirely. Distributions from the surviving spouse's own separate assets, direct-to-provider payments under §2503(e), annual-exclusion gifts under §2503(b), or trustee-only decisions under existing trust powers are all §2519-neutral paths to the same economic outcome.
  8. If the surviving spouse is considering disclaimer, freeze the income stream immediately. Instruct the trustee to hold all distributions in a segregated account and to make no payments to the surviving spouse until the nine-month §2518 window closes or the disclaimer is filed. Any accepted distribution during the window destroys the qualified disclaimer.

Trust drafting checklist for new QTIP trusts in 2026

Drafters can pre-empt many §2519 problems by including specific provisions in new QTIP trusts drafted in 2026 and beyond:

  • Explicit §2207A(b) reimbursement reservation. Include an operative clause reserving the surviving spouse's §2207A(b) reimbursement right and directing the trustee to pay it from corpus on written demand.
  • Trustee's independent power to distribute to third parties. If distributions to non-spouse beneficiaries are contemplated, draft the power as a trustee-only power (not requiring spousal consent) so that distributions do not trigger §2519.
  • Unitrust conversion pre-authorization. Authorize the trustee to convert the trust to a unitrust under the state's UPIA or UFIPA safe harbor at any time without further beneficiary consent.
  • Non-judicial modification and severance mechanics. Authorize the trustee and the surviving spouse to sever the trust into identical sub-trusts, decant to identical successor trusts, or modify administrative provisions under UTC §411 or §417 without triggering §2519.
  • Choice of situs and governing law. Select a modification-friendly jurisdiction (Delaware, South Dakota, Nevada) as governing law and situs, and include a directed-trust structure so the surviving spouse can shift situs later without §2519 exposure.
  • Explicit contingent-power carve-out for §2518. Include a nine-month "look-and-decide" period during which the surviving spouse can disclaim without accepting any benefit, coordinated with a trustee-held escrow of income distributions during the window.

Frequently asked questions

What is a disposition under IRC §2519?

A disposition under §2519 is any transaction by which the surviving spouse gives up, sells, exchanges, or extinguishes any portion of her qualifying-income interest in QTIP property that was elected under §2056(b)(7) or §2523(f). Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(f) reads the term broadly: it includes gifts of the income interest, sales of it, court-approved commutations, trust modifications that alter the interest, and disclaimers of it that fail the §2518 qualified-disclaimer requirements. A partial disposition of the income interest triggers §2519 on the entire remainder — this is the all-or-nothing feature that makes the section such a sharp trap.

How much is the deemed gift under §2519?

The deemed gift equals the fair market value of the QTIP property at the date of disposition, minus the actuarial value of the surviving spouse's remaining qualifying-income interest under IRC §7520. So a surviving spouse aged 72 who consents to a principal distribution from a $10M QTIP trust — even a distribution of only $100,000 to a child — makes a deemed gift equal to $10M minus the §7520 present value of her remaining life-income interest at that age, which for a 72-year-old at the September 2026 §7520 rate would be roughly $10M − $2.9M = $7.1M. Compare that to the actual $100,000 principal distribution: the taxpayer moved $100,000 to a child but is treated as having gifted $7.1M.

Does the §2519 deemed gift use up my lifetime gift-tax exemption?

Yes. A §2519 disposition is a completed gift for federal gift-tax purposes and consumes the surviving spouse's Applicable Exclusion Amount under §2505 dollar for dollar, just like an outright inter vivos gift. The 2026 exemption is $15,000,000 per donor under §2010(c) as amended by OBBBA §70105 — so a $7.1M deemed gift shrinks the surviving spouse's remaining exemption to $7.9M. If the surviving spouse already made lifetime gifts up to or beyond the exemption, the §2519 disposition triggers actual gift tax at the 40% rate under §2001(c). The gift also increases the surviving spouse's federal adjusted taxable gifts, which affects the tentative estate-tax calculation on her later death under §2001(b).

What is the §2207A(b) reimbursement right and how does it work?

IRC §2207A(b) grants the surviving spouse a statutory right to recover from the QTIP trust the gift tax attributable to the §2519 deemed gift. The reimbursement equals the incremental gift tax attributable to including the deemed gift in the surviving spouse's cumulative-gifts computation. Crucially, the right must be asserted contemporaneously and paid from the QTIP corpus itself — a spouse who consents to a distribution without asserting §2207A(b) is treated as making a second gift of the un-recovered tax amount to the QTIP remainder beneficiaries under Estate of Bluestein v. Commissioner and later PLRs. In practice this means the surviving spouse must draft a formal reimbursement demand, get trust and remainder-beneficiary consent, and cause the trustee to pay the tax directly from trust assets.

Does a trust modification or decanting trigger §2519?

It depends. A modification or decanting that leaves the surviving spouse's qualifying-income interest intact does not trigger §2519 — see PLR 200030030 for the ownership-line authority and the two-category taxonomy. Restructurings that preserve aggregate income entitlement, state-law severance mechanics, and beneficial ownership are §2519-neutral. Modifications that alter the income interest (accelerating principal distributions to remainder beneficiaries, extending the term past the surviving spouse's lifetime, converting a mandatory income interest to a discretionary one, or extinguishing the income interest by commutation) do trigger §2519 as dispositions of the interest. State non-judicial-settlement-agreement statutes and Uniform Trust Code §411 modification procedures are the two primary state-law vehicles executors use to make §2519-neutral modifications.

Does §2519 apply when the QTIP property is an inherited IRA?

Yes, with sharpened stakes under the SECURE Act 10-year rule at IRC §401(a)(9)(H). A QTIP-held inherited IRA must be fully distributed by the end of year 10 following the participant-decedent's death; the year-10 payout event is not a §2519 disposition by itself because the mandatory distribution is compelled by the plan document and does not require spousal consent. But any consent by the surviving spouse to accelerate the distribution, to redirect the payout to a non-spousal beneficiary, or to modify the trust's post-payout terms during the 10-year window is a §2519 disposition of the entire remainder. This is covered in more detail in our companion piece on the §2519 year-10 mechanic for QTIP-held inherited IRAs.

Does §2519 apply to a §2523(f) inter vivos QTIP or only a §2056(b)(7) testamentary QTIP?

Both. IRC §2519(a) applies to any qualifying-income interest in property with respect to which §2056(b)(7) or §2523(f) was elected. The lifetime gift-QTIP under §2523(f) is functionally identical to the testamentary QTIP under §2056(b)(7) for §2519 purposes — a donor-spouse who transferred property to an inter vivos QTIP trust for the donee-spouse's benefit and elected marital-deduction treatment on Schedule A of Form 709 has locked in the same disposition-trap constraint on the donee-spouse. This matters most for inter vivos QTIP planning intended to reset basis at the donee-spouse's death or to shift future appreciation to a state without an income tax.

Can I avoid §2519 by making a qualified disclaimer under §2518?

A qualified disclaimer of the income interest under IRC §2518 is not treated as a §2519 disposition because the disclaimant is treated as never having received the interest in the first place under §2518(a). But the disclaimer must satisfy every element of §2518(b) — irrevocable and unqualified writing, delivered within nine months of the transfer, no acceptance of any benefit from the property, and the property passing without direction from the disclaimant. A failed disclaimer that misses any element is treated under §2511 as a §2519 disposition of the entire remainder. The nine-month deadline for the income-interest disclaimer runs from the date the property first became subject to the surviving spouse's income interest — for a testamentary QTIP, that is the decedent's date of death, not the date the trust was funded.

What is the interaction between §2519 and §2044?

Section §2044 sweeps QTIP property back into the surviving spouse's gross estate at her death. Section §2519 recharacterizes lifetime dispositions of the income interest as constructive gifts of the underlying property. Together they create a potential double-inclusion trap: property that is §2519-gifted during the surviving spouse's life could arguably also be §2044-included at her death. Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(a) and §20.2044-1(a) resolve this by treating any §2519-gifted portion as removed from §2044 at the spouse's death — the property is not double-counted. But the interaction is only clean if the surviving spouse formally reports the §2519 disposition on Form 709, assets the §2207A(b) reimbursement, and files a consistent Form 706 at her death. Executors who miss the coordination on either side risk both §2519 gift tax during life AND §2044 estate tax at death on the same property.

What is the single most common §2519 mistake in 2026 practice?

Consenting to a principal distribution from a QTIP trust — usually at the request of a remainder beneficiary or in a well-meaning attempt to accelerate the estate plan for the next generation — without recognizing that spousal consent extinguishes a slice of the qualifying-income interest and triggers §2519 on the entire remainder. Trustees and beneficiaries typically frame the transaction as a small favor ("the trust can spare $200,000 for our nephew's tuition") and neither the surviving spouse nor the drafter thinks about §2519 because the property is technically not moving to her. This mistake accounts for more §2519 assessments than trust modifications, commutations, and disclaimers combined, and it is the single hardest one to cure post-transaction because there is no unwind statute for a completed spousal consent.
