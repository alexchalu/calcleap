IRC §2503(b) Annual Gift-Tax Exclusion in 2026: The $19,000 Per-Donee Amount, the Present-Interest Requirement, Crummey Powers, and the $190,000 §529(c)(5)(A) Front-Load

The annual exclusion is the workhorse of every wealth-transfer plan — $19,000 per donee, per calendar year, times as many donees as you like, without filing a return, without touching the $15M OBBBA lifetime exemption. But almost every trap in gift-tax practice hides in the words present interest and completed gift. This is the 2026 walkthrough of the mechanic that moves more wealth than every other section of Chapter 12 combined.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 10, 2026 21 min read How we research

Every year the Internal Revenue Service publishes an inflation-adjusted number for IRC §2503(b), and every year that number does more transfer-tax work than the rest of Chapter 12 combined. In 2026 the annual exclusion is $19,000 per donee, unchanged from 2025, set by Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section 4.42(1) under the inflation-adjustment mechanic of §2503(b)(2).[1] A single donor can move $19,000 to each of ten grandchildren — $190,000 total — and none of it appears on a Form 709, none of it counts as a taxable gift, and none of it reduces the $15 million per-person basic exclusion amount that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) made permanent effective January 1, 2026 under §2010(c)(3).[2]

The mechanic sounds simple. The application is not. §2503(b) is available only for gifts of a "present interest" — the recipient must have "an unrestricted right to the immediate use, possession, or enjoyment of property or the income from property" under Treas. Reg. §25.2503-3(b).[3] A gift in trust that does not distribute now is a future interest and gets zero exclusion until the Crummey doctrine (Crummey v. Commissioner, 397 F.2d 82 (9th Cir. 1968))[4] retrofits a present-interest gate onto the transfer. The §2513 gift-splitting election, the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging election, the §2503(c) minor's-trust exception, the §2523(i)(2) noncitizen-spouse enlarged exclusion — each is a distinct rule with its own filing consequence and its own way of failing quietly.

This guide walks the statute, the regulations, the leading cases, and the Form 709 mechanics that actually move wealth through the annual exclusion. Every dollar limit is the 2026 figure. Every rule cited traces to primary authority. When you're ready to size a plan, our gift-tax calculator, estate-tax calculator, and 529-plan calculator handle the arithmetic side.

The §2503(b) statutory framework

IRC §2503 titles itself "Taxable gifts" and does most of its work by subtraction. §2503(a) defines "taxable gifts" as the total gifts made during the calendar year, less the deductions in §§2522 (charitable), 2523 (marital), and less the exclusions in the balance of §2503. §2503(b)(1) then provides that the first $10,000 of gifts (other than gifts of future interests) to any person during the calendar year is not included in the total amount of gifts made — that is the annual exclusion.[5]

§2503(b)(2) is the inflation-adjustment engine. It ties the $10,000 base to the cost-of-living adjustment under §1(f)(3), rounds down to the next lowest multiple of $1,000, and republishes the result annually through a revenue procedure. Because the rounding step captures only whole $1,000 movements, the annual exclusion typically holds flat for one or two years at a stretch, then jumps. The sequence since 2018:

The four-part test that unlocks the exclusion

Combining §2503(b)(1) with Treas. Reg. §25.2511-1, §25.2511-2, and §25.2503-3, a transfer must clear four gates to consume the annual exclusion instead of the lifetime exemption:

  1. A completed gift under §2511. The donor must have parted with dominion and control such that no power to change the disposition remains (Treas. Reg. §25.2511-2(b)). A revocable transfer is not a gift.
  2. A present interest under §2503(b)(1). The donee must have an unrestricted right to the immediate use, possession, or enjoyment of the property or its income (Treas. Reg. §25.2503-3(b)). Future interests — including reversions, remainders, and interests conditioned on a future event — get zero exclusion.
  3. To a natural person or a §2503(c) qualifying minor's trust. Gifts to a corporation or to most trusts that fail the present-interest test do not qualify unless a Crummey withdrawal power is grafted onto the trust or the trust satisfies §2503(c).
  4. Within the calendar-year, per-donee cap of $19,000 (or $194,000 for a gift to a noncitizen spouse under §2523(i)(2)).

Miss any one gate and the entire transfer converts from a §2503(b) exclusion gift into a taxable gift that reduces the §2010(c) applicable exclusion amount. That's not fatal — the OBBBA $15M exemption is enormous — but it burns Form 709 preparation cost, reduces future planning flexibility, and, for a couple planning to use §2513 gift-splitting, requires filings that a purely-annual-exclusion strategy would avoid.

The rhythm to remember

Annual exclusion = completed + present-interest + per-donee + under the cap. Any one missing and the gift is taxable — meaning it consumes lifetime exemption, requires Form 709, and eventually reports on Schedule G of the donor's Form 706 estate return.

The present-interest requirement — where most trust gifts fail

The single biggest trap in the annual-exclusion mechanic is the present-interest rule. Treas. Reg. §25.2503-3(a) is blunt: "No part of the value of a gift of a future interest may be excluded in determining the total amount of gifts made during the calendar year." Section (b) then defines a present interest as an "unrestricted right to the immediate use, possession, or enjoyment of property or the income from property."[3]

The regulation's own examples are unforgiving. An outright gift of cash qualifies. A gift in trust that pays income currently to a named beneficiary qualifies (as to the income interest). A gift in trust with a corpus distribution deferred until age 30 does not qualify — even for the income interest, if the trustee has discretion whether to distribute income. A gift subject to a condition precedent (e.g., "if my son graduates law school") is a future interest until the condition is met.

Fondren v. Commissioner, 324 U.S. 18 (1945), remains the canonical Supreme Court statement: the "future interest" concept "connotes the right to the use, possession, or enjoyment which will commence at some future date or time." A trust that permits accumulation of income at the trustee's discretion is a future-interest trust because the beneficiary has no immediate legal claim on that income.[6]

Why direct cash gifts always work

A check or wire transfer of $19,000 to an adult donee is the easiest present-interest gift there is. The moment the funds clear, the donee has full legal title, no restrictions, no conditions. The regulation calls this an "unrestricted right to the immediate use, possession, or enjoyment of property" — the exact statutory phrase — and no §2513 election, no Crummey power, no trust drafting is required. The exclusion applies automatically, no Form 709 is required if this is the only gift to that donee in the calendar year, and no reduction to the §2010(c) $15M exemption occurs.

This is why the annual exclusion is described as the "workhorse" of wealth transfer: the strategy of writing 10 checks of $19,000 apiece to 10 grandchildren every January 2 is legally trivial, requires no filings, and moves $190,000 out of the estate every year. Doubled by a §2513 gift-splitting election with a spouse, that same donor and spouse move $380,000 per year, or $3.8 million over a decade, without a single dollar of estate-tax exposure.

The Crummey solution for gifts in trust

The problem the Crummey doctrine solves: a donor wants to put money into an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) or dynasty trust and use the annual exclusion, but the trust doesn't distribute currently. The gift to the trust is a gift of a future interest. Without help, no annual exclusion is available for that contribution.

The Crummey v. Commissioner case (Ninth Circuit, 1968) held that a beneficiary's temporary right to withdraw a trust contribution — even if the beneficiary never exercises the right and even if the trust is otherwise a future-interest trust — is itself a present interest sufficient to qualify the contribution for the annual exclusion.[4] The IRS acquiesced in Rev. Rul. 73-405, and the doctrine has been the backbone of gift-tax-efficient trust drafting ever since.

The Crummey mechanic in three moving parts

(1) Notice. The trustee sends each beneficiary written notice of the contribution and the beneficiary's right to withdraw. (2) Window. The beneficiary has a reasonable period — usually 30 to 60 days — to demand withdrawal of a portion up to the §2503(b) annual exclusion. (3) Lapse. If the beneficiary does not exercise the withdrawal right during the window, the right lapses and the contribution remains in trust. The right itself, not the exercise of it, creates the present interest.

The Cristofani extension to contingent beneficiaries

Estate of Cristofani v. Commissioner, 97 T.C. 74 (1991), extended Crummey to withdrawal rights granted to contingent beneficiaries — persons whose interest in the trust would materialize only if a prior beneficiary died. The IRS initially resisted (AOD 1996-010, "Nonacquiescence") but did not appeal, and Cristofani powers granted to contingent remainder beneficiaries continue to work in practice.[7] The design trick: a dynasty trust with 20 potential beneficiaries — parents, children, grandchildren, and contingent remainder great-grandchildren — can, with careful drafting, support 20 separate annual exclusions per year, or $380,000 in tax-free contributions from a single donor. In practice, most trust drafters cap the exclusions at the number of currently-living beneficiaries plus one generation.

The lapse-of-withdrawal-power trap under §2514(e)

Crummey powers create a subtle collateral problem. When a beneficiary's withdrawal right lapses unexercised, the lapse is treated as a release of a general power of appointment under §2514(b) — which itself is a taxable transfer by the beneficiary. §2514(e) mitigates this by treating a lapse as a release only to the extent it exceeds the greater of $5,000 or 5% of the trust corpus (the "5-and-5 power"). Above that threshold, the excess is a taxable gift by the beneficiary back into the trust (or to the other beneficiaries).[5]

Practically: a Crummey power capped at $5,000 per year avoids §2514(e) exposure entirely. A power capped at the full $19,000 exclusion generates a $14,000 excess ($19,000 − $5,000) if 5% of corpus is under $19,000; that excess is a taxable gift by the beneficiary. Advanced drafters use "hanging powers" that let the withdrawal right lapse only in $5,000 annual chunks, spreading the release over multiple years to stay inside §2514(e).

§2513 gift-splitting to $38,000

IRC §2513 lets a married couple treat gifts made by one spouse as if made half by each spouse for gift-tax purposes. Effect: the $19,000 annual exclusion per donee doubles to $38,000 per donee, without the second spouse actually needing to transfer any assets.[8] The mechanic requires:

  • Both spouses U.S. citizens or residents at the time of the gift and (for consent) at the time of the election.
  • Married throughout the calendar year unless the marriage ended by death or divorce (in which case neither remarried before year-end).
  • Consent from both spouses signaled on a timely-filed Form 709 — one spouse's return with a signature by the other under Item 18 on page 1, or two separate Forms 709 where each signs the other's, per the Form 709 Instructions.[9]

If the couple splits, all gifts made by either spouse to third parties during the year are split — you cannot cherry-pick. Gifts between spouses (which typically qualify for the unlimited §2523 marital deduction) are excluded from the split.

The §2513 vs Crummey vs §529 stacking order

In practice the stacks combine. A married couple who wants to move maximum wealth to a granddaughter in 2026 can:

  • Give $38,000 in cash outright (§2503(b) + §2513 split): fully excluded.
  • Contribute another $38,000 to a Crummey ILIT: fully excluded assuming Crummey notice is delivered and hanging-power drafting handles §2514(e).
  • Front-load $380,000 into a §529 plan for the granddaughter under §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging with §2513 split (5 × $38,000): fully excluded, but consumes the granddaughter's annual exclusion from both spouses for five years.

Total tax-free transfer in that year alone: $456,000 to one grandchild. Repeat across five grandchildren and a couple moves $2.28 million out of the estate in a single January without touching the §2010(c) $15M-per-person lifetime exemption.

§529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging — the $190,000 move

IRC §529(c)(5)(A) allows a donor to elect to treat a contribution to a §529 qualified tuition program as if made ratably over five calendar years for §2503(b) annual-exclusion purposes.[10] In 2026 that means:

  • Single donor: Front-load up to 5 × $19,000 = $95,000 per beneficiary into a §529 plan without any current-year taxable gift.
  • Married couple with §2513 election: Front-load up to 5 × $38,000 = $190,000 per beneficiary.
  • Contributions above the front-load cap in Year 1 are current-year taxable gifts (reduce §2010(c) exemption or generate tax if the exemption is exhausted).

Mechanics: check box B on Schedule A, Part 1 of Form 709 in the year of contribution. The donor is then locked out of making additional annual-exclusion gifts to that beneficiary for the balance of the five-year window, because the entire annual exclusion is already committed to the ratable-over-five treatment. If the donor makes additional gifts to the beneficiary during the window (say, a $10,000 birthday gift in Year 3), those additional gifts are fully taxable and consume lifetime exemption — no annual exclusion is available.[10]

The §529(c)(4)(C) death-during-window pullback

If the donor dies during the five-year averaging window, IRC §529(c)(4)(C) requires the unused portion of the accelerated gift to be included in the donor's gross estate. Practical implication: a donor who front-loads $95,000 in Year 1 and dies in Year 3 has "used up" $57,000 of exclusion ($19,000 × 3) but $38,000 ($19,000 × 2) is pulled back into the estate. This creates a subtle tension for elderly donors who might otherwise favor the five-year averaging — an outright completed gift into the §529 without the election, taking a Year-1 taxable gift of $76,000 ($95K − $19K annual exclusion) against lifetime exemption, may be preferable if death within five years is realistically foreseeable.

The §529 gift-tax hierarchy

A contribution to a §529 plan is a completed gift to the beneficiary under Treas. Reg. §25.2503-6, even though the account owner retains substantial control (including the right to withdraw funds for non-qualified purposes, subject to §529(c)(6) 10% penalty tax on earnings). This is a special rule for §529 — the account-owner's dominion and control does not defeat completed-gift status. The §2523(i)(2) noncitizen-spouse enlarged exclusion

When the donee spouse is not a U.S. citizen, IRC §2523(i)(1) denies the unlimited marital deduction under §2523(a). Congress substituted an enlarged annual exclusion in §2523(i)(2) — a special §2503(b)-style figure for spousal gifts to noncitizen spouses, adjusted for inflation under §2523(i)(2)(B). For 2026 the amount is $194,000 per Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.42(2).[11]

Important nuances:

  • The enlarged exclusion applies only to present-interest gifts to the noncitizen spouse. Future-interest gifts get zero.
  • The $194,000 is separate from and stacks with any gifts to other donees — a U.S. citizen donor can give $19,000 to each of 20 grandchildren AND $194,000 to a noncitizen spouse in the same year without any taxable gift.
  • The Qualified Domestic Trust (QDOT) framework under §2056A operates on the estate-tax side to preserve the marital deduction at death; the §2523(i)(2) annual exclusion is the gift-tax analogue that operates during life.

Noncitizen doesn't mean non-resident

The §2523(i) test turns on U.S. citizenship, not residency. A green-card holder married to a U.S. citizen is a noncitizen for §2523(i) purposes even though they file a joint income-tax return. Domicile matters for the donor's exposure (a nondomiciliary donor is taxed only on U.S.-situs gifts under §2501(a)(2)) but does not shift the §2523(i) mechanic for a U.S.-domiciled donor.

§2503(c) minor's trust exception

Separate from the general §2503(b) annual exclusion, IRC §2503(c) provides a distinct rule for gifts in trust to minors that would otherwise fail the present-interest test. A trust qualifies as a §2503(c) minor's trust — and its contributions get the annual exclusion — if:[12]

  1. The property and its income may be expended by the trustee for the benefit of the minor before age 21;
  2. Any portion not expended passes to the minor at age 21 (or to the minor's estate or under a general power of appointment held by the minor if the minor dies before 21);
  3. The trust has no restrictions that would prevent the minor from receiving the property outright at 21.

The §2503(c) trust is functionally an alternative to a UTMA/UGMA custodial account for older minors and to a Crummey ILIT for younger minors. It has a specific advantage: no Crummey notices required, so drafting is simpler. It has a specific disadvantage: the mandatory-distribution-at-21 rule can be uncomfortable for donors who don't trust the beneficiary at that age. A common workaround: give the beneficiary a right to withdraw at 21 that lapses if not exercised in a 30- to 60-day window, at which point the trust becomes a standard discretionary trust for the rest of the beneficiary's life (per Rev. Rul. 74-43).

§2503(e) direct-pay tuition and medical — stacks on top of §2503(b)

IRC §2503(e) is a separate, unlimited gift-tax exclusion that runs parallel to and stacks with §2503(b). It covers two categories:[13]

  • §2503(e)(2)(A) — Qualified tuition. Amounts paid on behalf of another individual as tuition to a qualifying educational institution under §170(b)(1)(A)(ii). The payment must be made directly to the institution, not to the student or a parent. Room, board, books, and supplies do not qualify.
  • §2503(e)(2)(B) — Qualified medical care. Amounts paid on behalf of another individual as medical care under §213(d), including insurance premiums. Payment must be made directly to the provider.

The stack: a grandparent can pay $80,000 in tuition directly to a private university, another $20,000 in medical bills directly to a hospital, and give the grandchild $19,000 in cash under §2503(b), all in the same year, without any current-year taxable gift and without filing a Form 709. That is $119,000 out of the estate for a single grandchild in one year via three stacked exclusions.

The "protective" Form 709 filing

When a valuation is uncertain (e.g., a gift of closely-held stock), many practitioners file Form 709 even when the annual exclusion clearly covers the gift, using the "adequate disclosure" rules under Treas. Reg. §301.6501(c)-1(f) to start the three-year §6501 statute of limitations running. Without adequate disclosure, the IRS has an unlimited period to challenge the valuation on the donor's later Form 706. On a $19,000 gift of appreciated startup stock that later matures, this protection is worth the filing cost.

The OBBBA $15M lifetime exemption interaction

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 2025) permanently raised the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount to $15 million per person effective January 1, 2026, indexed to Chained CPI from a 2025 base under §2010(c)(3)(C).[2] That figure — $15M in 2026, roughly $15.3M in 2027, and continuing to rise — is the lifetime cap. Every taxable gift a donor makes reduces the remaining §2010(c) exemption dollar-for-dollar.

The annual exclusion sits outside that mechanic. A gift within §2503(b) is not a "taxable gift" — it does not appear on Line 3 of Form 709 Schedule A (Part 1, Column H) as a "value of gifts less annual exclusion." A donor could give away $19,000 per year to each of 100 donees for 40 years — $76 million total — and still have the full $15M exemption available at death.

This is why estate planners obsess over the annual exclusion even for very high-net-worth clients. The OBBBA $15M exemption sounds enormous, but for a family with, say, $80M in taxable estate assets, the exemption covers less than 20% of the estate. The other $65M is subject to the 40% top rate under §2001 — $26M in estate tax. Every $19,000 gift moved out annually is $7,600 of estate tax saved (40% × $19,000). Twenty donees for twenty years is $152,000/year in tax savings, or $3.04M over the horizon, without touching lifetime exemption at all.

The GST §2642(c) alignment

The generation-skipping transfer tax under IRC Chapter 13 imposes an additional 40% flat tax on transfers to skip persons (grandchildren, great-grandchildren, unrelated persons more than 37.5 years younger). §2642(c) provides that direct-skip gifts that qualify for the §2503(b) or §2503(e) exclusions are also zero-inclusion-ratio for GST purposes — meaning zero GST tax and no automatic §2632(b) exemption allocation. A grandparent giving $19,000 in cash to a grandchild in 2026 pays no gift tax, no GST tax, and consumes no §2631(c) GST exemption. The gift moves through both tax layers with zero tax cost.[14]

The alignment breaks for trust gifts. A $19,000 contribution to a dynasty trust with Crummey powers qualifies for §2503(b) annual exclusion for gift-tax purposes but does not automatically qualify for §2642(c) zero-inclusion-ratio treatment because §2642(c)(2) imposes additional trust-level requirements (the trust must qualify as a §2503(c) minor's trust or the transferred property must be includible in the beneficiary's gross estate at death). Missing the §2642(c) alignment requires allocating GST exemption on Form 709 Schedule D to avoid a nonzero inclusion ratio on later distributions.

State gift-tax exposure — Connecticut is the outlier

Only Connecticut imposes a state-level gift tax in 2026. Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-640 taxes lifetime gifts by Connecticut residents (and nonresidents' gifts of Connecticut-situs real or tangible personal property) using the same unified federal-conforming mechanic on the state side.[15] Key 2026 figures:

  • Connecticut annual exclusion: Tracks the federal §2503(b) figure of $19,000. Available for present-interest gifts to any donee.
  • Connecticut lifetime exemption: Unified with the estate tax at $13.99 million for 2026 under Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-391(g) (revised 2023). Below the federal $15M but above every other state.
  • Rate: Flat 12% on gifts exceeding the exemption.
  • Return: Form CT-706/709 due at the same time as the federal Form 709.

Every other U.S. jurisdiction imposes no state-level gift tax. Connecticut retains the tax primarily to close off a straightforward avoidance strategy: without a state gift tax, a wealthy Connecticut resident could move all appreciable assets by lifetime gift and defeat the state estate tax. Tennessee (repealed 2012), Louisiana (2008), and North Carolina (2009) all abandoned state gift taxes in the last two decades, following a broader trend toward federal conformity or outright repeal at the state transfer-tax level.

Notice what they did not do: they never touched lifetime exemption. They filed Form 709 in Year 1 solely because of the §2513 election and the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging election. In Years 2–5 they filed Form 709 solely because of the §2513 election. Every dollar of the $15M-per-person lifetime exemption is still available at death for the remainder of the $52M estate. The couple's remaining taxable estate at death — projected around $43.5M — still faces $28M above the $30M combined exemption, meaning $11.2M in federal estate tax. Without the annual-exclusion plan, that number would have been $14.6M — the $3.4M difference is the reward for a mechanic that requires no advanced planning at all.

The invisible power of stack + repeat

The annual exclusion is boring, mechanical, and unglamorous. It is also the single largest wealth-transfer channel in the U.S. tax code by aggregate dollar flow. Every high-net-worth family should be running the full stack (§2503(b) + §2513 + §529 + §2503(e)) every January 1, without exception, for as long as the donors are able.

Six recurring §2503(b) mistakes

  1. Waiting until December. An annual-exclusion gift must be complete by December 31. A check written December 30 but not deposited until January 3 is a Year 2 gift under Rev. Rul. 96-56 (the check-cashing rule). Best practice: mail checks by December 15, or use wire transfers.
  2. Skipping the Crummey notice. A trustee who forgets to send Crummey notices in a given year loses the annual exclusion for that year's contribution to the trust. The IRS has argued (with mixed success) that habitual failure to send notices proves the withdrawal rights are illusory. Best practice: document notices in the trust file every year, even when the beneficiaries would never exercise them.
  3. Over-relying on §529 front-loading. The five-year lockout means additional gifts to the same beneficiary during the window are fully taxable. Donors who like to give birthday and holiday cash to the same grandchildren should not front-load — regular $19,000-per-year contributions preserve the flexibility.
  4. Ignoring §2514(e) hanging powers. ILIT contributions above $5,000 per beneficiary create §2514 exposure at each beneficiary's lapse. Failing to design hanging powers can turn a beneficiary into an unintended donor for gift-tax purposes.
  5. Confusing §2503(e) direct-pay for "loans." The tuition or medical payment must be made directly to the institution or provider. Wiring $60,000 to the student's checking account so they can pay tuition themselves is a §2503(b)-only gift capped at $19,000; the remaining $41,000 is a taxable gift.
  6. Failing to file for §2513. The gift-splitting election requires a Form 709. Couples who intend to split but never file end up with only one $19,000 exclusion per donee, not two, if the IRS later audits. Best practice: file Form 709 in any year §2513 is intended, even when the exclusions alone would waive the filing.

Annual-exclusion action checklist

  1. Build the donee list before January 1. Children, children-in-law, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, nieces/nephews, close friends — every real name gets $19,000 (or $38,000 with §2513). Blank slots waste an entire year of exclusion.
  2. Front-load §529s for the youngest generation. A married couple with four young grandchildren can move $760,000 into §529s under §529(c)(5)(A) with §2513 in one January. Repeat every five years.
  3. Route tuition and medical directly to the institution or provider. §2503(e) is unlimited and stacks with §2503(b). Ask the school to accept wire transfers from the grandparents' account.
  4. Send Crummey notices immediately after any ILIT contribution. Set a January calendar reminder. Document in the trust file.
  5. Elect §2513 gift-splitting even in "no-tax" years. The election establishes the pattern for the IRS and is cheap protection.
  6. File a "protective" Form 709 for any illiquid or hard-to-value asset gift. Adequate disclosure starts the §6501 three-year statute of limitations running.
  7. Coordinate with the §2010(c) portability election on the first-decedent's Form 706. DSUE portability preserves the deceased spouse's unused $15M for the survivor — critical when annual-exclusion gifting hasn't drained the estate below $30M combined. See our §2010(c) DSUE portability election deep-dive.
  8. Update the plan every October. The IRS publishes the following year's Rev. Proc. inflation adjustments in October (Rev. Proc. 2025-32 for 2026 was released October 9, 2025). If the annual exclusion bumps from $19,000 to $20,000, every donee's Year-1 allocation for the new year needs to move up.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 2026 annual gift-tax exclusion under IRC §2503(b)?

$19,000 per donee per calendar year for gifts to any person other than a noncitizen spouse. Set by Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section 4.42(1), applying the §2503(b)(2) inflation-adjustment mechanic to the $10,000 base. It is the same figure as 2025 because the CPI-U through the August 2025 measurement period did not clear the next $1,000 rounding step under §2503(b)(2)(A). Married couples can double it to $38,000 per donee by electing §2513 gift-splitting on a timely-filed Form 709.

Does a §2503(b) gift consume any of the OBBBA $15 million lifetime exemption?

No. A completed gift that fits inside the $19,000 annual exclusion is not a taxable gift under §2503(b)(1). It does not appear on Form 709 as a taxable transfer, does not require a return at all if it is the only gift to that donee for the year, and does not reduce the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount (which is $15M per person in 2026 after OBBBA §70106, Public Law 119-21). Gifts above the exclusion amount are taxable gifts that reduce the lifetime exemption dollar-for-dollar until it is exhausted, at which point the 40% top gift-tax rate under §2502 applies.

What is the "present interest" requirement and why do most trust gifts fail it?

§2503(b)(1) allows the exclusion only for gifts of a present interest — "an unrestricted right to the immediate use, possession, or enjoyment of property or the income from property" under Treas. Reg. §25.2503-3(b). A gift in trust that only permits future distributions is a future interest and gets zero exclusion. The Crummey withdrawal power (Crummey v. Commissioner, 397 F.2d 82 (9th Cir. 1968)) solves this by giving each beneficiary a temporary, non-cumulative right to withdraw the contribution; the IRS accepted the doctrine in Rev. Rul. 73-405 and extended it to contingent beneficiaries in Estate of Cristofani v. Commissioner, 97 T.C. 74 (1991).

How does §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging work in 2026?

A donor may elect to treat a contribution to a §529 plan as if it were made ratably over five years for §2503(b) annual-exclusion purposes. In 2026 that means a single donor can front-load $95,000 (5 × $19,000) per beneficiary into a §529 without any of it becoming a taxable gift, and a married couple electing §2513 gift-splitting can front-load $190,000 per beneficiary. The election is made on Form 709 by checking box B on Schedule A, Part 1. If the donor dies during the five-year window, the unused portion is pulled back into the gross estate under §529(c)(4)(C).

What is the 2026 annual exclusion for a gift to a noncitizen spouse?

$194,000 in 2026 under IRC §2523(i)(2), per Rev. Proc. 2025-32 section 4.42(2). This higher figure exists because §2523(i) denies the unlimited marital deduction under §2523(a) when the donee spouse is not a U.S. citizen — the enlarged annual exclusion is the substitute. The exclusion applies only to present-interest gifts to the noncitizen spouse and requires the same Treas. Reg. §25.2503-3 present-interest analysis as any other §2503(b) gift.

Do I have to file Form 709 for a $19,000 gift?

No, if the gift qualifies for the annual exclusion (present-interest gift under $19,000 to a single donee) and you are not electing §2513 gift-splitting. Form 709 is triggered by: (1) a gift to any single donee exceeding $19,000; (2) any gift of a future interest, regardless of amount; (3) a §2513 gift-splitting election, even when the individual gifts fit inside the exclusion; (4) a §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging election; and (5) any generation-skipping transfer, per Form 709 Instructions (2025 tax year). The 2026 Form 709 is due April 15, 2027 for gifts made during calendar year 2026.

Does a state gift tax exist in 2026?

Connecticut is the only U.S. jurisdiction with a state-level gift tax as of 2026, under Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-640. Connecticut's exemption is unified with its estate tax at $13.99 million for 2026 and rises with the federal amount, and its state annual exclusion tracks the federal §2503(b) figure of $19,000. All other states impose no gift tax at all — a change from decades past when Tennessee, Louisiana, and North Carolina all had state gift taxes (Tennessee repealed 2012, Louisiana 2008, North Carolina 2009).

What is the difference between §2503(b) annual exclusion and §2503(e) direct-pay exclusion?

§2503(b) is a $19,000-per-donee annual cap for any purpose. §2503(e) is a separate, unlimited exclusion that applies only to (a) tuition paid directly to a §170(b)(1)(A)(ii) educational institution, and (b) medical care paid directly to the provider under §213(d). They stack: a grandparent can pay $80,000 in college tuition directly to Harvard under §2503(e) and additionally give the grandchild $19,000 in cash under §2503(b) in the same year without either transfer becoming a taxable gift. The §2503(e) exclusion has no filing requirement regardless of amount.
