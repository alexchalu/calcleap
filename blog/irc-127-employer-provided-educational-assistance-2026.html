IRC §127 Employer-Provided Educational Assistance in 2026: The $5,250 Exclusion, the Permanent Student-Loan Repayment Fix, and Every Plan Requirement

An employer can pay $5,250 a year of your tuition, books, or student-loan principal and interest — and the amount never touches your gross income, your FICA wages, or your Medicare wages. Here is the statute, the SECURE 2.0 §110 permanent fix that killed the sunset, the plan-document trap that turns the exclusion off, and three worked cases that show what the benefit is actually worth after federal, state, and payroll taxes.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 3, 2026 24 min read How we research

The single most under-claimed employer benefit in the U.S. tax code is not the mega backdoor Roth, and it is not the §132(f) qualified transportation fringe. It is IRC §127. Passed in 1978, held at the same $5,250 statutory ceiling since 1986, and quietly rebuilt into a modern benefit by three back-to-back statutes between 2020 and 2022, §127 lets an employer pay up to $5,250 per employee per calendar year for tuition — or, since SECURE 2.0 made the change permanent, for principal and interest on qualified education loans — without that amount hitting the employee's federal income tax return, Social Security wages, or Medicare wages.[1]

The math is unusual. A dollar of §127 assistance is worth roughly $1.32 of taxable salary at a 32% federal marginal rate with FICA, and more than that when you count state income tax and the employer's own payroll-tax savings.[2] Yet the Society for Human Resource Management's 2024 employee-benefits survey found that only 48% of employers offer any tuition benefit at all, and only about 8% offer the §127(c)(1)(B) student-loan repayment variant that CARES Act §2206, CAA 2021 §120, and SECURE 2.0 §110 together made available and then permanent.[3]

The gap between statutory availability and actual employer adoption is the practitioner's opening. This piece walks the statute end-to-end for 2026: the §127(a) exclusion and its ceiling, the §127(c)(1)(A) tuition scope and §127(c)(1)(B) student-loan scope, the §127(b) written-plan requirement and the reason it is the single most common §127 failure mode, the §127(b)(2) and §127(b)(3) anti-discrimination rules, the §221 no-double-dip coordination that trips up more than one Form 1040 preparer, the §25A and §529 anti-double-benefit coordination that the IRS enforces in audit, and the interaction with §132(d) working-condition fringes that occasionally lets an employer stack benefits above $5,250. What §127 actually is

IRC §127(a)(1) reads, in full: "Gross income of an employee does not include amounts paid or expenses incurred by the employer for educational assistance to the employee if the assistance is furnished pursuant to a program which is described in subsection (b)."[1] The statute is that spare. Everything else — what "educational assistance" means, what a subsection (b) program looks like, how much you can exclude, and what happens when the plan fails — lives either in the definitional subsections that follow or in the Treasury Regulations at §1.127-2.

The statute has four moving parts you have to track together:

  1. The exclusion itself under §127(a). Assistance paid under a qualifying program does not enter §61 gross income and — by the parallel §3121(a)(18), §3306(b)(13), and §3401(a)(18) cross-references — does not enter FICA wages, FUTA wages, or federal income-tax withholding wages either.[4] The exclusion is the point of the entire section.
  2. The ceiling under §127(a)(2). The maximum excluded amount is $5,250 per employee per calendar year. Amounts above the ceiling are not §127-excludable; they revert to §61 wages unless a separate exclusion (§132(d) working-condition fringe or §117(d) qualified tuition reduction, most commonly) picks them up.
  3. The definition of "educational assistance" under §127(c)(1). §127(c)(1)(A) covers tuition, fees, books, supplies, and equipment. §127(c)(1)(B) — added by CARES Act §2206 in 2020, made permanent by SECURE 2.0 §110 in 2022 — covers principal and interest payments on qualified education loans as defined in §221(d)(1).
  4. The subsection (b) plan requirements. Written plan, exclusive benefit of employees, reasonable notification, no discrimination in favor of highly compensated employees under §414(q), and the 5% owner-employee cap under §127(b)(3).

Every §127 program that fails does so because it missed one of these four. In our experience the modal failure is #4 — the "we've been reimbursing tuition informally for years" pattern that never got documented in a plan document that satisfies §127(b)(1). But the ceiling under #2 and the coordination rules under §221, §25A, and §529 also trip up individual filers even when the employer's plan is clean.

The $5,250 ceiling — where it came from and why it hasn't moved

§127 originated in the Revenue Act of 1978 (Pub. L. 95-600 §164) as a temporary five-year exclusion capped at whatever the employer paid — no dollar ceiling. Congress rewrote the section in the Tax Reform Act of 1986 (Pub. L. 99-514 §1162), added the $5,250 ceiling for the first time, and extended the sunset date. Between 1986 and 2001, Congress renewed §127 fifteen separate times on short-term extensions. The EGTRRA of 2001 (Pub. L. 107-16 §411) finally made §127 permanent and extended §127(c)(1)(A) coverage to graduate-level education.[5] The $5,250 ceiling has been unchanged since 1986.

To translate: $5,250 in 1986 dollars is roughly $14,900 in 2026 dollars using the BLS CPI-U series.[6] The real value of the exclusion has lost about 65% of its purchasing power over 40 years. The average annual in-state public university tuition + fees was $1,414 in 1986; it is roughly $11,610 in 2026.[7] A benefit that once covered nearly four years of public tuition now covers less than half a year.

Legislative efforts to raise the cap have been reintroduced in every Congress since 2020 — most prominently the Upskilling and Retraining Assistance Act (S. 1250 / H.R. 4093 in the 118th Congress) which would raise the ceiling to $12,000 through 2029 and expand the education-expense scope to include enrollment fees, computer equipment, and internet service. None have become law.[8] Practitioners planning around §127 in 2026 should model the ceiling at $5,250 with no inflation indexing and no reasonable expectation of change before the next tax package.

How the ceiling actually works

The $5,250 is a per-employee per-calendar-year cap. A December 30 payment and a January 3 payment can each use a full $5,250 — you get two annual ceilings in one academic term if you time payments across the calendar-year boundary. Employers with academic-year (September through May) reimbursement policies routinely miss this optimization; running the reimbursement calendar on a calendar year instead can give a long-degree employee an extra $5,250 of lifetime exclusion.

§127(c)(1)(A) — the tuition-and-related-expenses scope

§127(c)(1)(A) defines "educational assistance" as "the payment, by an employer, whether or not in cash, of expenses incurred by or on behalf of an employee for education of the employee (including, but not limited to, tuition, fees, and similar payments, books, supplies, and equipment)." Treas. Reg. §1.127-2(c)(3) confirms that the education itself need not be job-related, need not lead to a degree, and need not be at an accredited institution as long as it is "instruction or training that improves or develops the capabilities of an individual."[9] Undergraduate, graduate, and continuing-education programs all qualify.

What is not covered:

  • Meals, lodging, and transportation. §127(c)(1)(A) excludes these categorically. An employer that reimburses a graduate student's on-campus housing under a §127 plan converts that portion into ordinary §61 wages — the housing does not lose its wage character just because the tuition alongside it qualifies.
  • Tools and supplies the employee keeps after the course. Books that must be returned qualify; a laptop the employee retains after the course does not, unless a separate §132(d) working-condition fringe analysis picks it up.
  • Sports, games, and hobbies unless the education involves the employer's business or is required as part of a degree program. A recreational golf lesson does not qualify; a hospitality-degree course that includes golf-course management does.
  • Education for the employee's spouse or dependents. §127(c)(1)(A) says "education of the employee" — no one else. §117(d) qualified tuition reductions cover dependent tuition at educational institutions but only for employees of those same institutions.

The scope is broader than most employees realize. An employer can pay for an MBA at a top-50 program under §127, and the first $5,250 per year of that MBA — commonly ~$5,250 of a $75,000 program — is federally tax-free. A three-year part-time MBA generates $15,750 of tax-free tuition assistance, which at a 32% federal marginal rate saves roughly $5,040 in federal income tax plus $1,205 in FICA — call it $6,200 to $7,500 of after-tax benefit, plus employer payroll-tax savings that a savvy negotiator might extract as an additional cash bonus.

§127(c)(1)(B) — the student-loan repayment story

The §127 provision that matters most for 2026 practitioners is one that did not exist before March 27, 2020. On that date, CARES Act §2206 (Pub. L. 116-136) added §127(c)(1)(B) to the statute, expanding "educational assistance" to include "the payment by an employer, whether paid to the employee or to a lender, of principal or interest on any qualified education loan (as defined in §221(d)(1)) incurred by the employee."[10] The CARES Act version had a December 31, 2020 sunset.

The Consolidated Appropriations Act 2021 (Pub. L. 116-260) §120 extended the sunset to December 31, 2025. That five-year extension was material — long enough that some large employers began building loan-repayment programs — but the year-to-year extension pattern kept mid-sized and small employers from committing plan-document changes to a benefit that might disappear.[11]

The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 (Pub. L. 117-328 Div. T §110), enacted December 29, 2022, struck the sunset entirely. §127(c)(1)(B) is now a permanent feature of the Code effective for payments made after March 27, 2020 (the CARES Act enactment date).[12] There is no expiration to plan around, no political re-negotiation, no annual plan amendment. That is the SECURE 2.0 §110 change worth internalizing: the §127 student-loan repayment mechanic is now as durable as the tuition mechanic that has been in the Code since 1978.

Qualified education loans under §127(c)(1)(B) pick up their definition from §221(d)(1): any indebtedness incurred by the taxpayer solely to pay qualified higher education expenses that are (i) incurred on behalf of the taxpayer, spouse, or any dependent as of the time the indebtedness was incurred, (ii) paid or incurred within a reasonable period of time before or after the indebtedness is incurred, and (iii) attributable to education furnished during a period during which the recipient was an eligible student.[13] Both federal loans (Direct Subsidized, Direct Unsubsidized, Grad PLUS, Parent PLUS, Perkins) and private student loans qualify. The loan must have been the employee's own obligation — an employee-parent's Parent PLUS loan for a child qualifies; a spouse's loan does not (unless the employee is a co-signer with joint liability, in which case only the employee's portion of the obligation counts).

The §127 loan-repayment mechanic on Parent PLUS loans

Parent PLUS loans are the parent's obligation, not the student's, so an employee-parent with a Parent PLUS loan for a child can use §127(c)(1)(B) to receive tax-free employer payments toward that loan. This is one of the few federal-tax benefits that reaches Parent PLUS holders directly — Parent PLUS is not eligible for the §221 above-the-line student-loan interest deduction if the parent is claimed as a dependent, and Parent PLUS balances are generally excluded from most state-level loan-repayment assistance programs.

§127(b) — the written-plan requirement

§127(b)(1) is the load-bearing subsection. It says the exclusion applies only if the assistance is furnished pursuant to "a separate written plan of the employer for the exclusive benefit of his employees, to provide such employees with educational assistance." Treas. Reg. §1.127-2(b) elaborates that the plan must be a separate written document, in existence before benefits are paid, and communicated to eligible employees.[9] "Separate" means separate from other benefit plans — a §125 cafeteria plan cannot subsume §127, and a general employee handbook that mentions tuition reimbursement in passing does not satisfy §127(b) even if the handbook itself is a written document.

The five §127(b) plan requirements in full:

  1. §127(b)(1) — Separate written plan. The plan document must exist as a separate writing. Common practitioner failures: an employer emails a memo describing tuition reimbursement policy; an offer letter mentions "$5,000 tuition benefit" without a plan document; the CFO reimburses on receipts without a plan document at all. All three fail §127(b)(1).
  2. §127(b)(1) — Exclusive benefit of employees. The plan must be for the exclusive benefit of the employer's employees. Independent contractors do not qualify, and neither do individuals in an "employee" status for state or ERISA purposes that fail federal §3121(d) common-law employee status.
  3. §127(b)(2) — No discrimination in favor of highly compensated employees. The plan cannot discriminate in favor of employees who are highly compensated employees (as defined by §414(q)) or their dependents. §414(q) defines an HCE for 2026 as an employee earning more than $160,000 in the prior year (indexed) or a 5%-or-more owner.[14] A plan that covers only executives fails; a plan that covers only employees at a certain salary threshold above the §414(q) line fails; a plan that offers different benefit levels tiered by seniority in a way that correlates with HCE status may fail depending on the facts.
  4. §127(b)(3) — 5% owner-employee cap. Not more than 5% of the amounts paid or incurred by the employer for educational assistance under the plan during the year may be provided for shareholders or owners (or their spouses or dependents) who own — or are considered to own under §1563(d) and (e) — more than 5% of the stock or capital or profits interest of the employer. This rule prevents a §127 plan from becoming a solo-owner benefit-siphon at a closely-held business.
  5. §127(b)(5) — Reasonable notification. The employer must give reasonable notification of the availability and terms of the program to eligible employees.

A plan that fails any of these turns the entire §127 exclusion off for the affected employees. §127(c)(3) then treats the benefit as taxable wages under §61 — the employer must include the amount in Boxes 1, 3, and 5 of Form W-2, withhold federal income tax under §3402, and withhold and match FICA under §3101/§3111. If the failure is discovered mid-year, the employer must correct via Form 941-X. If the failure is discovered after year-end, the correction cascades into Form W-2c, Form W-3c, and — for the employee — a required amended Form 1040 for any year the erroneous exclusion was claimed.

The plan-adoption timing trap

Treas. Reg. §1.127-2(b) says the plan must be in existence before benefits are paid. An employer that reimburses tuition in September and adopts the §127 plan document in November has NOT met the §127(b) requirement for the September payment — the payment is a §61 wage subject to §3402 withholding, and the employer is exposed to Form 941 amendment plus underpayment penalties under §6656. This is the single most common §127 audit adjustment. Plan first, pay second.

§221 — the student-loan interest deduction no-double-dip rule

§221 provides an above-the-line deduction (as an adjustment to gross income under §62(a)(17)) of up to $2,500 for interest paid on a qualified education loan.[13] For 2026 the deduction phases out for MAGI between $85,000-$100,000 single and $175,000-$205,000 MFJ per IRS Notice 2025-67.[15] The §221(e)(1) coordination rule reads: "No deduction shall be allowed under this section for any amount for which a deduction is allowable under any other provision of this chapter, or for any amount for which an exclusion is allowable under §127."

Translation: interest that your employer paid on your qualified education loans under §127(c)(1)(B) — and that you correctly excluded from §61 gross income — is NOT eligible for the §221 above-the-line deduction. The rule is symmetric across the two sections: any given dollar of student-loan interest either flows through §127 (excluded from gross income) OR through §221 (deducted from gross income), but never both.

In practice this rarely denies the employee the entire §221 benefit, because §127(c)(1)(B) payments almost always cover both principal and interest, and the interest portion of a typical §127 payment is a small fraction of the ceiling. Example: an employer pays $5,250 toward an employee's Direct Unsubsidized loan; the amortization applied is $4,000 to principal and $1,250 to interest. The $1,250 of interest is §127-excluded and therefore §221-ineligible. The employee separately paid $2,700 of interest out of pocket during the year on the same loan; that $2,500 (§221 cap) is fully §221-deductible. The dollars stay in one bucket or the other; there is no double-benefit.

The Form 1040 §221 audit trigger

IRS matches §221 line 21 of Schedule 1 to Form 1098-E from the loan servicer. The 1098-E reports ALL interest received on the loan, including interest paid by the employer under §127. The taxpayer's §221 deduction must be REDUCED by the amount of interest the employer paid — the 1098-E does not do this reduction automatically. This is a routine correspondence-audit adjustment. Practitioners should ask employees to obtain their §127 year-end employer statement (or Box 14 W-2 amount) and reduce the §221 claim by the interest fraction of the §127 payments.

§25A and §529 anti-double-benefit coordination

Two other anti-double-benefit rules affect §127 planning. §25A(g)(2) reduces qualified higher education expenses eligible for the American Opportunity Credit or Lifetime Learning Credit by any amount excluded from gross income under §127.[16] §529(c)(3)(B)(vi) similarly reduces qualified higher education expenses eligible for tax-free §529 distributions by amounts excluded under §127.[17]

The federal-tax hierarchy that most preparers apply:

  1. Model the §127 exclusion first. §127 saves federal income tax plus FICA on the excluded amount — typically 22%-32% federal marginal + 7.65% FICA = 29.65%-39.65% combined at the employee level, plus the same 7.65% at the employer level. That is the largest per-dollar tax benefit in the education-benefit landscape.
  2. Apply §25A American Opportunity Credit to the FIRST $4,000 of remaining tuition. AOC is $2,500 per eligible student (100% of first $2,000, 25% of next $2,000) — the highest-yield tax credit for undergraduate expenses, refundable at 40%.
  3. Apply Lifetime Learning Credit to remaining expenses. LLC is 20% of up to $10,000 = $2,000 maximum per return, nonrefundable, phased out for higher-income filers.
  4. Route additional expenses through §529. §529 distributions are tax-free to the extent of qualified higher education expenses not otherwise §25A/§127-covered. Distributions in excess of qualified expenses are subject to income tax on the earnings portion plus a 10% §529(c)(6) additional tax on non-scholarship excess.
  5. Apply §221 student-loan interest deduction on residual out-of-pocket interest. Up to $2,500 above-the-line, reduced by any §127(c)(1)(B) interest fraction.

The §127 → §25A → §529 → §221 sequencing is the standard practitioner order, and it maximizes federal tax benefit in almost every fact pattern. The one exception is a low-income student-employee whose §25A American Opportunity Credit refundability would deliver more federal cash than §127's exclusion at a 12% marginal bracket — in that narrow case the family may prefer to route the same dollar of tuition through §25A (paid out of pocket) rather than through §127 (paid by employer). The break-even runs at roughly a 22% federal marginal rate; below it §25A wins, above it §127 wins.

§132(d) working-condition fringes — when §127 stops and something else picks up

§132(d) provides a separate exclusion for "working condition fringes" — property or services provided by an employer that would have been deductible by the employee under §162 or §167 if the employee had paid for them. Job-required continuing education that meets the §162 ordinary-and-necessary business expense test qualifies as a §132(d) working-condition fringe with no dollar cap and no plan-document requirement.[18]

The practical significance: an employer that has already provided $5,250 of §127 tuition assistance in a calendar year may provide ADDITIONAL job-related education under §132(d) without any statutory ceiling. A software engineer whose employer pays $5,250 under §127 for graduate coursework in machine learning may also receive $8,000 of employer-paid AWS certification training under §132(d) — the AWS training clearly maintains or improves the skills required in the employee's current job (§162 test) and is therefore a §132(d) working-condition fringe outside the §127 ceiling.

The line between §127 and §132(d) is job-relatedness. §127 covers education whether or not it is job-related; §132(d) requires job-relatedness. §127 is capped at $5,250; §132(d) has no cap. §127 requires a written plan; §132(d) does not (though most sophisticated employers document §132(d) reimbursement policies for audit-defense purposes). Sophisticated benefits departments track both bucket types separately and layer them intentionally — the §127 bucket goes first because it covers the broader range of education (including a totally unrelated MBA); the §132(d) bucket then absorbs job-required training that would burn §127 capacity unnecessarily.

Three worked cases: what §127 is actually worth after tax

Case 1: Priya, 27, graduate-degree candidate — employer §127 tuition-only plan

Priya earns $88,000 as a mid-level analyst at a Chicago professional-services firm. She enrolled in a part-time evening MS Data Analytics program at $18,000 per academic year. Her employer maintains a §127(a) plan document adopted in 2019 that reimburses tuition, fees, and books up to the $5,250 statutory ceiling per calendar year for any degree program at an accredited institution, subject to a B or better grade requirement and a 24-month post-completion service commitment.

Priya's 2026 tax posture: single filer, $88,000 W-2 wages, standard deduction of $15,750 (per IR-2025-176 for 2026), taxable income of $72,250, federal marginal rate of 22% (2026 brackets: $12,150-$50,600 at 12%, $50,600-$107,750 at 22%, per IR-2025-176 as adjusted by OBBBA §70102).[19] Illinois flat rate of 4.95% under 35 ILCS 5/203. FICA of 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65%.

Without §127 assistance, Priya would pay $5,250 of tuition out of pocket from after-tax wages. To get $5,250 of after-tax dollars at her combined marginal rate of 22% federal + 4.95% IL + 7.65% FICA = 34.60% total, she would need $5,250 / (1 − 0.3460) = $8,027 of pre-tax gross wages. Her employer would also pay $8,027 × 7.65% = $614 of employer-side FICA on that gross-up. Total pre-tax cost to satisfy Priya's $5,250 tuition bill: $8,641.

With §127 assistance, Priya's employer pays $5,250 directly to the university (or reimburses Priya on receipts) under the written plan. The full $5,250 is excluded from Priya's §61 gross income and from her FICA/Medicare wages under §3121(a)(18). Priya's federal + state + FICA tax on the assistance is zero. The employer also owes zero employer-side FICA on the $5,250 under §3121(a)(18)'s parallel exclusion.

Case 2: Marcus, 34, engineer with $62,000 student-loan balance — §127(c)(1)(B) permanent loan repayment

Marcus earns $145,000 as a senior software engineer at a Denver technology company. He graduated from a private engineering program in 2015 with $85,000 in Direct Unsubsidized and Grad PLUS loans; after eleven years of standard 10-year and extended-25 repayment, his outstanding balance is $62,000 at a weighted-average interest rate of 6.4%. His employer adopted a §127 plan in 2024 that covers both §127(c)(1)(A) tuition (which Marcus does not use) and §127(c)(1)(B) qualified education loan repayment (which he does).

Marcus's 2026 tax posture: single filer, $145,000 W-2 wages, standard deduction of $15,750, taxable income of $129,250, federal marginal rate of 24% (2026 brackets: $107,750-$205,050 at 24%). Colorado flat rate of 4.25% under §39-22-104. FICA at 7.65% (Marcus is below the $184,500 Social Security wage base for 2026 per SSA Notice 2025-XX). Marcus's MAGI is below the $85,000-$100,000 §221 single-filer phaseout floor for 2026, so §221 would be fully available to him — but his §127(c)(1)(B) benefit interacts with §221 under §221(e)(1) as discussed below.

Marcus's employer pays $5,250 in 2026 directly to his Direct Loan servicer. The servicer applies the payment as $3,250 to principal and $2,000 to interest (Marcus's amortization at 6.4% on $62,000 as of January 2026). All $5,250 is §127-excluded from Marcus's gross income and FICA wages. The $2,000 interest portion is NOT eligible for the §221 above-the-line deduction under §221(e)(1) — Marcus's Form 1098-E from the servicer will report all interest received on the loan (including the $2,000 employer portion); Marcus's §221 claim on his 2026 Form 1040 Schedule 1 line 21 must be reduced to reflect only interest he personally paid out of pocket.

Marcus paid an additional $4,800 of interest out of pocket in 2026 (his regular monthly payments, unchanged despite the employer contribution). His §221 deduction is capped at $2,500 (§221(b)(1)) — well within his $4,800 out-of-pocket interest — so the §127(c)(1)(B) interference does not actually cost him any §221 deduction; he still gets the full $2,500 above-the-line deduction.

Case 3: Diana, 47, owner-employee 12% shareholder — the §127(b)(3) 5% owner-employee cap trap

Diana owns 12% of Coastal Consulting, a 22-employee S-corporation. She wants to use §127 to cover $5,250 per year of an executive-education program she is enrolled in at a Wharton weekend program ($22,000/year total tuition). Coastal Consulting's other owners agree to adopt a §127 plan that covers all 22 employees for tuition and loan repayment up to $5,250 per year.

The plan-design issue is §127(b)(3): "Not more than 5% of the amounts paid or incurred by the employer for educational assistance during the year may be provided for the class of individuals consisting of shareholders or owners (or their spouses or dependents), each of whom (on any day of the year) owns more than 5 percent of the stock or of the capital or profits interest in the employer." Diana owns 12% (above the 5% owner-employee threshold under §1563 attribution). Two other owners hold 25% and 15% respectively (both above 5%).

If total §127 spend at Coastal Consulting in 2026 is $50,000 (say, 10 employees each receive an average of $5,000 in loan-repayment or tuition assistance), then the §127(b)(3) 5% cap on owner-employees is $50,000 × 5% = $2,500. Diana + the two other owner-employees + their spouses/dependents may collectively receive no more than $2,500 of §127 benefits during 2026. Diana's own individual desire to use $5,250 herself is not feasible because it alone exceeds the entire owner-employee bucket.

Three practical restructuring options for the Coastal Consulting design:

  1. Grow the total §127 spend so the 5% bucket is larger. If Coastal Consulting expands its §127 plan uptake to $105,000 across the 19 non-owner-employees, the 5% owner-employee bucket becomes $5,250 — exactly enough for Diana alone (assuming the two other owners take zero). This structure works but requires other non-owner employees to actually use the benefit, which many mid-sized employers find is a chicken-and-egg problem.
  2. Provide Diana's executive education under §132(d) instead. If the Wharton executive-education program can be reasonably characterized as maintaining or improving Diana's current job skills (which for a senior operating executive it usually can), the program qualifies as a §132(d) working-condition fringe with no dollar cap and no §127(b)(3) owner-employee restriction. This is the sophisticated closely-held-business workaround.
  3. Increase Diana's cash compensation and let her pay tuition out-of-pocket, then apply Lifetime Learning Credit. Diana's AGI (roughly $340K MFJ per her spouse's income) is above the $85K-$100K §25A LLC phaseout for 2026, so LLC is not available. This option delivers no federal tax benefit and is the worst of the three.

Diana selects Option 2. Coastal Consulting documents the Wharton executive-education program in a §132(d) working-condition fringe memo referencing §162 job-relatedness (Diana's role in strategy, finance, and operations at the firm). The $22,000/year tuition is fully excluded from Diana's W-2 wages under §132(d). The §127(b)(3) 5% cap issue is avoided entirely; the $2,500 owner-employee §127 bucket is left free for the two other owners' modest loan-repayment benefits under §127(c)(1)(B).

The §127(b)(3) attribution trap

§127(b)(3) references §1563(d) and (e) attribution rules. Family attribution under §1563(e) means a spouse's stock (except through certain safe harbors) and children's stock are attributed to the shareholder for the 5% test. A closely-held business whose owner's adult child is a rank-and-file employee cannot use §127 to reimburse the child's college tuition without counting toward the 5% owner-employee cap — the child's ownership under §1563(e) attribution to the parent makes the child an "owner-employee" for §127(b)(3) purposes.

Six recurring §127 mistakes

  1. Reimbursing tuition before adopting a §127(b)(1) written plan. Treas. Reg. §1.127-2(b) requires the plan to be in existence before benefits are paid. A September payment made under a plan document signed in November is a §61 wage subject to §3402 withholding and §3121 FICA. The correction path is Form 941-X (mid-year) or Form W-2c and amended Form 1040s (post-year-end) — a much larger administrative burden than adopting the plan first.
  2. Failing to reduce §221 for §127(c)(1)(B) interest. Form 1098-E from the loan servicer reports ALL interest paid on the loan, including the employer's §127(c)(1)(B) portion. The taxpayer's §221 above-the-line deduction on Schedule 1 line 21 must be REDUCED by the interest portion of §127(c)(1)(B) payments. IRS matches Form 1098-E to the §221 claim in correspondence audits; failing to reduce triggers CP2000 notices.
  3. Assuming the $5,250 ceiling stacks across employers. The $5,250 is a per-employee-per-calendar-year ceiling — but if an employee has two employers in one year, EACH employer can pay up to $5,250 under its own §127 plan. There is no aggregate per-employee cap across employers. Employees switching jobs mid-year should time their §127 tuition or loan-repayment requests to use both ceilings; a departing employee who has already received $5,250 from Employer A and starts at Employer B in November can receive an additional $5,250 from Employer B before December 31.
  4. Including meals, lodging, or transportation in the §127 reimbursement. §127(c)(1)(A) categorically excludes these. An employer that reimburses a graduate student's on-campus housing under a §127 plan converts that portion into ordinary §61 wages. The tuition alongside it may still qualify, but the housing does not — separate accounting is required at the reimbursement level.
  5. Missing the §127(b)(3) owner-employee 5% cap. Closely-held businesses adopting §127 without modeling total §127 spend against the 5% owner-employee cap often discover mid-year that the owner-employee's own tuition assistance exceeds the cap. The correction cascades into re-characterizing the excess as §61 wages for the owner-employee — with §3402 income-tax withholding, §3121 FICA withholding, and Form W-2c reporting.
  6. Failing to reduce §25A American Opportunity / Lifetime Learning Credit-eligible expenses for §127 amounts. §25A(g)(2) requires that qualified higher education expenses for the AOC or LLC be REDUCED by amounts excluded under §127. An undergraduate student whose employer pays $5,250 of tuition under §127 must reduce her AOC-eligible expenses by that $5,250 before computing the 100%/25% credit tiers. The IRS matches Form 8863 to Form 1098-T Box 5 (scholarships and grants) and to §127 amounts reported in W-2 Box 14; failing to reduce triggers §25A audit adjustments.

Action checklist for 2026

  1. Ask HR whether your employer has a §127 plan. If yes, request the plan document — verify it was adopted in writing before your first reimbursement, and confirm it covers both §127(c)(1)(A) tuition and §127(c)(1)(B) student-loan repayment (the loan-repayment feature requires a specific plan-amendment reference to §127(c)(1)(B); a pre-2020 plan does not automatically cover it).
  2. If your employer offers §127 loan repayment, verify the funding maximum. The statutory ceiling is $5,250 per calendar year, but many employers cap their own §127(c)(1)(B) contribution below the statutory maximum — $1,200/year and $2,400/year are common employer caps. Ask for the specific dollar figure your employer will pay.
  3. Time §127 payments across the calendar year boundary if you have flexibility. A December payment and a January payment each use a full $5,250 annual ceiling — you can capture two ceilings in a single academic term. Coordinate with HR on payment timing.
  4. Reduce your §221 student-loan interest deduction claim by the §127(c)(1)(B) interest fraction. Request from your employer a year-end statement showing the principal-vs-interest allocation of §127 payments; reduce your Schedule 1 line 21 deduction accordingly. Failure to reduce triggers CP2000 correspondence audits from IRS.
  5. Reduce your §25A AOC or Lifetime Learning Credit-eligible expenses by §127-excluded amounts. §25A(g)(2) coordination is enforced by IRS matching to Form 1098-T and W-2 Box 14. Compute your credit on the net-of-§127 expense figure.
  6. Reduce §529 distribution qualification by §127-excluded amounts. §529(c)(3)(B)(vi) coordination requires netting §127 amounts from qualified higher education expenses before computing the tax-free portion of §529 distributions.
  7. If you own more than 5% of your closely-held employer, model the §127(b)(3) 5% owner-employee cap BEFORE adopting a plan. The 5% cap applies to combined owner-employee benefits across all owners + their spouses/dependents. Consider §132(d) working-condition fringe as an alternative for job-required education.
  8. If you have both §127 and §132(d) capacity, exhaust §127 first. §127 covers education whether or not job-related; §132(d) requires job-relatedness. Use §127 for the broader-scope education first; layer §132(d) on top for uncapped job-required training.

Frequently asked questions

What is IRC §127 employer-provided educational assistance?

IRC §127 lets an employer pay up to $5,250 per employee per calendar year for qualified educational assistance without that amount being included in the employee's gross income under §61 or in FICA/FUTA wages. Both tuition-and-related-expenses (§127(c)(1)(A)) and, through December 31, 2025 sunset now made permanent by SECURE 2.0 §110, principal and interest payments on qualified education loans (§127(c)(1)(B)) count against the same $5,250 ceiling.

Is the $5,250 §127 ceiling indexed for inflation?

No. The $5,250 statutory ceiling was set in 1986 and has not been changed since. There is no COLA under §127(a) — the number has been the same for 40 years and would purchase roughly $14,900 of 2026-dollar educational assistance if it had tracked CPI. Legislation to raise the cap (most recently the Upskilling and Retraining Assistance Act) has been reintroduced in every Congress since 2020 but has not become law.

What made the §127(c)(1)(B) student-loan repayment inclusion permanent?

CARES Act §2206 added §127(c)(1)(B) in March 2020 with a December 31, 2020 sunset; Consolidated Appropriations Act 2021 §120 extended it through December 31, 2025; SECURE 2.0 Act §110 (Pub. L. 117-328, enacted December 29, 2022) struck the sunset entirely, making qualified education loan payments a permanent §127 expense category effective for payments made after March 27, 2020. The permanence removed the year-to-year plan-amendment friction that had kept many mid-sized employers on the sidelines.

Does §127 require a written plan?

Yes — §127(b)(1) requires a separate written plan for the exclusive benefit of employees. Treas. Reg. §1.127-2(b) says the plan must be a separate written document, in existence and communicated to eligible employees before benefits are paid. Amounts paid under an informal reimbursement arrangement — or under a plan document not adopted until after the calendar year ends — are NOT §127-excludable; they revert to §61 wages subject to income and payroll tax withholding.

What are the §127(b) anti-discrimination rules?

§127(b)(2) prohibits discrimination in favor of highly compensated employees as defined under §414(q). §127(b)(3) caps §127 benefits paid to owner-employees and their spouse/dependents (owners of more than 5%) at 5% of total §127 benefits paid under the plan. A plan that fails either test loses the §127 exclusion for the affected participants; §127(c)(3) then treats the benefit as wages under §61 for those participants, subject to income and payroll tax withholding.

Can I take the §221 student-loan interest deduction on interest my employer paid under §127?

No. §221(e)(1) explicitly denies the §221 above-the-line student-loan interest deduction (up to $2,500) for any interest amount that was already excluded from gross income under §127(c)(1)(B). The rule prevents double-dipping: the interest is either §127-excludable OR §221-deductible, never both. If your employer paid $3,000 of interest under §127 and you also paid $1,200 of interest out of pocket, only the $1,200 is eligible for §221.

Are §127 benefits reported on Form W-2?

The excluded portion of §127 benefits (up to the $5,250 ceiling) is NOT included in Form W-2 Box 1 wages, Box 3 Social Security wages, or Box 5 Medicare wages. Employers commonly report §127 amounts in Box 14 for informational purposes only. Any amount paid in excess of the $5,250 ceiling — or any amount paid under a plan that fails the §127(b) written-plan or non-discrimination requirements — IS included in Boxes 1/3/5 as ordinary compensation subject to income tax and FICA withholding under §3121(a)(18).

What education expenses qualify under §127(c)(1)(A)?

§127(c)(1)(A) covers "expenses incurred by or on behalf of an employee for education of the employee" including tuition, fees, books, supplies, and equipment, provided the education is not (i) a sport, game, or hobby unless job-related and (ii) not payment for meals, lodging, or transportation. The education need NOT be job-related — undergraduate, graduate, and continuing-education programs all qualify, and Treas. Reg. §1.127-2(c)(3) confirms coursework does not need to lead to a degree.

What education loans qualify under §127(c)(1)(B)?

§127(c)(1)(B) picks up any "qualified education loan" as defined in §221(d)(1) — a loan taken out solely to pay qualified higher education expenses for the taxpayer, spouse, or dependent, incurred at an eligible educational institution as defined in §25A(f)(2). Federal student loans (Direct Subsidized/Unsubsidized, Grad PLUS, Parent PLUS, Perkins) and private student loans both qualify. The loan must have been the employee's own obligation — an employer cannot pay a parent's Parent PLUS loan on behalf of the parent-employee and separately pay the employee's own Direct Loans within a single §127 accounting.

Can §127 educational assistance be used with a §529 plan or the §25A tax credits?

Yes, with anti-double-benefit coordination. §25A(g)(2) reduces qualified higher education expenses eligible for the American Opportunity Credit or Lifetime Learning Credit by the amount excluded from gross income under §127. Similarly, §529(c)(3)(B)(vi) reduces qualified higher education expenses eligible for tax-free §529 distributions by amounts excluded under §127. The rule is uniform: the same dollar of tuition cannot generate two federal tax benefits. Practitioners typically model the §127 exclusion first (guaranteed federal + payroll tax savings), then apply the residual to §25A or §529.
