1997 UPIA §104 Power to Adjust in 2026: The Non-UFIPA State Override, the §105 Abuse-of-Discretion Standard, and the Contemporaneous-Memorandum Playbook for Trust-Held Inherited IRAs

In the 42 U.S. jurisdictions still governed by the 1997 Uniform Principal and Income Act, the trustee's §104 power to adjust is the load-bearing override for §409's default 10-percent-of-required-distribution allocation on inherited IRAs. Here is the full nine-factor §104(b) framework, the §105 abuse-of-discretion standard of review, the contemporaneous-memorandum practice that survives judicial scrutiny, state-by-state variation across New York, Texas, Massachusetts, Illinois, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Georgia, New Jersey, and North Carolina, and a worked $2.5M QTIP case study.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 18, 2026 26 min read How we research

The August 17, 2026 field guide walked the 42 non-UFIPA U.S. jurisdictions and mapped how 1997 UPIA §409's 10-percent-of-required-distribution rule characterizes annual retirement-plan distributions to a trust.[1] That piece flagged the default's core problem: in a SECURE Act 10-year-rule world, applying §409 mechanically often gives the income beneficiary of a trust-held inherited IRA a tiny share of a very large annual distribution, sends the balance to principal, and can jeopardize a QTIP marital deduction under IRC §2056(b)(7) if the split falls below the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment threshold. The piece named three fixes: an express drafting override in the trust instrument, an annual §104 power-to-adjust election by the trustee, and a state unitrust conversion under a §106-style statute. This is the deep-dive on the middle fix — the trustee's §104 power — which is the most flexible, the least invasive, the only one that requires no beneficiary notice, and the one that most inherited-IRA trustees will actually use during the 10-year drawdown window.[2]

§104 is a discretionary grant, not a mandate. Nothing in the statute forces a trustee to adjust. But nothing in the statute forces a trustee to default to §409 either — and where the §409 default produces an unreasonable result under the trust's specific facts, a trustee who fails to consider whether §104 relief is warranted may itself breach the trust's duty of impartiality under 1997 UPIA §103, the state UTC counterpart (typically §803), or the common-law Restatement (Third) of Trusts §79 impartiality doctrine.[3] Understanding §104 is not optional for a trustee holding an inherited IRA in a non-UFIPA state; it is the load-bearing skill the office demands.

§104 at the uniform-text level

1997 UPIA §104 is a compact provision — five subsections, roughly 850 words — but it packs three distinct grants of authority into that space. Reading the statutory text closely is the first step for any trustee considering an adjustment, because the specific language of §104(a) (the grant itself), §104(b) (the nine factors), §104(c) (the eight prohibitions), and §104(d) (the release procedure) all matter.[4]

§104(a) states the core grant: a trustee may adjust between income and principal to the extent the trustee considers necessary if the trustee invests and manages trust assets as a prudent investor under the state's prudent-investor rule, the terms of the trust describe the amount that may or must be distributed to a beneficiary by referring to the trust's income, and the trustee determines, after applying the rules in §103(a), that the trustee is unable to comply with §103(b) — the duty of impartiality between income and remainder beneficiaries. The last clause is the operative predicate. If the trustee cannot achieve impartiality by applying the default characterization rules, the trustee may adjust.

§104(b) enumerates the nine factors the trustee must consider. The Comment to §104 explains that the list is non-exclusive — a trustee may consider additional factors the trust's specific facts make relevant — but the trustee must at minimum consider the nine listed factors. This is where the contemporaneous-memorandum practice becomes essential: a memorandum that walks each of the nine factors, applies each to the specific trust's circumstances, and explains the trustee's weighing of them creates the factual record that will be reviewed under §105 if a beneficiary later challenges the adjustment.

§104(c) enumerates eight situations in which a trustee may not exercise the power to adjust. These prohibitions matter more than they might first appear because most inherited-IRA trusts run into at least one of them at some point in their life cycle. §104(c)(1) prohibits adjustment where the adjustment would diminish the income interest in a trust that requires all income to be distributed to a spouse and for which an estate tax or gift tax marital deduction would be allowed — this is the exception the QTIP planner must most carefully monitor. §104(c)(2) prohibits adjustment where the adjustment would reduce the actuarial value of an income interest in a trust in which one or more individuals hold the power to withdraw a specified percentage of the trust each year. §104(c)(3) prohibits adjustment where the trust would be a grantor trust for federal income tax purposes solely because of the adjustment. §104(c)(4) prohibits adjustment where possessing or exercising the power would cause an individual to be treated as the owner of all or part of the trust for federal income tax purposes. §104(c)(5)-(8) cover related tax-driven exclusions that arise in tax-sensitive drafting.

§104(d) provides the release procedure. A trustee who is a beneficiary — or whose adjustment would result in the trustee becoming a beneficiary — may irrevocably release the power to adjust, permanently or for a specified period, by delivering a signed writing to any other trustee or, if none, to a designated adult beneficiary. This procedure exists because §104(c) prohibits adjustment where the trustee's interest would be affected by the decision; releasing the power removes the disqualification and lets a co-trustee (or, in a single-trustee trust, a successor named in the release) make the adjustment.

The §104(c)(1) QTIP-preservation exception — read this carefully

§104(c)(1) prohibits an adjustment that would diminish the income interest in a QTIP-qualifying trust. This is often misread as prohibiting any §104 adjustment in a QTIP trust. It does not. It prohibits adjustments that reduce what the income beneficiary receives; it does not prohibit adjustments that increase what the income beneficiary receives. A trustee of a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA where §409's default sends 90 percent to principal may absolutely exercise §104 to reallocate some of that principal back to income for the surviving spouse — indeed, doing so may be required to preserve the QTIP election under Rev. Rul. 2006-26. What the trustee may not do is exercise §104 to reduce the income beneficiary's share below the §409 default. The prohibition runs in one direction only.

The nine §104(b) factors and how they apply to an inherited-IRA trust

The nine factors listed at §104(b) form the analytical spine of every §104 exercise. Below is each factor with a working analysis of how it typically resolves for a trust holding an inherited IRA under the SECURE Act 10-year rule. The framework is deliberately practitioner-oriented — a trustee reading this section should be able to draft the first pass of a contemporaneous memorandum by walking down the list.[5]

FactorStatutory languageTypical inherited-IRA analysis
Factor 1The nature, purpose, and expected duration of the trustA see-through trust holding an inherited IRA has a defined 10-year duration under the SECURE Act (from the account owner's death through December 31 of year 10 for a non-EDB designated beneficiary). This short duration and the compressed distribution schedule are load-bearing for the impartiality analysis: the remainder beneficiary receives principal at the end of year 10, not over 30+ years the way a life-expectancy stretch would have delivered it. This factor typically favors reallocating more to income than §409's default provides, because the remainder beneficiary's expected wait is short and the income beneficiary's cash-flow interest is compressed into the same ten-year window.
Factor 2The settlor's intentLook to the trust instrument for direct evidence of intent: does the trust use maximal income-distribution language ("the trustee shall distribute all trust accounting income at least annually"), does the trust name the income beneficiary as the settlor's spouse or a dependent, does the trust reference "support" as a distribution standard? Language pointing toward income-beneficiary primacy generally supports §104 reallocation to income. Language emphasizing corpus preservation ("the trustee shall preserve principal for the remainder beneficiaries") points the other way. Where the trust instrument is silent, look to non-trust indicia — the settlor's letter of wishes, the settlor's will, the settlor's estate-planning file — for extrinsic evidence of intent.
Factor 3The identity and circumstances of the beneficiariesA surviving spouse in a QTIP trust with limited outside resources is a very different income beneficiary than an adult child in a discretionary trust with an existing $2M net worth. The circumstances of both the income beneficiary and the remainder beneficiary matter: reallocation to a needy income beneficiary from a well-provided remainder beneficiary is very different from the reverse. This is the factor that most often tilts the analysis in favor of substantial reallocation from §409's default to income.
Factor 4The needs for liquidity, for regularity of income, and for preservation and appreciation of capitalThe inherited IRA is a highly liquid asset by nature — the custodian sends cash. This factor typically neutralizes for an IRA-heavy trust. But note the trust's non-IRA assets: if the trust holds concentrated closely-held business interests alongside the IRA, the liquidity need typically points toward reallocating IRA distributions to principal (to build a cash reserve against the illiquid business asset) rather than to income. The factor is trust-specific.
Factor 5The assets held in the trust, their character, and the extent an asset is used by a beneficiaryAn IRA-only trust with no other assets is one situation; a trust holding an IRA plus a family home used rent-free by the surviving spouse is another. The rent-free use of a trust asset by an income beneficiary is often accounted for in the impartiality analysis by reducing the income allocation the trustee would otherwise make under §104 (because the beneficiary is already receiving in-kind benefits from principal). Conversely, if the surviving spouse has no in-kind use of any trust asset, the reallocation typically runs larger.
Factor 6The net amount allocated to income under other sections of the Act and the increase or decrease in the value of principal assetsThis is the load-bearing factor for an inherited-IRA §104 analysis. §409's default gives income roughly 10 percent of the RMD. Under the 10-year rule that RMD is roughly 10-15 percent of the balance annually in pre-death-schedule years and can spike to 25-40 percent in year 10 acceleration. The income allocation under the default is thus roughly 1 percent of the trust's IRA balance annually in early years, growing to 3-4 percent in year 10. Reasonable-apportionment analysis (see Factor 9) typically requires an income allocation of 3-5 percent of fair market value annually — matching the range non-UFIPA states use in their unitrust conversion statutes. The §104 reallocation needed to close the gap is thus typically 200-300 percent of the §409 default.
Factor 7Whether and to what extent the terms of the trust give the trustee power to invade principal or accumulate incomeA trust that gives the trustee discretionary power to invade principal for the income beneficiary's support (HEMS or an equivalent standard) may reduce the pressure for large §104 reallocations because the trustee can address income-beneficiary cash-flow needs through principal invasions instead. A trust without such invasion power is more dependent on §104 for impartiality. A trust that requires income accumulation until a specified event ("no distributions to the income beneficiary until the youngest child reaches age 25") sharply reduces the impartiality justification for §104 reallocation.
Factor 8The actual and anticipated effect of economic conditions and inflation on principal and incomeIn an inflationary environment, a fixed-percentage income allocation on a growing principal is falling in real terms; §104 reallocation partially offsets this. In a stable-price environment the factor is more neutral. In deflation, the reverse pressure applies. As of August 2026, the BLS-reported year-over-year CPI-U rate sits in the 2-3 percent range per the most recent CPI news release, so the inflation adjustment component of the §104 analysis is modest but not zero.
Factor 9The anticipated tax consequences of an adjustmentThis factor requires the trustee to compare (a) the tax result of the §409 default, where 90 percent of the distribution stays in the trust and is taxed at compressed IRC §1(e) trust brackets (37 percent above $16,000 for 2026 per IRS Notice 2025-67), against (b) the tax result of a §104 reallocation, where a larger share is distributed to the income beneficiary and taxed at the beneficiary's individual bracket. For most inherited-IRA trusts with a lower-bracket income beneficiary, factor 9 sharply favors reallocation because the bracket arbitrage between the 37-percent compressed trust bracket and the income beneficiary's typically-lower individual bracket is substantial. For a wealthy income beneficiary already in the top individual bracket, the factor is neutral. For a trust that will be reformed or terminated within the 10-year window anyway, factor 9 becomes more nuanced.

The §105 abuse-of-discretion standard of review

1997 UPIA §105 codifies the standard of review a reviewing court applies to a trustee's §104 adjustment decision. The statutory language mirrors the Restatement (Third) of Trusts §50 standard for discretionary distributions: a court may reverse only if the trustee's decision was arbitrary, capricious, or an abuse of discretion, or if the trustee's decision was contrary to a provision of the Act or of the trust instrument. This is a deferential standard, and it explains why so few §104 decisions have been reversed in the twenty-plus years since 1997 UPIA §104 was first adopted by state legislatures.[6]

The deference has a condition. A court can defer to a trustee's judgment only if the trustee's judgment is documented. Where the record shows a genuine deliberation across the nine §104(b) factors — a contemporaneous memorandum, professional advice from counsel and a tax adviser, and a specific dollar decision tied to a stated rationale — courts have consistently upheld the trustee's decision. Where the record shows only the outcome — the trustee reallocated $X from principal to income and reported it on Schedule K-1 — courts have been considerably harder to persuade, and the deference typically evaporates.

The reason is procedural. Absent a documented deliberation, a beneficiary's abuse-of-discretion claim reduces to a case-by-case rerun of the factor analysis in a courtroom, with the trustee's post-hoc rationalizations tested against the beneficiary's after-the-fact critiques. In that posture the trustee has already ceded the analytical high ground; whether the trustee's decision would have been reasonable is beside the point if the trustee cannot show it was made reasonably. The contemporaneous-memorandum practice is not merely a fiduciary best practice — it is the practical prerequisite to §105 deference.

Where §105 deference typically breaks down

Three patterns account for most reversals of §104 adjustments in the reported decisions. First, the trustee applies a form-book memorandum that recites the nine factors abstractly without applying them to the trust's specific facts — a court reading the memorandum cannot tell whether the trustee actually considered the trust's circumstances or merely papered over the file. Second, the trustee applies §104 without accounting for the §104(c) prohibitions — most commonly, without recognizing that a QTIP trust triggers §104(c)(1)'s "cannot diminish" limitation, or that a grantor trust triggers §104(c)(3)-(4)'s tax-driven exclusions. Third, the trustee applies a very large reallocation (approaching 100 percent to income) without any factual anchor for a divergence of that magnitude — the disproportionately large reallocation itself becomes evidence that the trustee substituted personal preference for the impartiality analysis the Act requires.

The contemporaneous-memorandum playbook

A defensible §104 memorandum has five parts. Each part serves a distinct evidentiary purpose under §105 review, and skipping any of them weakens the record.[7]

Part 1: Identify the default characterization being displaced. The memorandum should state explicitly which section of the Act's default characterization rules the trustee is departing from. For an inherited-IRA trust in a non-UFIPA state, this is almost always §409's 10-percent-of-required-distribution allocation. Cite the state's specific §409 counterpart (EPTL §11-A-4.9 in New York, Property Code §116.172 in Texas, and so on). State the dollar amount of the annual distribution the trustee is characterizing. State the dollar amount the §409 default would allocate to income and the dollar amount it would allocate to principal. This lays the analytical foundation for everything that follows.

Part 2: Walk each of the nine §104(b) factors. The Comment to §104 confirms that all nine factors must be considered, though the trustee may weigh them differently. For each factor, state the factor, state the specific fact from the trust's circumstances relevant to that factor, and state whether the factor points toward reallocation to income, reallocation to principal, or is neutral. Do not skip a factor. Do not treat a factor as inapplicable without explaining why. Where a factor is genuinely inapplicable (Factor 4's liquidity need for an all-cash trust, for example), say so and briefly explain why.

Part 3: State the specific dollar decision. The memorandum should state the specific dollar amount the trustee is reallocating from principal to income (or, hypothetically, from income to principal, though the §104(c)(1) QTIP exception makes the latter direction rare in practice for IRA-holding trusts). Do not state a percentage — the amount matters because Form 1041 reports dollars, not percentages, and the trustee's decision should tie precisely to what the K-1 will show. Do not state a formula — state the amount.

Part 4: Explain the reasoning for that specific amount. The trustee's memorandum should explain why the specific dollar amount was chosen and why more or less would have been less appropriate. This is the part of the memorandum that most often fails on judicial review — the trustee reallocates $75,000 but the memorandum offers no reason $75,000 rather than $50,000 or $100,000 was the right number. A common technique that survives review is to tie the dollar amount to a specific benchmark: "the reallocated amount produces an aggregate income allocation of 4 percent of the trust's beginning-of-year fair market value, matching the [state] unitrust conversion default under [state statute §X], which the trustee considers a reasonable proxy for the impartiality standard the Act requires."

Part 5: Reference any professional advice obtained. If the trustee obtained an opinion of counsel, a memorandum from the trust's accountant, or an analysis from an investment adviser, reference each by author, date, and topic. This is not required by the statute but substantially strengthens the record on §105 review. It also protects the trustee against beneficiary claims of imprudence by showing the trustee sought professional input on a complex fiduciary decision.

State-by-state §104 variation across the ten highest-population non-UFIPA jurisdictions

All 42 non-UFIPA jurisdictions adopted 1997 UPIA §104 in some form. Most adopted the uniform text substantively verbatim. A few adopted variations — most commonly, expansions to the nine-factor list, or clarifications on the §104(c) exclusions. Below is the state-by-state summary for the ten highest-population non-UFIPA jurisdictions, which together hold roughly half of the U.S. trust population.[8]

New York — EPTL §11-2.3(b)(5)

New York codified the §104 power to adjust at Estates, Powers and Trusts Law §11-2.3(b)(5), effective January 1, 2002, as part of the New York Prudent Investor Act. The statutory language substantively tracks the 1997 UPIA §104 uniform text but is organized within the broader §11-2.3 framework of the Prudent Investor Act rather than as a freestanding provision. The nine §104(b) factors appear at §11-2.3(b)(5)(A) through (I). New York's §104(c) exclusions appear at §11-2.3(b)(5)(J). New York's judicial construction of §104 is developed through a body of Surrogate's Court decisions applying the abuse-of-discretion standard consistent with §105.[9]

Texas — Property Code §116.005

Texas codified §104 at Texas Property Code §116.005 as part of Chapter 116, the Texas Uniform Principal and Income Act, effective January 1, 2004. The Texas statutory language tracks the uniform text closely with one notable expansion: Texas added a tenth factor at §116.005(b)(10) permitting the trustee to consider "any other relevant factor," an explicit codification of the non-exclusivity the Comment to the uniform §104 confirms. Texas §104(c) exclusions appear at §116.005(c) and track the uniform text. Texas has the additional advantage that Texas imposes no state income tax on individuals, trusts, or beneficiaries — so Factor 9's anticipated-tax-consequences analysis reduces to federal considerations only, simplifying the memorandum's tax section substantially.[10]

Massachusetts — G.L. c. 203D §3

Massachusetts codified §104 at Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 203D §3 as part of the Massachusetts Principal and Income Act, originally enacted 2003 and updated 2013. The statutory language tracks the uniform text. Massachusetts's §104(b) factors appear at c. 203D §3(b) and follow the uniform nine-factor list. The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court has confirmed that a trustee's §3 adjustment is reviewed under an abuse-of-discretion standard consistent with §105, and that a trustee who documents the factor analysis in a contemporaneous memorandum receives substantial deference.[11]

New Jersey — N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-4

New Jersey codified §104 at N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-4 as part of the New Jersey Uniform Principal and Income Act, effective 2001. The New Jersey enactment includes the standard nine §104(b) factors and the eight §104(c) exclusions. New Jersey courts have applied the §105 standard of review deferentially where the trustee has produced a contemporaneous memorandum walking the factors.[12]

Georgia — O.C.G.A. §53-12-343

Georgia codified §104 at O.C.G.A. §53-12-343 as part of Georgia's Uniform Principal and Income Act, effective July 1, 2018. Georgia was among the last non-UFIPA states to enact 1997 UPIA; the 2018 enactment coincided with a broader Georgia trust-code modernization. Georgia's §104(b) factors track the uniform nine, with slight rephrasing but no substantive divergence. Georgia's §104(c) exclusions appear at §53-12-343(c).[13]

Illinois — 760 ILCS 3/104

Illinois codified §104 at 760 ILCS 3/104 as part of the Illinois Trust Code recodification, effective January 1, 2020. Illinois folded the 1997 UPIA into its broader Trust Code enactment rather than adopting the Act freestanding. The Illinois §104(b) factors appear at 760 ILCS 3/104(b) and expand the uniform list to eleven factors, adding Illinois-specific considerations for the effect of adjustment on trust-held business interests and on the trust's charitable remainder if any. Illinois §104(c) exclusions appear at 760 ILCS 3/104(c).[14]

Pennsylvania — 20 Pa.C.S. §8104

Pennsylvania codified §104 at 20 Pa.C.S. §8104 as part of the Pennsylvania Uniform Principal and Income Act at Title 20 Chapter 81, effective July 1, 2002. Pennsylvania's §8104 substantively tracks the uniform §104 text. Pennsylvania has a distinctive state-tax treatment worth noting for Factor 9 analysis: Pennsylvania Personal Income Tax excludes certain retirement plan distributions from state tax for Pennsylvania residents, which affects the after-tax comparison between §409 default characterization (retention in trust) and §104 reallocation (distribution to beneficiary) differently than in most other non-UFIPA states.[15]

Ohio — Ohio Rev. Code §5812.03

Ohio codified §104 at Ohio Revised Code §5812.03 as part of Chapter 5812, the Ohio Uniform Principal and Income Act, effective January 1, 2003. Ohio's §5812.03 substantively tracks the uniform §104 text. Ohio's version adds an explicit reference to the state's Ohio Uniform Trust Code (Chapter 5810) as the applicable prudent-investor framework governing the trustee's investment discretion. Ohio has also enacted a robust decanting statute at §5808.18 that offers an alternative path — a decanting to a new trust with an express §409 override built in — for trustees who conclude the annual §104 mechanic is not the right long-term solution.[16]

Michigan — MCL 555.504

Michigan codified §104 at Michigan Compiled Laws §555.504 as part of the Michigan Uniform Principal and Income Act, effective September 1, 2004. Michigan's §555.504 substantively tracks the uniform §104 text. Michigan supplements the framework with a modernized version of the Uniform Trust Code at Chapter 700, which provides additional trustee-power mechanics that can complement a §104 adjustment (including expanded modification and reformation powers under MCL 700.7411 and MCL 700.7412).[17]

North Carolina — N.C. Gen. Stat. §37A-1-104

North Carolina codified §104 at N.C. Gen. Stat. §37A-1-104 as part of Chapter 37A, the North Carolina Uniform Principal and Income Act, effective January 1, 2004. The North Carolina statute tracks the uniform §104 text closely. North Carolina has a well-developed body of appellate case law on trustee discretion generally, which informs the specific abuse-of-discretion analysis under §105 for §104 adjustments.[18]

Interaction with the SECURE Act 10-year rule and TD 10001

The SECURE Act of December 2019 and Treasury's final regulations at TD 10001 (July 19, 2024) implementing the 10-year rule fundamentally changed the fact pattern §104 must operate against.[19] Under the pre-SECURE stretch-IRA regime, a trust receiving annual distributions from an inherited IRA typically received 4-6 percent of the balance annually over the beneficiary's life expectancy — 20-30 years for adult designated beneficiaries. §409's 10-percent-of-RMD allocation on a 5-percent RMD produced a 0.5 percent income allocation on the trust's IRA balance, which was small but arguably reasonable for a life-expectancy schedule under the impartiality standard.

Under the 10-year rule as clarified by TD 10001, a non-EDB trust beneficiary of an account owner who died on or after the required beginning date must take annual RMDs computed under the pre-death schedule PLUS complete distribution by December 31 of year 10. In practice, the annual RMD runs 10-15 percent of the balance in the pre-death-schedule years, and the year-10 acceleration typically pushes the aggregate distribution for the final year to 25-40 percent of the remaining balance. §409's mechanical 10-percent-of-RMD allocation on a 10-percent RMD produces a 1 percent income allocation on the trust's IRA balance — larger than the pre-SECURE calibration, but often still much smaller than a reasonable impartiality analysis would support given the compressed distribution schedule and the shortened remainder-beneficiary wait.

Factor 1 (the trust's expected duration) and Factor 6 (the net income allocated under other sections of the Act) do the heaviest lifting in the SECURE-era §104 analysis. Factor 1 pushes toward reallocation because the trust's short 10-year life span shortens the remainder beneficiary's expected wait dramatically — a reallocation from principal to income has much less effect on the remainder beneficiary's expected receipt (they will receive the balance at year 10 regardless) than the equivalent reallocation would have had under the stretch regime. Factor 6 pushes toward reallocation because §409's default income allocation, measured against the trust's total annual receipts from the IRA, is now proportionally smaller than the impartiality standard supports.

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 and the reasonable-apportionment standard

The IRS confirmed in Rev. Rul. 2006-26 that a state-law characterization framework qualifies for QTIP marital-deduction purposes only if the framework provides a reasonable apportionment between the income and remainder beneficiaries — and specifically identified state-law provisions permitting adjustments (like 1997 UPIA §104) or unitrust conversions (like §106 or state-specific equivalents) as satisfying the reasonable-apportionment standard. The Ruling is why a §104 adjustment in a QTIP context is not merely a discretionary trustee decision but often a QTIP-preservation necessity: without the adjustment, the state's default characterization may itself fail Rev. Rul. 2006-26, disqualifying the QTIP election and triggering federal estate tax deficiency exposure. The trustee's contemporaneous memorandum in a QTIP context should explicitly reference Rev. Rul. 2006-26 and state the trustee's basis for concluding that the adjusted allocation satisfies the Ruling's reasonable-apportionment standard.

Case study: $2.5M inherited IRA in a New York QTIP trust

Consider a concrete fact pattern that surfaces the §104 mechanic under realistic 2026 conditions. Robert died in March 2026 at age 74, past his required beginning date. His $2.5M traditional IRA at Fidelity named a testamentary QTIP trust as beneficiary. The QTIP names his surviving spouse Elena (age 72, remarried after Robert's death — Robert's second marriage; Elena's third) as sole income beneficiary and his two adult children from his first marriage (David, age 45; Jennifer, age 43) as remainder beneficiaries in equal shares. The trust is administered under New York law with the New York Attorney General's office as ancillary jurisdictional overseer per EPTL Article 7 for charitable-adjacent testamentary trusts. First Union Trust Company serves as corporate trustee. The QTIP election was made on Robert's estate tax return (Form 706, due December 2026 with an automatic six-month extension) to preserve the federal estate tax marital deduction under IRC §2056(b)(7).

The trust's non-IRA assets are $600,000 (a diversified equity portfolio managed by First Union). The IRA is the trust's dominant asset at $2.5M — roughly 80 percent of the trust's total value. Under the SECURE Act 10-year rule and TD 10001, the trust must complete distribution of the inherited IRA by December 31, 2036. In year 1 (calendar 2026), First Union takes the year-1 RMD of $102,000 (computed under Robert's pre-death Uniform Lifetime Table divisor of 24.6 at age 74, applied to the $2.5M balance).

Default §409 characterization. Under EPTL §11-A-4.9 — New York's codification of 1997 UPIA §409 — 10 percent of the $102,000 RMD ($10,200) is trust accounting income; the remaining $91,800 is trust principal. Under IRC §2056(b)(7), the QTIP trust must distribute all income to Elena annually. Elena would receive $10,200 for the year. On a $2.5M IRA producing $102,000 of taxable distributions, Elena's $10,200 represents 10 percent of the annual distribution and 0.4 percent of the trust's IRA balance.

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment concern. First Union's counsel evaluates whether the $10,200 allocation satisfies the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment standard. On a $2.5M IRA distributing $102,000 to a QTIP trust, an income allocation of $10,200 (about 0.4 percent of fair market value annually) is at the low end of what practitioners consider defensible. Counsel notes that New York's own unitrust conversion default at EPTL §11-2.4 uses 4 percent of net fair market value — implying that the New York legislature considers 4 percent a reasonable default rate for income beneficiaries in a similar situation. A reallocation to a 4-percent-of-fair-market-value benchmark would produce an income allocation of $100,000 (4 percent of $2.5M), of which the §409 default already provides $10,200, requiring a §104 reallocation of $89,800 from principal to income.

The §104(b) factor analysis. First Union's trust officer prepares a §104 memorandum walking each of the nine factors:

  • Factor 1 (trust duration): 10-year window under SECURE Act; remainder beneficiaries wait only ten years for principal. Points toward reallocation.
  • Factor 2 (settlor intent): The trust instrument uses standard QTIP language requiring all income to be distributed annually, and Robert's letter of wishes filed with First Union states an intent that "my wife receive meaningful lifetime income from my retirement assets." Points strongly toward reallocation.
  • Factor 3 (beneficiary circumstances): Elena has modest independent means ($200K in retirement savings from her prior career; Social Security of $2,400/month); the remainder beneficiaries David and Jennifer are both established professionals (David is a partner at a NY law firm; Jennifer is a physician) with substantial existing net worth of their own. Points strongly toward reallocation.
  • Factor 4 (liquidity, regularity, capital preservation): IRA is fully liquid; no capital preservation concerns arise from the reallocation itself. Neutral.
  • Factor 5 (assets held; beneficiary use): Elena does not use any trust asset in kind. Points toward reallocation.
  • Factor 6 (net amount to income under other sections): §409's $10,200 default is 0.4 percent of IRA balance; the New York unitrust default is 4 percent, and the reasonable-apportionment standard per Rev. Rul. 2006-26 typically requires something in the 3-5 percent range. Points strongly toward reallocation.
  • Factor 7 (invasion power): The trust gives First Union discretionary power to invade principal for Elena's support (HEMS standard), which reduces some pressure on §104. But invading principal for Elena would still count against the remainder beneficiaries' expected share; reallocating via §104 is functionally equivalent but cleaner administratively. Modestly toward reallocation.
  • Factor 8 (economic conditions, inflation): 2026 CPI-U running roughly 2.5 percent year-over-year per the most recent BLS release; modest inflation adjustment supports some reallocation to income to preserve real purchasing power. Modestly toward reallocation.
  • Factor 9 (tax consequences): Elena's individual income tax bracket for 2026 (single filer at $12,000/month income from various sources) is 24 percent federal + 6 percent New York state. Retaining the $89,800 in the trust would tax it at the trust's 37 percent federal bracket (all above $16,000) + 8.82 percent New York top-of-schedule bracket for trusts. The bracket arbitrage is roughly 15 percentage points, or about $13,500 in annual federal/state tax savings on the year-1 reallocation. Strongly toward reallocation.

The §104 decision. First Union reallocates $89,800 from principal to income under EPTL §11-2.3(b)(5), producing an aggregate income allocation of $100,000 for the year (4 percent of the $2.5M IRA balance, matching New York's EPTL §11-2.4 unitrust default rate). First Union distributes the $100,000 to Elena. First Union's memorandum documents the factor analysis above, cites Rev. Rul. 2006-26 as the basis for concluding the 4-percent-of-fair-market-value benchmark satisfies reasonable apportionment, and references opinion of trust counsel and the tax analysis from First Union's in-house tax department.

Six recurring §104 mistakes on inherited-IRA trusts

The fiduciary-litigation record over the past two decades surfaces six recurring mistakes that trustees make with §104 in the inherited-IRA context. Every one is avoidable with the right process.[20]

  1. No contemporaneous memorandum. The trustee makes the §104 decision but does not document the nine-factor analysis before the decision. Under §105 review, the trustee then attempts to reconstruct the reasoning after a beneficiary challenge — with materially less deference than the trustee would have received if the reasoning had been contemporaneously documented.
  2. Form-book memoranda. The trustee copies a template memorandum that recites the nine factors abstractly without applying them to the specific trust's facts. Courts have consistently discounted such memoranda as pro-forma and denied the §105 deference the memoranda were intended to secure.
  3. Ignoring Factor 9's compressed-bracket math. The trustee makes a §104 decision without accounting for the trust's compressed IRC §1(e) bracket schedule at 37 percent above $16,000 for 2026 (per IRS Notice 2025-67) versus individual brackets at 37 percent above $640K. For most inherited-IRA trusts with a lower-bracket income beneficiary, the tax-arbitrage math strongly favors substantial reallocation to income; ignoring this factor typically produces a suboptimal decision and undermines the memorandum's Factor 9 documentation.
  4. Applying §104 when the trust instrument already contains an express §409 override. The trustee applies §104 to reallocate an inherited-IRA distribution when the trust instrument itself already contains a provision expressly displacing §409's default characterization. The express override controls under §103; the §104 adjustment is redundant, and if it produces a different allocation than the express override provides, it may itself be a breach of the trust's terms.
  5. Applying §104 to a grantor trust. The trustee applies §104 to a trust that is a grantor trust for federal income tax purposes (all income and gain taxed to the grantor, not to the trust). The §104 adjustment has no federal-tax effect because both principal and income are taxed to the grantor regardless. Beneficiary distributions to non-grantor beneficiaries may be treated as gifts. The trustee typically should not exercise §104 on a grantor trust at all, or should limit the exercise to state-tax and state-law-accounting purposes only.
  6. Failing to update the memorandum in later years. The trustee prepares a contemporaneous memorandum in year 1 and then relies on it as authority for the same §104 reallocation in years 2 through 10 without updating. Facts change — Elena's outside resources may improve or deteriorate, the trust's non-IRA assets may appreciate or depreciate, David's and Jennifer's circumstances may shift, market conditions may cycle, tax law may amend. A memorandum that documented a Year 1 factor analysis does not automatically justify the same reallocation in Year 5; the trustee must reconsider each year and document the reconsideration.

Action checklist for a §104 exercise on a trust-held inherited IRA

If you are the trustee of a see-through trust holding an inherited IRA in a non-UFIPA state and you are considering whether to exercise §104 for an annual distribution, work through this eight-item checklist before making the reallocation.

  1. Confirm the state's §104 citation and check for state-specific variations. Look up the specific §104 counterpart in your state's code (see the state-by-state table above for the ten highest-population non-UFIPA jurisdictions). Note whether the state has adopted variations — Texas's tenth "any other relevant factor," Illinois's eleven-factor expansion, or state-specific §104(c) exclusions.
  2. Read the trust instrument for an express §409 override. If the trust instrument expressly displaces §409's default characterization, the express override controls and §104 is unnecessary. Do not apply §104 to a trust with an express override that specifies a different result; that itself would be a breach of the trust's terms.
  3. Confirm the trust does not fall within a §104(c) prohibition. Walk through each of the eight §104(c) exclusions. The most common trap for QTIP trusts is §104(c)(1)'s "cannot diminish the income interest" prohibition — but recall that the prohibition runs one direction only and does not prevent reallocation that increases the income beneficiary's share. Grantor trusts trigger §104(c)(3)-(4). Trusts with beneficiary withdrawal powers trigger §104(c)(2).
  4. Compute the §409 default allocation and identify the reasonable-apportionment benchmark. Take 10 percent of the required-distribution portion of the annual distribution — that is the §409 default income allocation. Compare that to a reasonable-apportionment benchmark: typically 3-5 percent of the trust's beginning-of-year fair market value (matching state unitrust conversion default ranges) or the state's specific unitrust rate (4 percent in New York).
  5. Walk each of the nine §104(b) factors and document the analysis. Do not skip any factor. For each, state the specific fact from the trust's circumstances, state how the factor cuts (toward or against reallocation), and state the weight the trustee assigns to it. This is the analytical spine that must appear in the contemporaneous memorandum.
  6. Decide on a specific dollar reallocation amount and document the reasoning. State the specific dollar amount (not a percentage). Explain why that specific amount rather than a larger or smaller one. Tie the amount to a specific benchmark (typically the state's unitrust rate or the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment analysis for QTIP trusts).
  7. Reference professional advice and confirm QTIP interaction if applicable. If the trust is a QTIP marital-deduction trust, expressly reference Rev. Rul. 2006-26 and IRC §2056(b)(7) in the memorandum. If the trustee obtained opinion of counsel or tax advice, reference each by author, date, and topic. Save the underlying advice memoranda in the trust's records.
  8. Model the §104 reallocation against alternatives and report on Form 1041. Use the CalcLeap retirement calculator, the 401(k) withdrawal calculator, the traditional IRA calculator, the Roth IRA calculator, and the estate tax calculator to project the after-tax outcome under each characterization approach. Report the reallocated income on the trust's Form 1041 and the distribution on the beneficiary's Schedule K-1.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 1997 UPIA §104 power to adjust and why does it matter for a trust holding an inherited IRA?

1997 UPIA §104 is the statutory grant of discretionary authority letting a trustee reallocate amounts between trust accounting income and trust principal when the default characterization rules — including §409's 10-percent-of-required-distribution rule for retirement-plan distributions — produce an unfair result under the trust's specific circumstances. For an inherited IRA, §409 typically allocates only about 10 percent of the annual RMD to income, sending 90 percent to principal, and in a SECURE Act 10-year-rule world that split often fails the trust's own duty of impartiality and can jeopardize a QTIP marital deduction under IRC §2056(b)(7). §104 lets the trustee reallocate without amending the trust, without going to court, and without notifying beneficiaries in advance.

What are the nine statutory factors under §104(b)?

§104(b) enumerates nine factors: (1) trust nature, purpose, and expected duration; (2) settlor's intent; (3) identity and circumstances of beneficiaries; (4) needs for liquidity, regularity of income, and capital preservation and appreciation; (5) assets held in the trust and beneficiary use; (6) net income allocated under other sections of the Act; (7) trustee's power to invade principal or accumulate income; (8) economic conditions and inflation; (9) anticipated tax consequences of the adjustment. Most non-UFIPA states adopt this list verbatim; Illinois expands it to eleven and Texas adds a tenth "any other relevant factor."

What is the §105 abuse-of-discretion standard of review?

1997 UPIA §105 codifies an arbitrary-or-capricious-or-abuse-of-discretion standard — the deferential standard the Restatement (Third) of Trusts §50 applies to discretionary distributions. A reviewing court must not substitute its own judgment for the trustee's; it may reverse only if the trustee's decision fell outside the range of reasonable choices given the §104(b) factors. Courts have consistently upheld §104 adjustments where the trustee documented the analysis in a contemporaneous memorandum before making the decision.

How does the SECURE Act 10-year rule change the §104 calculus?

The SECURE Act 10-year rule compresses inherited-IRA distributions from a life-expectancy stretch (4-6 percent of balance annually over 20-30 years) into a hard 10-year window with annual distributions of roughly 10-15 percent of balance and a year-10 acceleration to complete drawdown. §409's default 10-percent-of-RMD income allocation on a 5-percent RMD (pre-SECURE) produced a 0.5-percent income allocation; on a 10-15-percent RMD (post-SECURE) it produces a 1-percent allocation. Both are typically below the 3-5 percent reasonable-apportionment benchmark for QTIP purposes. §104 is the primary tool for closing that gap without amending the trust or converting to a unitrust.

How does §104 interact with the QTIP marital deduction under IRC §2056(b)(7)?

IRC §2056(b)(7) requires the QTIP trust to distribute all trust accounting income to the surviving spouse annually. Rev. Rul. 2006-26 confirms that the IRS respects the state-law characterization framework only if it provides a reasonable apportionment between income and remainder beneficiaries. In a non-UFIPA state, the §104 power to adjust is the primary mechanism for making a §409 default allocation reasonable. Failure to make the adjustment when the default is unreasonable can retroactively disqualify the QTIP election and trigger federal estate tax deficiency exposure on the deceased spouse's estate. The §104 adjustment should be documented specifically as a QTIP-preservation adjustment referencing Rev. Rul. 2006-26.

Does exercising §104 require beneficiary notice or court approval?

No. §104 does not require the trustee to give beneficiary notice in advance or to obtain court approval. This is the mechanic's most important practical advantage over a state unitrust conversion under §106 (which typically requires notice with prescribed content) and over a trust reformation (which requires a petition and court order or a nonjudicial settlement agreement). That said, prudent trustees handling substantial inherited-IRA adjustments often provide voluntary post-decision notice to all beneficiaries — a beneficiary who first learns of the adjustment months later on a Form 1041 K-1 is more likely to challenge the decision than one who received a timely explanation.

Can a trustee exercise §104 to allocate 100 percent of an inherited-IRA distribution to income?

In principle yes, but only if the nine §104(b) factors — properly documented in the contemporaneous memorandum — support that outcome. A 100-percent allocation is defensible in narrow cases (a QTIP trust for a surviving spouse of a first marriage with no children in common; substantial appreciating assets outside the IRA; documented income-beneficiary cash-flow need). It is much harder to defend in most other fact patterns. The safer default is a partial adjustment — enough to satisfy the reasonable-apportionment standard for QTIP or duty-of-impartiality purposes without eliminating the remainder beneficiaries' expected share. §104(c)(1) may also constrain an over-large adjustment in the QTIP context.

What is the contemporaneous-memorandum practice and why does it matter?

The contemporaneous-memorandum practice is the fiduciary-industry standard for documenting a §104 adjustment. Before making the decision — not after a beneficiary challenges it — the trustee prepares a written memorandum that (1) identifies the specific default characterization being displaced, (2) walks each of the nine §104(b) factors and applies each to the trust's specific facts, (3) states the specific dollar amount reallocated, (4) explains the trustee's reasoning for that specific amount, and (5) references any professional advice obtained. In every published §104 challenge decision, courts have deferred to trustees who produced a genuine contemporaneous memorandum and reversed trustees who did not.

How does §104 differ from a state unitrust conversion under §106?

§104 is a per-year discretionary reallocation; §106 (or its state-law equivalent) is a permanent conversion of the entire trust from income-only structure into a percentage-of-value unitrust. §104 makes a fresh determination each year; §106 fixes a percentage (typically 3-5 percent, 4 percent in New York) applied to the trust's net fair market value annually. §104 preserves flexibility; §106 provides predictability. §104 requires no beneficiary notice; §106 typically requires notice with statutory content. For a trust holding a single inherited IRA subject to the 10-year rule, §104 is usually the better tool because the trustee can calibrate each year to the actual distribution requirement rather than committing to a fixed percentage.

What are the most common §104 mistakes on inherited-IRA trusts?

Six recurring mistakes: (1) no contemporaneous memorandum; (2) form-book memoranda reciting factors abstractly without applying them to the trust's facts; (3) ignoring Factor 9's compressed-bracket math (37% above $16,000 for 2026 trust brackets versus $640K for individuals per IRS Notice 2025-67); (4) applying §104 when the trust instrument already contains an express §409 override; (5) applying §104 to a grantor trust where the adjustment has no federal-tax effect; (6) failing to update the memorandum in later years when the trust's facts change.
