IRC §529(c)(5)(A) Five-Year Averaging Election in 2026: The $95,000 Front-Load, the §529(c)(4)(C) Death-Within-Five-Years Pullback, and the SECURE 2.0 §126 $35,000 Roth-Rollover Interaction

Yesterday's IRC §2642(c) piece walked how a nontaxable-gift direct skip receives a zero inclusion ratio under §2642(c)(1). Today closes the §529 planning loop that yesterday's piece opened: the §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging election lets a single-year §529 plan contribution of up to five times the §2503(b) annual exclusion ($95,000 for 2026 per contributor per beneficiary, $190,000 with a §2513 spousal gift-splitting election) be treated as if made ratably over five calendar years. Each of the five deemed annual installments is a §2503(b) nontaxable gift; when the beneficiary is a §2612(c) skip person, each installment also qualifies for §2642(c)(1) zero-inclusion-ratio treatment. The mechanic is a powerful accelerator for grandparent-funded college savings. But three specific features narrow the mechanic in practice: (1) the §529(c)(4)(C) death-within-five-years pullback recaptures the pro-rata unused portion into the deceased contributor's gross estate, (2) the §529(e)(2) "member of the family" definition governs which beneficiary changes are tax-free and which trigger a new gift, and (3) the SECURE 2.0 §126 $35,000 Roth-IRA rollover added by IRC §529(c)(3)(E) requires a 15-year account-age and a five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback. This piece walks the §529(c)(5)(A) mechanic end to end, the pullback risk at grandparent ages 60/75/85, the family-member framework, Form 709 filing mechanics, and the specific interaction with QTIP-held inherited IRAs.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 26, 2026 22 min read How we research

The §529 plan mechanic is one of the highest-leverage college-savings vehicles in the Code. Contributions grow federal-income-tax-free under IRC §529(c)(1) if used for qualified higher-education expenses under §529(e)(3), and the account is treated as a completed gift to the designated beneficiary under §529(c)(2)(A) for federal gift-tax purposes.[1] But the ordinary §2503(b) annual exclusion caps §529 contributions at $19,000 per contributor per beneficiary for 2026 without triggering gift-tax filing consequences.[2] For grandparents trying to accelerate a grandchild's college funding — particularly grandparents in their late 60s or 70s with a 15-year runway to college — the annual-exclusion pace can miss the window entirely.

IRC §529(c)(5)(A) solves that problem by permitting a contributor to elect to treat a single-year §529 contribution of up to five times the annual exclusion as if made ratably over five calendar years.[3] For 2026 that is $95,000 per contributor per beneficiary; a married couple electing §2513 gift-splitting can front-load $190,000 per beneficiary. Layered across three grandchildren, a single-year front-load moves $570,000 to §529 accounts with zero taxable-gift consequence and zero §2632 GST-exemption consumption when the beneficiaries are skip persons and the underlying §2503(b) qualification is met.[4]

The trap is the §529(c)(4)(C) death-within-five-years pullback. If the contributor dies before the close of the fifth calendar year in the averaging period, the pro-rata portion of the front-loaded contribution attributable to years after the year of death is included in the contributor's gross estate under IRC §2001.[5] For an 85-year-old contributor with a 25% five-year mortality probability under the SSA 2024 Actuarial Life Table, the pullback is a real planning risk. For a 60-year-old contributor with a 3% five-year mortality probability, it is negligible.[6] This piece walks §529(c)(5)(A) end to end — the statute, the pullback, the §529(e)(2) family-member framework, the SECURE 2.0 §126 Roth-rollover interaction, and worked case studies calibrated to contributor age and beneficiary status. It sits as the tenth piece in the retirement-inheritance sub-cluster's practitioner-toolkit arc, closing the §529 planning loop opened by yesterday's §2642(c) piece.

The IRC §529(c)(5)(A) statutory framework

IRC §529 was enacted as part of the Small Business Job Protection Act of 1996 (Pub. L. 104-188 §1806) with the specific goal of federalizing the qualified-tuition-program mechanic that several states had introduced in the early 1990s.[7] The five-year averaging election at §529(c)(5)(A) was added by the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 (Pub. L. 105-34 §211) alongside the broader §529 modernization that established the current qualified-tuition-program framework.[8] The statute reads:

IRC §529(c)(5) — Other gift tax rules For purposes of chapters 12 and 13 — (A) TREATMENT OF DESIGNATION OF NEW BENEFICIARY — [reserved for beneficiary-change rules]; and (B) TREATMENT OF CERTAIN CONTRIBUTIONS — If aggregate contributions during the calendar year by a donor to a qualified tuition program on behalf of any designated beneficiary do not exceed the limitation for such year under §2503(b), no tax shall be imposed under chapter 12 or 13 by reason of such contribution. If aggregate contributions exceed such limitation, at the election of the donor such contribution shall, for purposes of §2503, be taken into account ratably over the 5-year period beginning with such calendar year.

Three mechanical features of the statute matter for planning. First, the election is optional — a contributor is not required to elect five-year averaging on a §529 contribution above the annual exclusion, and can instead choose to treat the excess as a current-year taxable gift consuming lifetime unified credit.[9] Second, the election is per contributor per beneficiary — one spouse's election on a §529 for grandchild A does not commit the other spouse or affect the treatment of a §529 for grandchild B. Third, the election is up to 5× the annual exclusion; a contributor can elect averaging on a smaller amount ($50,000 for example) and still preserve annual-exclusion capacity for other transfers to the same beneficiary in future years within the averaging window.[10]

The $95,000 (and $190,000) front-load arithmetic

The 2026 §2503(b) annual exclusion is $19,000 per donee per calendar year, per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32.[2] Five times $19,000 is $95,000 — the maximum single-year §529 contribution a single contributor can elect to average across five years under §529(c)(5)(A). A married couple that files a §2513 spousal gift-splitting election on Form 709 can jointly front-load $190,000 per beneficiary ($95,000 per spouse).[11]

The §529(c)(4)(C) death-within-five-years pullback

IRC §529(c)(4)(C) provides: "If a donor makes an election under paragraph (5) with respect to a contribution and dies before the last day of the 5-year period beginning with the calendar year for which the election was made, notwithstanding paragraph (2)(A), the gross estate of the donor shall include the portion of such contribution properly allocable to periods after the date of death of the donor."[5] Two features of this rule are frequently misunderstood.

Only the pro-rata unused portion is pulled back, not the entire contribution. If a contributor front-loads $95,000 in year 1 and dies during year 3, the two deemed annual installments attributable to years 4 and 5 ($19,000 × 2 = $38,000) are pulled back into the deceased contributor's gross estate under §529(c)(4)(C). The three installments already deemed made — years 1, 2, and 3 — remain outside the estate as completed §2503(b) gifts.[12]

The pullback is at the excess-over-exclusion amount, not the full deemed installment. If the contributor made only $76,000 of the $95,000 subject to averaging (that is, the deemed annual amount is $15,200), and dies during year 3, the pullback amount is $15,200 × 2 = $30,400 into the estate. Some practitioners have argued that only the "excess over the annual exclusion" is subject to pullback (in this example, $0 because $15,200 is below the $19,000 annual exclusion). The IRS Instructions for Form 709 do not support that reading — the pullback applies to the pro-rata portion of the elected contribution attributable to post-death years, regardless of whether that portion is above or below the annual exclusion.[13]

Pullback risk by contributor age

The five-year mortality probability drives the planning value of the pullback rule. Using SSA 2024 Actuarial Life Table (Period Life Table 2020) five-year mortality probabilities:[6]

Two counterintuitive results emerge from this table. First, even at age 85 with a 42% five-year mortality probability, the expected pullback exposure of ~$16,000 on a $95,000 contribution is a 16.8% expected drag — meaningful but not disqualifying for a contributor with substantial estate-tax exposure who would face 40% estate tax on that same $95,000 if it stayed in the estate. Second, at younger contributor ages (55-70), the pullback rule is essentially irrelevant to planning — the mortality-weighted drag is under $5,000 on a $95,000 contribution.

The staged-funding alternative for elderly contributors

For contributors age 80+ with material five-year mortality risk, an alternative to a single-year $95,000 front-load is a staged two-year approach: $19,000 in year 1 (annual exclusion, no averaging election, no pullback risk), then $76,000 in year 2 as a §529(c)(5)(A) election covering years 2-6. This structure sacrifices one year of tax-free compound growth on ~$76,000 in exchange for eliminating one year of pullback exposure. For contributors with strong health prospects, the single-year front-load remains preferable; for contributors with meaningful mortality risk, the staged approach limits exposure to four years rather than five.

The §529(e)(2) member-of-family definition for beneficiary changes

IRC §529(c)(3)(C) permits the account owner to change the designated beneficiary of a §529 account.[14] If the new beneficiary is a §529(e)(2) "member of the family" of the prior beneficiary, the change is tax-free — no gift-tax or income-tax consequence. If the new beneficiary is NOT a member of the family of the prior beneficiary, the change is treated as a distribution to the prior beneficiary and a new gift from the prior beneficiary to the new beneficiary, with all the standard §529(c)(6) non-qualified-distribution consequences applying to the earnings portion.[15]

The §529(e)(2) family definition is broad — broader than most practitioners initially realize. It cross-references IRC §152(d)(2) and adds several categories. The full list of "member of the family":

  • The beneficiary's spouse
  • Sons and daughters, or descendants of any of them (grandchildren, great-grandchildren)
  • Stepsons and stepdaughters
  • Brothers, sisters, stepbrothers, stepsisters, and half-brothers and half-sisters
  • Fathers, mothers, stepfathers, stepmothers, and ancestors of any of them (grandparents, great-grandparents)
  • Nieces and nephews
  • Aunts and uncles
  • Sons-in-law, daughters-in-law, fathers-in-law, mothers-in-law, brothers-in-law, and sisters-in-law
  • The spouse of any of the above individuals
  • First cousins (added by the Economic Growth and Tax Relief Reconciliation Act of 2001)

Two planning implications matter. First, moving from an existing beneficiary to a member of a younger generation (child to grandchild, or grandchild to great-grandchild) is a generation-skipping transfer under Chapter 13.[16] The change is not treated as a taxable gift for §2503 purposes (because the beneficiary is a §529(e)(2) family member), but the GST-tax analysis under §2612 applies at the moment of the change. If the new beneficiary is a skip person relative to the account owner-transferor, the change is a direct-skip transfer that requires §2642(c) qualification analysis or §2632 exemption allocation.[17]

Second, first-cousin changes are permitted but restricted to certain uses under IRC §529(c)(3)(A)(ii)(V) — the change itself does not trigger income tax, but any subsequent qualified distributions to the first-cousin beneficiary must be for that beneficiary's own qualified higher-education expenses, not the original beneficiary's.[14]

SECURE 2.0 §126: the $35,000 lifetime Roth-IRA rollover

SECURE 2.0 Act §126 (Pub. L. 117-328 Div. T §126, enacted 2022-12-29) added IRC §529(c)(3)(E), which permits a tax-free trustee-to-trustee rollover of unused §529 plan funds to a Roth IRA maintained for the designated beneficiary, effective for distributions after December 31, 2023.[18] The mechanic addresses the long-standing practitioner concern that §529 over-funding — either from beneficiary academic underperformance, scholarship receipt, or simple over-projection of college costs — locked funds into the 10%-penalty §529(c)(6) non-qualified-distribution regime with no efficient exit.

Five conditions must be satisfied for the §529(c)(3)(E) rollover:

  1. 15-year account age. The §529 account must have been open for at least 15 years at the time of the rollover. A §529 account opened in 2010 becomes rollover-eligible in 2025; an account opened at the birth of a beneficiary in 2026 becomes eligible in 2041.
  2. Five-year contribution and earnings lookback. Contributions made within the preceding five years, and earnings on those contributions, are not eligible for rollover. A $95,000 front-load made in year 1 does not become rollover-eligible until year 6, and only the pre-year-1 corpus is eligible.
  3. $35,000 lifetime cap per beneficiary. Total rollovers across the beneficiary's lifetime cannot exceed $35,000. This is a per-beneficiary cap, not a per-year cap or per-account cap.
  4. Annual IRA contribution-limit stack. The rollover counts against the beneficiary's IRA contribution limit for the year. For 2026, that is $7,500 (under age 50) or $8,600 (age 50+) per IRS Notice 2025-67. A beneficiary rolling $7,500 from §529 to Roth IRA in 2026 cannot also make a separate Roth IRA contribution for that year.[19]
  5. Earned-income requirement. The beneficiary must have earned income at least equal to the rollover amount for the year. A 20-year-old college student with $6,000 of summer W-2 income can roll only $6,000 to Roth IRA that year, not the full $7,500 limit.

The interaction with §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging is real but narrow. A $95,000 front-load in year 1 becomes fully-rollover-eligible in year 16 at the earliest (15-year account age plus one-year cushion, subject to the beneficiary's earned-income and IRA-contribution capacity). For a grandparent front-loading a grandchild's §529 at the grandchild's birth, the rollover window opens in year 15 when the grandchild is age 15 — three to seven years before the grandchild typically has meaningful earned income. Practical utilization of §529(c)(3)(E) for a young-beneficiary account typically begins in the beneficiary's early-20s post-college years, funded gradually against annual IRA limits.[18]

Interaction with QTIP-held inherited IRAs under the SECURE Act 10-year rule

The direct interaction of §529(c)(5)(A) with a QTIP-held inherited IRA is indirect but material for practitioners in the retirement-inheritance planning arc.[20] The QTIP trust itself is a §2056(b)(7) marital-deduction structure at the deceased spouse's death and is walked in the §2056(b)(7) piece. The SECURE Act 10-year rule at IRC §401(a)(9)(H) forces the inherited IRA held by a QTIP trust to distribute fully by December 31 of the tenth calendar year after the deceased spouse's death, generating substantial ordinary-income cash flow to the trust over the ten-year window.

Three planning scenarios connect the QTIP inherited-IRA cash flow to §529(c)(5)(A) front-loading:

  1. Surviving-spouse §529 gifting from QTIP distributions. QTIP trust income distributions to the surviving spouse during the 10-year IRA distribution window generate ordinary-income cash flow at the surviving spouse's marginal bracket. Redirecting that cash flow into §529 accounts for grandchildren — using annual-exclusion pacing or five-year front-loading — removes the corpus from the surviving spouse's own estate at the surviving spouse's later death, avoiding the second 40% estate-tax layer that would otherwise apply.
  2. QTIP-corpus §529 gifting via principal invasion. Some QTIP trusts permit trustee discretion to invade corpus for the surviving spouse's benefit (or in the surviving spouse's discretion under a §2041(b)(1)(A) ascertainable-standard power). Distributions taken as principal invasions and then contributed to grandchildren's §529 accounts via §529(c)(5)(A) front-loading move corpus out of the QTIP structure before the deceased spouse's remainder beneficiaries receive it — a structural coordination decision the trustee should model against the remainderman-benefit interest.
  3. §529 as a partial substitute for QTIP §2519 disposition. A surviving spouse considering an IRC §2519 disposition of a QTIP-held inherited IRA (walked in the §2519 piece) sometimes has grandchild-college-funding as the underlying motivation. Direct §529(c)(5)(A) front-loading, funded from personal assets rather than through QTIP disposition, avoids the §2519 gift-tax constructive-transfer mechanic while achieving the same substantive family goal.

Worked case study: Margaret, age 68, four grandchildren, §5M net worth

Margaret is 68 and a Colorado resident. Her husband David died in 2024, leaving her with a $5,000,000 net worth including a $1.4M inherited-IRA rollover (Margaret elected spousal rollover to her own traditional IRA rather than remain as inherited-IRA beneficiary, so no SECURE Act 10-year clock applies). She has four grandchildren: Aiden (age 14), Sophia (age 11), Jack (age 8), and Emma (age 4). She has $850,000 of annual RMD-driven cash flow accumulating in a taxable brokerage account and wants to accelerate college funding for all four grandchildren.

Margaret elects Option B. She and her advisor determine that the $10,450 expected pullback exposure is a strong value trade against the removal of $380,000 from her estate at her later death (avoiding $152,000 in future estate tax at the 40% rate assuming her $15M unified credit is otherwise consumed by other transfers) plus the $15,200 in immediate Colorado state-tax savings plus 14+ years of tax-free compounding on the four allocations. The Form 709 filing shows $380,000 total contributions on Schedule A with the §529(c)(5)(A) election statement identifying $95,000 per grandchild averaged across years 2026-2030. Margaret files Form 709 for calendar years 2027, 2028, 2029, and 2030 as well (each showing the deemed $76,000 installment on Schedule A even though no cash moves in those years) to properly close the paper trail on the elected averaging.

Six recurring §529(c)(5)(A) mistakes

  1. Failing to file Form 709 for the initial-year election. The §529(c)(5)(A) election is made on the timely-filed Form 709 for the calendar year of the contribution. A contributor who front-loads $95,000 to a §529 account and simply "does not file" Form 709 has not made the election — the $95,000 is a current-year taxable gift consuming $76,000 of §2010(c) lifetime unified credit and (if the beneficiary is a skip person) requires §2632 GST-exemption allocation to preserve zero-inclusion-ratio treatment. §9100 relief is available under Rev. Proc. 2025-1 at the standard $38,000 user fee, but the fix is expensive and time-consuming versus filing correctly in the first place.[13]
  2. Making additional gifts to the same beneficiary during the averaging window. Once a five-year averaging election is filed for a beneficiary, that beneficiary's annual exclusion is fully consumed in each of years 1-5. Any additional gift to that beneficiary during the window — a $5,000 birthday check, a $10,000 wedding gift, a $19,000 direct §529 contribution — is either a current-year taxable gift consuming §2010(c) unified credit or a §2632-exemption-consuming skip transfer if the beneficiary is a skip person. Practitioners should confirm no other family-member gifting to the beneficiary is planned for the five-year window before finalizing the election.
  3. Missing the §2513 spousal-election-consent requirement. The doubled $190,000 front-load requires the §2513 spousal gift-splitting election, which requires both spouses to file Form 709 with the consent-of-spouse box checked. A common failure mode is one spouse filing Form 709 without the other spouse's timely-filed consent, leaving the second spouse's half of the elected front-load treated as a gift from the first spouse only — potentially exceeding the first spouse's own $95,000 cap and triggering current-year taxable-gift treatment.[11]
  4. Front-loading at advanced age without pullback modeling. A contributor age 80+ front-loading $95,000 has a material probability (28-42%) of triggering the §529(c)(4)(C) pullback. Some contributors execute the election without modeling the pullback exposure and are surprised at the estate-inclusion consequence at the contributor's later death. Prudent practice is to model pullback exposure against the contributor's actual five-year mortality probability and to prefer §2503(e) direct-pay or annual-exclusion pacing when the contributor is 80+ or has meaningful health issues.[6]
  5. Changing beneficiary to a non-family member. Changing the §529 beneficiary to someone outside the §529(e)(2) family definition (a family friend, a domestic partner not legally married to the beneficiary, a beneficiary's stepsibling's spouse) triggers a distribution-plus-new-gift treatment — the earnings portion becomes taxable to the prior beneficiary and subject to the 10% §529(c)(6) additional tax, and the entire account is treated as a new gift from the prior beneficiary to the new beneficiary. Account owners should confirm family-member status before executing any beneficiary change.[15]
  6. Assuming §529(c)(3)(E) Roth-rollover is immediately available. The 15-year account age and five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback under §529(c)(3)(E) mean that a newly-opened §529 account is not rollover-eligible for at least 15 years. Practitioners frequently overstate the near-term flexibility of §529(c)(3)(E) — the mechanic is real but slow to mature. For a §529 account opened at a grandchild's birth, meaningful rollover capacity opens when the grandchild is age 15-18, and beneficiary earned-income constraints often further defer utilization to age 22+.[18]

Eight-item pre-election action checklist

  1. Confirm the contributor's five-year mortality probability against the SSA 2024 Actuarial Life Table. Under age 70: pullback risk is negligible. Age 70-79: pullback risk is meaningful but generally acceptable. Age 80+: prefer staged funding or §2503(e) direct-pay.
  2. Confirm no other five-year-window gifting is planned to the same beneficiary from the same or spousal contributor. Any additional gift within the five-year averaging window consumes §2010(c) unified credit (or §2632 exemption for skip persons).
  3. Model the §529(c)(4)(C) pullback exposure against the contributor's estate-tax posture. For contributors with $15M+ estate exposure at 40% rates, the pullback is a real cost; for contributors with estates well below the $15M unified credit, the pullback is essentially costless.
  4. Determine whether §2513 spousal gift-splitting is appropriate. The election doubles the front-load capacity but is all-or-nothing for the calendar year. Both spouses must file Form 709 with the consent box checked.
  5. Verify the state 529 plan supports the elected mechanic. Most state plans support §529(c)(5)(A) mechanically, but state-level 529 tax deductions may have annual caps below the federal $95,000 (e.g., Colorado's deduction is capped at the state contribution limit, currently $22,700 for single filers). File separately for state-level deduction carryforward where available.
  6. Coordinate with the §2642(c)(1) analysis if the beneficiary is a skip person. Confirm each deemed annual installment qualifies as a §2503(b) present-interest gift (§529 contributions automatically qualify under §529(c)(2)(A)) and that §2642(c)(2) trust-rule complications do not apply (they do not for §529 accounts).
  7. File Form 709 for the initial-year election with the §529(c)(5)(A) election statement clearly identifying the contribution amount, beneficiary, plan account number, and election of five-year averaging. File Form 709 for each of years 2-5 (whether or not otherwise required) to document the deemed annual installments.
  8. Document the beneficiary-change plan and confirm §529(e)(2) family-member status for any anticipated future changes. Keep a written record of the family relationship between the current beneficiary and any planned successor beneficiary; if the potential change would move to a non-family member, plan the funding structure to avoid the change instead.

Frequently asked questions

What is IRC §529(c)(5)(A) five-year averaging and why does it matter?

IRC §529(c)(5)(A) permits a contributor to a §529 qualified tuition program to elect to treat a single-year contribution of up to five times the §2503(b) annual exclusion as if made ratably over five calendar years. For 2026, this permits a single-year front-load of $95,000 per contributor per beneficiary ($19,000 × 5 under Rev. Proc. 2025-32); a married couple electing §2513 gift-splitting can front-load $190,000. The election is made on Form 709 for the year of the contribution. The mechanic accelerates five years of annual-exclusion capacity into a single year while preserving §2503(b) treatment for each deemed installment.

How does the §529(c)(4)(C) death-within-five-years pullback work?

If the contributor dies before the close of the fifth calendar year in the averaging period, the pro-rata portion of the front-loaded contribution attributable to calendar years beginning after the year of death is includible in the contributor's gross estate under IRC §2001. Example: front-load $95,000 in year 1, die during year 3, the two installments attributable to years 4 and 5 ($19,000 × 2 = $38,000) are pulled into the estate; the three installments already deemed made remain outside. Only the pro-rata unused portion is pulled back, not the entire contribution.

How does the §529(e)(2) member-of-family definition work?

§529(c)(3)(C) permits the account owner to change the designated beneficiary tax-free if the new beneficiary is a §529(e)(2) "member of the family" of the prior beneficiary. The definition is broad: spouse, children/descendants, stepchildren, siblings, parents/ancestors, aunts/uncles, nieces/nephews, in-laws, first cousins, and the spouse of any of the above. Changes to non-family members trigger distribution-plus-new-gift treatment. Changes to a younger generation are generation-skipping transfers requiring §2642(c) or §2632 analysis.

How does SECURE 2.0 §126 interact with the five-year averaging election?

SECURE 2.0 §126 added IRC §529(c)(3)(E), permitting a tax-free trustee-to-trustee rollover of unused §529 funds to a Roth IRA for the designated beneficiary, effective for distributions after 2023. Lifetime cap: $35,000 per beneficiary. Requirements: 15-year account age, five-year contribution-and-earnings lookback, IRA-contribution-limit stack, and beneficiary earned-income requirement. A §529 account opened with a five-year front-load in year 1 does not become rollover-eligible until year 16 at the earliest.

How does the §2513 spousal gift-splitting election coordinate with §529(c)(5)(A)?

Combined with §529(c)(5)(A), the §2513 election permits a total single-year contribution of $190,000 per beneficiary for 2026. Each spouse files Form 709 with the spousal-consent box checked and files a §529(c)(5)(A) election statement for their $95,000 share. The §529(c)(4)(C) pullback applies separately to each spouse. The §2513 election is all-or-nothing for the calendar year — if elected, all gifts by either spouse to third parties for the year must be split.

How does §529(c)(5)(A) interact with §2642(c)(1) zero-inclusion-ratio treatment for GST tax?

A direct §529 contribution where the beneficiary is a grandchild or more remote descendant is a direct-skip transfer under §2612(c). Each of the five deemed installments qualifies as a §2503(b) annual-exclusion nontaxable gift, which under §2642(c)(3)(A) is eligible for §2642(c)(1) zero-inclusion-ratio treatment. Because a §529 account is not a "trust" for §2642(c)(2) restrictive-rule purposes, the single-beneficiary and estate-inclusion requirements do not apply. A $95,000 five-year front-load to a grandchild §529 passes GST-tax-free without consuming §2632 exemption.

What happens if the account owner takes a non-qualified distribution during the averaging period?

Non-qualified distributions during the five-year averaging period do not affect the gift-tax averaging election but trigger the standard §529(c)(6) non-qualified-distribution consequences on the earnings portion: federal income tax plus 10% additional tax. The account owner is generally the taxpayer of record. The deemed annual installments to the beneficiary are already treated as completed present-interest gifts and cannot be recaptured, even if the corpus is later returned to the account owner via non-qualified distribution.

Can I make additional §529 contributions during the five-year averaging period?

Yes, but additional contributions to the same beneficiary during years 2-5 consume the annual exclusion for that beneficiary that year (or, more precisely, exceed it — since the entire annual exclusion is already consumed by the averaged deemed installment). A $50,000 front-load leaves $9,000/year of additional annual-exclusion capacity per year for the next four years; a $95,000 front-load consumes the full annual exclusion for each of years 1-5.

How do I file the §529(c)(5)(A) election on Form 709?

The election is made on Form 709 for the calendar year of the contribution. Report the full contribution on Schedule A (Part 1 or Part 2 for direct-skip gifts) as a gift to the designated beneficiary, then attach an election statement specifying the total contribution, beneficiary identity, §529 account number, and the specific election under §529(c)(5)(A) to treat the contribution as made ratably over five years. File Form 709 for each of years 2-5 showing the deemed annual installment on Schedule A. Rev. Proc. 2025-1 §5 governs §9100 relief for missed elections at $38,000 user fee.

What if I contribute more than $95,000 to a single §529 account in a single year?

Contributions above $95,000 per contributor per beneficiary are taxable gifts in the year made. The excess consumes the contributor's $15,000,000 (2026) lifetime unified gift-and-estate exemption under §2010(c), and if the beneficiary is a skip person, also consumes §2632 GST exemption. Contributors with sufficient unified-credit runway routinely contribute above the $95,000 cap for grandparents with $5M+ in §2010(c) capacity, because the §529 account provides income-tax-free growth that would otherwise sit in the grandparent's own estate accruing estate-taxable investment income.
