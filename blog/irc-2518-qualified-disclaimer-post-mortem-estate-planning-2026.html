The §2518 qualified disclaimer is the highest-leverage post-mortem estate-planning tool in the Internal Revenue Code. Correctly executed, a disclaimer accomplishes a full property transfer from the disclaimant to the next taker — with no gift-tax consequence, no consumption of the disclaimant's Applicable Exclusion Amount under §2010(c), no §2035 three-year gross-up on the disclaimant's own gross estate, and no §2513 spousal-consent gift split. The disclaimant is treated for federal transfer-tax purposes as though she never received the interest at all.[1]

Why §2518 exists: the transferor-preserving principle

Before Congress added §2518 to the Code in the Tax Reform Act of 1976, the federal transfer-tax treatment of a disclaimer was a common-law question that varied by state.[3] Some states treated a disclaimant as a transferor (making the redirection a taxable gift); others treated the disclaimant as never having received the interest (making the redirection tax-free). Taxpayers who lived in the second group of states could achieve post-mortem redirection results that were unavailable to identically-situated taxpayers in the first group. Congress ended the disparity by enacting §2518 as a uniform federal-tax-recognition standard: a disclaimer that satisfies four objective elements is respected as a non-event for the disclaimant, regardless of what any state's common law would say.

The design principle is transferor-preservation. §2518 is not a redirection statute — the disclaimant may not name the next taker, may not direct the timing or amount of the redirection, and may not retain any residual power over the disposition (except the narrow §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception discussed below). What the disclaimer accomplishes is the elimination of the disclaimant as a taker from the transferor's original disposition plan; the property then passes to the next taker as specified by the transferor in the will, trust, or beneficiary designation, or, if the transferor did not name a contingent taker, under the state's intestacy statute.

This is why §2518 is a post-mortem estate-planning tool — it lets the transferor's original plan continue to operate through the contingent-taker layer without requiring the disclaimant to make a lifetime gift. But it is also why the disclaimer's power is limited: the disclaimant cannot invent a new next taker. If the transferor's will names the son as primary and no contingent taker, the son's §2518 disclaimer causes the property to pass by intestacy to the son's siblings or up to the son's parents (if living) — not to the son's children unless the intestacy statute happens to route through them by representation.

The transferor-preservation principle in one line

§2518 does not let the disclaimant redirect property to a person of her choosing. It lets the disclaimant remove herself from the transferor's original plan, so that the property passes to whomever the transferor named as the contingent taker (or as intestate successor). The higher-leverage §2518 disclaimers are always ones where the transferor's original plan already routes to a preferred contingent taker — a testamentary bypass trust, a grandchildren-take-by-representation clause, or a state intestacy statute that produces the desired result.

The §2518 statutory framework

IRC §2518(a) reads: "For purposes of this subtitle, if a person makes a qualified disclaimer with respect to any interest in property, this subtitle shall apply with respect to such interest as if the interest had never been transferred to such person." "This subtitle" is Subtitle B of the Code — the estate, gift, and generation-skipping transfer tax provisions. The disclaimant is treated as never having received the interest for estate, gift, and GST purposes; other tax provisions (income tax, e.g. under §61 or §691) apply independently.[4]

IRC §2518(b) defines the four elements of a qualified disclaimer. All four must be satisfied; failure on any one recharacterizes the attempted disclaimer as an inter vivos gift by the disclaimant to whoever ends up with the property. The four elements:

  • §2518(b)(1) — Irrevocable and unqualified writing. The disclaimer must be in writing, signed by the disclaimant, irrevocable once delivered, and unqualified — no conditions, no reservations, no "if the property turns out to be worth less than X" clauses. A conditional or contingent disclaimer is not a §2518 disclaimer.
  • §2518(b)(2) — Nine-month deadline. The writing must be received by the transferor of the interest (or the transferor's legal representative) not later than nine months after the later of (A) the day the transfer creating the interest is made or (B) the day the disclaimant attains age 21. For a testamentary transfer, the "day the transfer is made" is the day of death, not the day the will is probated or the day the executor takes office.
  • §2518(b)(3) — No acceptance of the interest or any of its benefits. The disclaimant may not have accepted the interest or any of its benefits before executing the disclaimer. Acceptance is read broadly under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d) to include any exercise of dominion or control, any receipt of income or principal, any use of the property, or any act by which the disclaimant treats the property as her own.
  • §2518(b)(4) — Passage without direction. As a result of the disclaimer, the property must pass either to the transferor's surviving spouse or to a person other than the disclaimant, and it must pass without any direction on the part of the disclaimant.[5]

The four-part test in operational form

A §2518 diagnostic runs each of the four elements as a checkbox against the specific facts of the disclaimer. All four must clear before the disclaimant executes:

What property can be disclaimed

§2518 reaches "any interest in property" and Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(a)(1) reads the term broadly. The following property types are routinely disclaimed in modern practice:

Real estate

A residence, vacation home, farm, undeveloped land, or commercial building bequeathed under a will or held in a revocable living trust is squarely §2518-disclaimable. The disclaimer document is executed by the beneficiary, delivered to the executor of the decedent's estate or the trustee of the funding trust, and recorded in the county land records under state UDPIA or equivalent state disclaimer statute to establish record title. The nine-month clock runs from the date of death. A common trap is a disclaimant who has moved into the bequeathed residence in the interim — that use is §2518(b)(3) acceptance and destroys the disclaimer. A disclaimant who receives rent from a bequeathed rental property (the executor collects rent post-death and distributes to the beneficiary) is likewise in §2518(b)(3) trouble.

Brokerage and bank accounts

Beneficiary designations on transfer-on-death (TOD) brokerage accounts, payable-on-death (POD) bank accounts, and joint-with-right-of-survivorship (JTWROS) accounts are disclaimable. The nine-month clock runs from the date of the transferor's death (for TOD and POD accounts, the "transfer" occurs at death). For JTWROS accounts, the analysis is more subtle — the survivor's fractional interest existed pre-death and only the decedent's fractional share is disclaimable, unless state law treats the survivorship interest as arising at death (in which case the entire account is disclaimable up to the deceased owner's contributed share). The disclaimer is delivered to the custodian, which then re-titles the account to the next taker under the account's beneficiary designation cascade or (if no contingent beneficiary named) to the decedent's estate.

Life insurance proceeds

Death benefits paid to a named beneficiary under a life insurance policy are §2518-disclaimable. This is one of the highest-value disclaimer uses — a primary beneficiary whose own estate is exhausting the $15M §2010(c) exemption can disclaim the death benefit, causing the proceeds to pass to the contingent beneficiary (or to the insured's estate) without gift-tax consequence. The nine-month clock runs from the date of death. The disclaimer is delivered to the insurance carrier before the death benefit is paid — a disclaimer executed after receipt of the proceeds is §2518(b)(3) acceptance and is void.

Will and trust bequests

Specific bequests, pecuniary bequests, and residuary bequests under a will are all §2518-disclaimable, subject to Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3's partial-disclaimer mechanics for severable portions. A disclaimer of a $200,000 specific pecuniary bequest under a will causes that $200,000 to pour to the residuary; a disclaimer of the residuary itself causes the property to pass to whoever the residuary contingent takers are named (or to intestate successors).

Tangible personal property

Jewelry, artwork, collectibles, household furnishings, and other tangible personalty is disclaimable. The §2518(b)(3) no-acceptance test is particularly sensitive here because possession and use are the ordinary ways one enjoys tangible property — a beneficiary who takes possession of a painting bequeathed under the will, or who displays inherited jewelry, is in acceptance and cannot later disclaim.

Retirement accounts (by cross-reference)

Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, 401(k) accounts, 403(b) accounts, and non-qualified deferred comp interests are §2518-disclaimable, but the mechanics are governed by an overlay of Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4 designated-beneficiary rules, the SECURE Act §401 and SECURE 2.0 §110 modifications, and Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-3 five-year and ten-year rules. Because those rules interact with the §2518 disclaimer analysis in ways that are unique to retirement accounts — the year-of-death RMD trap under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(4) Example 4, the October-31-following-death paperwork deadline for designated-beneficiary treatment, and the SECURE Act 10-year rule for QTIP-held inherited IRAs — retirement-account disclaimers are treated in the specialized companion piece.

The state-law UDPIA overlay

§2518 is a federal-tax-recognition standard. It does NOT itself transfer property. What actually moves the property from the disclaimant to the next taker is the state's property-law disclaimer statute, applied against the transferor's will, trust, beneficiary designation, or intestacy statute. The two must both succeed: state law must effectively transfer the property, and §2518 must recognize the transfer as non-taxable.[6]

The Uniform Disclaimer of Property Interests Act of 1999 (UDPIA), with amendments through 2006 and 2010, is the model statute the Uniform Law Commission promulgated to provide state-law effect to disclaimers. UDPIA has been adopted in roughly 30 states and the District of Columbia — including large jurisdictions such as California, Florida, Illinois, Massachusetts, Michigan, New Jersey, New York (as EPTL §2-1.11), North Carolina, Ohio, and Texas. Non-UDPIA states have their own disclaimer statutes that vary in mechanics but generally reach similar results.[7]

The federal / state timing mismatch

UDPIA §5 provides that a disclaimer is effective under state law when "delivered" per §12, with no fixed time limit — a UDPIA disclaimer is effective under state law whenever the beneficiary chooses to execute and deliver it, subject only to the requirement that it be delivered before the beneficiary has accepted the interest under state property-law standards. Some non-UDPIA states have their own timing rules — Louisiana Code Civ. Proc. Art. 947 gives one year, Pennsylvania 20 Pa.C.S. §6203 gives nine months, and a handful of states impose no time limit at all.

The federal-tax nine-month deadline under §2518(b)(2) is independent of and often stricter than the state timing rule. A California disclaimer delivered on day 275 is valid under Cal. Prob. Code §280 (California UDPIA equivalent) and effectively transfers the property to the next taker under state law. But because it was delivered after the federal nine-month window closed, §2518(a) does not recognize the transfer — the disclaimant is treated as having accepted the interest and then made an inter vivos gift to the next taker under §2511, consuming her §2010(c) exemption and potentially triggering gift tax under §2001(c).

This is the single most-cited state-federal mismatch in disclaimer practice. A beneficiary who relies on a state-law bar's advice ("California has no nine-month rule for disclaimers") without the accompanying federal-tax overlay will often execute a state-valid disclaimer that is federally recharacterized as a gift. The cure is the federal nine-month deadline, which controls for federal-tax purposes regardless of what state law allows.

Community property and forced-heirship overlays

Nine states operate community property regimes: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. Alaska and Tennessee provide opt-in community property regimes via community property trust statutes. In community property states, the surviving spouse already owns her one-half interest in the community estate at the moment of the first spouse's death — she cannot disclaim what she already owns. She may disclaim (i) the decedent's one-half share of the community property, if bequeathed to her under the decedent's will or under state community property succession rules, and (ii) any separate property bequeathed to her under the will.[8]

Mechanically, the surviving spouse's disclaimer document must clearly identify the disclaimed interest (typically "the decedent's one-half interest in the community property listed on Schedule A") and preserve the survivor's own one-half interest as not disclaimed. Failure to sever cleanly can produce a disclaimer that is arguably over-broad (attempting to disclaim community property the survivor already owned) or ambiguous under state law — either result may cause the state-law transfer to fail and, transitively, the federal-tax recognition to fail as well.

Louisiana forced heirship

Louisiana's civil-law regime under La. Civ. Code Art. 1493 imposes forced-heirship: the decedent's children under age 24 (or disabled at any age) are entitled to a mandatory share (the "forced portion") of the decedent's estate, and the decedent may not disinherit them except for statutory just cause under Art. 1621. A Louisiana disclaimer by a forced heir of the forced portion is void as against the forced-heirship claim — the forced portion re-enters the estate and passes as though the disclaimant continued to own it. Non-forced-portion interests may be disclaimed freely under La. Civ. Proc. Art. 947.

The §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception

The general §2518(b)(4) passage-without-direction rule prohibits the disclaimant from receiving any residual interest in the disclaimed property. But IRC §2518(c)(3) creates an important exception for the surviving spouse: a surviving spouse may disclaim an interest in property AND retain a life-income interest, a discretionary distribution power under an ascertainable standard, or another beneficial interest in the disclaimed property so long as the disclaimed property passes under a trust structure that was created by the transferor (not by the disclaimant).[9]

This is the exception that makes the most powerful post-mortem estate-planning move possible: a surviving spouse disclaims an outright bequest into a decedent-drafted bypass trust or QTIP trust in which she is a lifetime beneficiary. She achieves two results simultaneously — the outright bequest is redirected to the bypass trust that consumes the decedent's Applicable Exclusion Amount and shelters the underlying property from her later estate under §2044 (or, if a bypass trust rather than QTIP, escapes §2044 inclusion entirely) — while she remains the beneficiary with lifetime access to income and, under an ascertainable standard, discretionary access to principal.

The §2518(c)(3) exception in one line

A surviving spouse can eat her cake and have it too: disclaim into a bypass trust for the estate-tax savings, while retaining income and ascertainable-standard principal access as a lifetime beneficiary — so long as the trust structure was drafted by the decedent (not amended into being by the survivor after death) and the survivor's beneficial interest under the trust is capped at what §2518(c)(3) permits.

Bypass-trust drafting for §2518 flexibility

Testamentary bypass trusts drafted with §2518 flexibility in mind typically include a "disclaimer clause" in the will or trust: language providing that if the surviving spouse disclaims all or any portion of the outright marital bequest, the disclaimed portion pours into the bypass trust (drafted to use the decedent's remaining Applicable Exclusion Amount). Well-drafted disclaimer clauses (a) name the bypass trust as the contingent taker for any disclaimed portion, (b) preserve the surviving spouse's beneficial interest under the bypass trust for §2518(c)(3) compliance, (c) authorize the executor to compute the exact pecuniary amount that maximizes the decedent's exemption use, and (d) permit the surviving spouse to disclaim iteratively across successive interests if the exact amount cannot be determined pre-disclaimer.

Three worked case studies for 2026 disclaimers

Case 1: Marital-deduction preservation — Karen and the $8M outright bequest

Karen is 68. Her husband David died on May 4, 2026 in Illinois, leaving under his will a $8M outright marital bequest to Karen and a residuary trust to the couple's two adult children. David's remaining §2010(c) Applicable Exclusion Amount at death was $15M (David made no taxable lifetime gifts). David's will includes a disclaimer clause providing that any portion of the marital bequest disclaimed by Karen pours into a testamentary credit-shelter (bypass) trust with Karen as lifetime beneficiary under a HEMS (health-education-maintenance-support) ascertainable standard.

Karen's own separate assets total $9M and her likely additional wealth accumulation over her expected 20-year post-death life is $3M more. If Karen accepts the $8M outright, her projected gross estate at her later death is $8M + $9M + $3M = $20M, of which $5M exceeds her own $15M exemption. Federal estate tax at 40% on the $5M excess is $2M — a family-level second-death cost.

Karen's disclaimer mechanics. Karen retains estate-planning counsel by June 10, 2026 (five weeks after death). Counsel drafts a §2518-compliant disclaimer of $8M of the marital bequest, delivered to the executor of David's estate on August 15, 2026 (three months and eleven days after death — well inside the nine-month §2518(b)(2) window). Between May 4 and August 15, Karen has taken no distribution from the marital bequest — the executor has held the bequest as executor property, not distributed to Karen — so the §2518(b)(3) no-acceptance test is clean. Under the will's disclaimer clause the $8M pours into the bypass trust with Karen as HEMS beneficiary; the §2518(c)(3) exception permits her retained beneficial interest.

Family net effect. The disclaimer preserves $8M of David's Applicable Exclusion Amount inside the bypass trust, which grows over Karen's remaining 20 years without §2044 inclusion at her death. Karen retains full lifetime access to income (mandatory) and to principal for HEMS purposes (discretionary, standard-limited). Her own estate at death is smaller by the $8M that would otherwise have been outright — that $8M is sheltered inside the bypass trust and passes to the children free of estate tax at her later death. The family-level second-death saving is on the order of $800K in 2036 dollars, or roughly $530K in present value at a 4.5% discount rate.

Why full disclaimer beats partial in this fact pattern. Full disclaimer of the $8M marital bequest fully uses David's $15M exemption to the extent of the disclaimed portion; the residual $7M ports to Karen as DSUE. Under alternative 3, a $5M partial disclaimer uses less of David's direct exemption but ports more DSUE — the mathematical result at Karen's later death is nearly identical because the DSUE is indexed identically to the direct-use exemption. But partial disclaimer preserves marginal flexibility for Karen if she needs the $3M outright — the trade is against slight state-tax exposure in Illinois (Illinois has a $4M estate-tax threshold with progressive rates under 35 ILCS 405, so a bypass trust that grows above $4M inside Karen's Illinois residency exposes the growth to Illinois estate tax at her death). In Karen's case, counsel recommends the full $8M disclaimer because the family's expected residence is Illinois for the survivor's 20-year window and the state-tax exposure inside the bypass trust is not materially different than the exposure that would otherwise attach to the accepted marital portion in Karen's own estate.

Case 2: GST leverage — Marcus disclaims to grandchildren

Marcus is 62. His father Harold died on March 12, 2026 in Texas, leaving under his will a $2M pecuniary bequest to Marcus and residuary bequests to Harold's other children. Harold made no taxable lifetime gifts; his §2631(a) GST exemption at death is the full $15M (indexed 2026 amount). Marcus's own estate is projected at $18M at his death — already above the §2010(c) exemption threshold — and Marcus has three adult children (Harold's grandchildren) who each face their own future estate exposure.

Marcus's disclaimer analysis: if Marcus accepts the $2M pecuniary bequest, his own gross estate grows by $2M plus appreciation. At Marcus's projected 20-year remaining life and a 4% real growth rate, the $2M bequest becomes $4.38M in Marcus's gross estate at his death. Federal estate tax at 40% on the $4.38M excess is $1.75M — a compound loss across two generations.

Alternative: Marcus disclaims the $2M pecuniary bequest under Harold's will. Harold's will names Marcus's three children (Harold's grandchildren) as the contingent takers of any bequest Marcus disclaims. Marcus's disclaimer is delivered on October 10, 2026 (six months and 28 days after death, inside the nine-month window). Under Harold's will the $2M pours in equal shares to the three grandchildren — $666,667 each.

The GST tax analysis: because Harold is Marcus's parent and the grandchildren are two generations below Harold, the transfer to the grandchildren via Marcus's disclaimer is a "generation-skipping transfer" under §2611 subject to GST tax. But Harold's executor allocates $2M of Harold's §2631(a) GST exemption to the disclaimed pecuniary bequest on Harold's Form 706, producing a zero inclusion ratio under §2642(a)(1). The transfer to the grandchildren is fully GST-exempt.[10]

Marcus's disclaimer is drafted by his estate-planning counsel to identify the specific $2M pecuniary bequest, to be irrevocable and unqualified, and to be delivered to Harold's executor along with a request that Harold's executor confirm the disclaimer clause under the will directs the $2M to the grandchildren. On Harold's Form 706 (due December 12, 2026, nine months after death), the executor allocates $2M of Harold's §2631 GST exemption to the disclaimed pecuniary bequest and reports the disclaimer under Schedule R-1 and a Notice of Allocation. The grandchildren receive $666,667 each in December 2026.

Why disclaimer beats the alternative "accept and re-gift" path. A common but suboptimal alternative to §2518 disclaimer is for Marcus to accept the $2M and then make a lifetime gift of $2M to the grandchildren. Marcus's own §2010(c) exemption is consumed by $2M; Marcus's own §2631 GST exemption is consumed by $2M; and any subsequent appreciation happens outside Marcus's estate but at the cost of $2M of Marcus's exemption. The §2518 path preserves Marcus's exemption for his own future gifts, uses Harold's exemption instead (which would otherwise be lost at Harold's death if not fully utilized), and produces the same generation-skipping economic result without touching Marcus's exemption at all.

Case 3: Creditor and Medicaid preservation — Elena disclaims the $4M life insurance benefit

Elena is 71 in Michigan. Her sister Alicia died on January 20, 2026, leaving Elena as the primary beneficiary of a $4M term life insurance policy. Alicia's contingent beneficiary is the Alicia Family Trust, a discretionary trust for Alicia's four adult children (Elena's nieces and nephews). Elena is admitted to a skilled nursing facility on February 12, 2026, having been diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's. Elena applies for Michigan Medicaid nursing-home benefits on March 1, 2026.

Elena's Medicaid eligibility analysis: Michigan Medicaid's asset test (as of 2026) permits an individual applicant to hold $2,000 in countable resources plus specific exempt assets. If Elena accepts the $4M life insurance benefit, her countable resources jump to approximately $4,000,000 and she is immediately Medicaid-ineligible, requiring her to pay privately for nursing care at approximately $12,000/month (Michigan average as of Q2 2026) — spending down the $4M over 27+ years until she becomes Medicaid-eligible again.

Elena's counsel recommends a §2518 disclaimer of the life insurance benefit. The disclaimer is delivered to Alicia's life insurance carrier on July 15, 2026 (five months and 25 days after Alicia's death) — inside the nine-month §2518(b)(2) window. The disclaimer is executed by Elena's court-appointed conservator with Michigan Probate Court approval (required for Medicaid-related disclaimers under Michigan MCL 700.2911 and the state UDPIA equivalent). Under Alicia's beneficiary designation, the $4M pours to the Alicia Family Trust (the contingent beneficiary) and is distributed at the trustee's discretion among Alicia's four adult children over the next several years.

The Medicaid disqualifying-transfer trap. Here's the critical federal-vs-state layer: for federal §2518 purposes, Elena is treated as never having received the $4M. But for Michigan Medicaid purposes, the disclaimer is a "transfer for less than fair market value" under 42 U.S.C. §1396p(c)(1) and Michigan Bridges Eligibility Manual (BEM) 405.3, which counts a disclaimer of an inheritance as a Medicaid-disqualifying transfer subject to the 60-month look-back period. Elena's disclaimer causes her to lose Medicaid eligibility for a penalty period computed as $4,000,000 ÷ Michigan's monthly private-pay rate ($12,000) = 333 months — beyond her expected lifetime.[11]

The cure and the sharpened lesson. Because the Medicaid penalty period would render Elena permanently ineligible, her counsel structures the disclaimer around a different result: instead of a straight §2518 disclaimer, counsel arranges for the Alicia Family Trust to include a "special-needs" sub-share for Elena that provides supplemental support (not primary support) for her lifetime while preserving her Medicaid eligibility under the 42 U.S.C. §1396p(d)(4)(A) or (d)(4)(C) special-needs-trust exception. The mechanics: Elena's disclaimer is honored under §2518 for federal-tax purposes (the $4M passes to the Alicia Family Trust cleanly); Alicia's trustee, on receipt, exercises decanting powers under Michigan MCL 700.7820a to create a special-needs sub-share for Elena's supplemental benefit. Elena's Michigan Medicaid eligibility is preserved because the special-needs sub-share is not a countable resource under 42 U.S.C. §1396p(d)(4).

The broader Case 3 lesson. §2518 disclaimers do not automatically preserve state benefits or protect against creditors. The Medicaid disqualifying-transfer rule applies to disclaimers just as it applies to outright gifts. Similarly, a disclaimer by a beneficiary who is in bankruptcy is generally set aside as a "fraudulent transfer" under state and federal fraudulent-transfer statutes and Bankruptcy Code §548. Practitioners running §2518 disclaimers for creditor-protection or benefits-preservation purposes MUST work the disclaimer through the applicable non-tax overlay (Medicaid, bankruptcy, ERISA §514, or state debtor-creditor law) before executing.[12]

Six most-expensive §2518 mistakes

The pattern of §2518 failures in 2026 practice is remarkably consistent. The six most common:

  1. Missing the nine-month deadline because the custodian is slow. Banks, brokerages, insurance carriers, and transfer agents routinely take 30-60 days to acknowledge death and begin re-titling. Beneficiaries who wait for the custodian to move first lose that time off the front of the fixed nine-month window. The cure: execute the disclaimer against the beneficial interest that vested at death (regardless of custodian paperwork), and deliver to the executor of the estate as the transferor's legal representative under §2518(b)(2).
  2. Accepting a small benefit before the disclaimer is executed. A single dividend check cashed, a single rent payment received and used, a single distribution accepted from the trust — any of these is §2518(b)(3) acceptance and destroys the disclaimer of the accepted asset in its entirety. The cure: freeze all distributions the day the beneficiary knows a disclaimer is possible. Keep post-death income in a segregated account under the executor's control until the disclaimer question is resolved.
  3. Naming the next taker in the disclaimer document. A disclaimer that says "I disclaim in favor of my son" or "I disclaim the property to be distributed equally to my children" fails §2518(b)(4) — the disclaimant is directing the passage. The cure: the disclaimer must simply refuse; the property passes automatically under the will, trust, beneficiary designation, or intestacy statute to whomever the transferor's disposition plan names.
  4. Missing the state / federal timing mismatch. A disclaimer delivered inside the state UDPIA time frame (which for UDPIA states is effectively "before acceptance") but outside the federal nine-month window is state-law valid and federal-tax invalid — the property does transfer to the next taker, but at the cost of a §2511 gift by the disclaimant. The cure: always run the federal nine-month clock, not the state clock, as the binding deadline.
  5. Failing to preserve §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse benefits. A surviving spouse who disclaims into a bypass trust must retain her beneficial interest under a trust structure that was drafted by the decedent (not created by the survivor). A disclaimer document that adds a new bypass trust — or that redirects to a trust the survivor amended into being — fails the §2518(c)(3) exception and produces a plain §2518(b)(4) violation. The cure: verify the bypass trust was established by the decedent's will or by a lifetime trust the decedent created; if there is no decedent-drafted bypass, the disclaimer's marital-deduction preservation goal cannot be achieved via §2518(c)(3).
  6. Ignoring the Medicaid or creditor overlay. A §2518 disclaimer is a valid federal-tax non-event but is a "transfer" for purposes of state Medicaid disqualifying-transfer rules, federal bankruptcy fraudulent-transfer rules, and most state debtor-creditor statutes. A disclaimer by a beneficiary facing any of those overlays must be structured to preserve the state or federal benefit through a special-needs sub-share, a bankruptcy-court-approved settlement, or a state-court fraudulent-transfer safe harbor. The cure: run the disclaimer through non-tax counsel (elder law, bankruptcy, creditor's rights) before executing.

The eight-item pre-disclaimer action checklist

Every 2026 executor and beneficiary considering a §2518 disclaimer should walk this checklist inside the first 60 days after death to preserve maximum flexibility. Waiting past 60 days consumes the front of the nine-month window and reduces the family's tactical options:

  1. Compute the exact nine-month deadline. Day of death plus nine calendar months, computed under the "corresponding date" rule — a February 4 death produces a November 4 deadline, not a November 3 or November 5. Where the calendar day does not exist in the ninth month (a March 30 death producing "December 30" is fine but a March 31 → November 31 → falls back to November 30), the rule is well-settled but jurisdictionally variable in edge cases. Enter the exact deadline in the file and in the beneficiary's own calendar.
  2. Freeze all distributions to the beneficiary. The moment the beneficiary knows a disclaimer is possible, freeze every income and principal distribution from every disclaimable interest. Instruct the executor, the trustee, and every custodian to hold all payments in a segregated non-beneficiary account until the disclaimer question is resolved. A single accepted distribution destroys the ability to disclaim that interest.
  3. Model the accept-vs-disclaim math. Compute the disclaimant's own projected gross estate at her death under both alternatives (accepting the inheritance vs disclaiming). Model federal estate tax at 40%, state estate tax where the disclaimant resides, and the family-level cumulative tax across two generations. The disclaimer's family net benefit is the delta between the two paths, discounted to present value at a reasonable rate (4-5% is conventional).
  4. Verify the transferor's disposition plan names a preferred contingent taker. Read the will, trust, beneficiary designation, and any state intestacy backup. Confirm the property will pass to the desired next taker upon disclaimer. If the disposition plan does not route the property to a preferred contingent taker, the disclaimer's redirection goal cannot be achieved — the disclaimer will send the property to whomever the transferor named or to intestate successors.
  5. Confirm §2518(c)(3) applicability for surviving-spouse disclaimers. Where the disclaimant is the surviving spouse and the goal is to disclaim into a bypass trust while retaining beneficial interest, verify the bypass trust structure was drafted by the decedent (will, revocable trust, or codicil). If no decedent-drafted bypass trust exists, the §2518(c)(3) exception cannot be invoked and the surviving spouse cannot retain a beneficial interest without violating §2518(b)(4).
  6. Run the state-law disclaimer statute compliance check. Determine whether the state is UDPIA (roughly 30 states) or non-UDPIA, whether the state has a fixed deadline (Illinois nine months, Pennsylvania nine months, Louisiana one year), and what state-specific delivery and recordation requirements apply. For real estate, prepare the county-clerk recordation filing.
  7. Run the non-tax overlay check. If the disclaimant faces any Medicaid, bankruptcy, creditor, or divorce exposure, engage non-tax counsel (elder-law, bankruptcy, creditor's rights, family-law) to run the disclaimer through the applicable overlay. Structure the disclaimer as a §1396p(d)(4)(A)/(C) special-needs sub-share redirect, bankruptcy-court-approved settlement, or other overlay-compliant path if the plain-vanilla §2518 disclaimer would trigger disqualification or clawback.
  8. Draft and deliver by day 200. Target delivery of the executed disclaimer document to the transferor's executor or legal representative by day 200 after death — allowing a 74-day buffer against the nine-month deadline for any state-law recordation, custodian confirmation, or IRS Form 706 coordination that may be required. Do not wait for day 250 or day 270 — a courier delay, an executor unavailability, or an unexpected complication in the last 30 days can destroy the disclaimer.

Trust drafting checklist for new estate plans

Estate plans drafted in 2026 for testators who want to preserve maximum §2518 flexibility for their surviving spouse and adult children should include the following provisions:

  • Explicit disclaimer clause naming the contingent taker. The will or revocable trust should provide that any disclaimed interest pours to a specified contingent taker — typically the testator's residuary trust, a testamentary bypass trust, or the testator's children by representation. A silent disposition plan leaves the disclaimer to state intestacy, which may not match the testator's intent.
  • Testamentary bypass trust with HEMS beneficial interest for the surviving spouse. Drafting the bypass trust with the surviving spouse as HEMS beneficiary (mandatory income + ascertainable-standard principal) permits the §2518(c)(3) surviving-spouse exception on any disclaimer of an outright marital bequest.
  • Grandchildren-by-representation clause for children's shares. Providing that each child's share passes to the child's issue by representation if the child disclaims allows an adult-child disclaimer to funnel to grandchildren without triggering intestacy or a routing to siblings.
  • Formula-clause capacity for pecuniary disclaimers. A well-drafted disclaimer clause should permit the disclaimant to disclaim a specific pecuniary amount (rather than a fractional share) so the surviving spouse can precisely target the decedent's remaining Applicable Exclusion Amount for full utilization.
  • Executor-directed §2518 computation. Authorize the executor to determine the exact pecuniary amount of the surviving spouse's optimal disclaimer, taking into account state estate-tax thresholds, portability DSUE math, and the timing of the disclaimer relative to the Form 706 filing deadline.
  • GST exemption allocation authority. Grant the executor authority to allocate the decedent's §2631 GST exemption to any pecuniary disclaimer that produces a generation-skipping transfer, so the disclaimer's grand-child benefit is not eroded by GST tax.

Frequently asked questions

What is a qualified disclaimer under IRC §2518?

A qualified disclaimer under IRC §2518 is a written, irrevocable, and unqualified refusal by a beneficiary to accept an interest in property. If the disclaimer satisfies the four elements of §2518(b) — irrevocable and unqualified writing, delivery within nine months of the transfer, no acceptance of the interest or any of its benefits, and passage without any direction from the disclaimant — then §2518(a) treats the disclaimant as never having received the interest at all for federal transfer-tax purposes. The property passes to the next taker under the transferor's will, trust, or state intestacy law as though the disclaimant had predeceased the transferor, and no gift-tax consequences attach to the disclaimant.

What is the nine-month deadline and when does it start running?

Under IRC §2518(b)(2), the written disclaimer must be received by the transferor of the interest (or by the transferor's legal representative — usually the executor of the decedent's estate, the trustee of a testamentary or lifetime trust, or the account custodian for a beneficiary designation) within nine months of the later of two dates: the date the transfer creating the interest is made (usually the decedent's date of death), or the date the disclaimant reaches age 21 for interests created during the disclaimant's minority. For successive interests (an income interest followed by a remainder), the nine months runs from the date the disclaimant's interest first becomes indefeasibly vested — so a contingent remainderman may have a nine-month window that starts years or decades after the original transfer.

What counts as acceptance of the interest or its benefits under §2518(b)(3)?

Treas. Reg. §25.2518-2(d)(1) reads acceptance broadly. Any act by which the disclaimant treats the property as her own is acceptance: taking a distribution, taking possession of tangible property, exercising dominion or control (voting shares, directing the trustee), pledging the interest as collateral, receiving rents or dividends, using the property for personal purposes, or accepting the economic benefit of the property in any way. Acceptance of any portion of the interest — even a single dividend check cashed by the disclaimant — is acceptance of the entire interest for §2518 purposes, subject to the partial-disclaimer rules under Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3 that permit disclaiming a severable interest so long as the retained portion and the disclaimed portion are separately identifiable. A beneficiary who is considering disclaimer must freeze the interest immediately at the date of death and avoid any receipt or exercise of dominion until the disclaimer is executed and delivered.

What does "passage without direction" mean under §2518(b)(4)?

IRC §2518(b)(4) requires that as a result of the disclaimer the property must pass either to the surviving spouse of the decedent OR to a person other than the disclaimant, and it must pass without any direction on the part of the disclaimant. In practice this means the disclaimer document may not name the next taker — the property passes under the operation of the transferor's will, trust, beneficiary designation, or state intestacy statute as though the disclaimant had predeceased the transferor. A disclaimer that attempts to redirect the property to a specific person (even if the person named is the same person who would take by operation of law) is a §2518 failure and is recharacterized as an inter vivos gift by the disclaimant under §2511. The disclaimant may not have any residual power over the timing, amount, or beneficiary of the redirection.

Who typically uses §2518 disclaimers for post-mortem estate planning?

Four beneficiary populations account for the bulk of §2518 disclaimers in modern practice. First, surviving spouses use disclaimers to redirect assets from the outright bequest to a credit-shelter (bypass) trust that preserves the deceased spouse's Applicable Exclusion Amount under §2010(c) — the most common single use case. Second, adult children use disclaimers to redirect assets to their own children (the grandchildren of the decedent) to skip a generation of estate tax under §2601 and take advantage of unused §2631 GST exemption. Third, beneficiaries with pending creditor claims, active bankruptcy proceedings, or divorce actions use disclaimers to keep the inheritance out of the creditor pool by causing the property to pass directly to their contingent takers. Fourth, elderly beneficiaries with pending or actual Medicaid nursing-home coverage use disclaimers to avoid the inheritance being counted as an available resource — subject to a critical caveat that the Medicaid rules in most states treat a §2518 disclaimer as a disqualifying transfer for the 60-month look-back period, so this use case must be worked with the state's Medicaid regulations, not federal §2518, as the controlling framework.

Can I make a partial disclaimer?

Yes, subject to strict rules. Treas. Reg. §25.2518-3 permits four types of partial disclaimers: a disclaimer of a specific undivided fraction of the interest ("I disclaim a 40% interest in the property"), a disclaimer of a specific pecuniary amount ("I disclaim $200,000 of the $500,000 bequest"), a disclaimer of a specific asset from a larger residuary or pecuniary bequest ("I disclaim my one-half interest in the beach house from the residuary estate"), and a disclaimer of a specific pecuniary or residuary bequest itself. What is NOT permitted is a disclaimer that carves out different economic characteristics from a single asset ("I disclaim the appreciation but keep the basis") — the retained interest and the disclaimed interest must be severable in a way that preserves the character of each. Disclaimers of separately-designated income and remainder interests are permitted; a disclaimer of only the income component of an ordinary bequest of stock is generally not.

How does §2518 interact with state law?

State law determines whether the disclaimer effectively passes the property under the transferor's will, trust, beneficiary designation, or state intestacy statute — §2518 is a federal-tax-recognition standard, not a property-transfer statute. The Uniform Disclaimer of Property Interests Act of 1999 (with amendments through 2006 and 2010) has been adopted in roughly 30 states and the District of Columbia; it provides a permissive framework that does NOT include a nine-month deadline (state UDPIA disclaimers are effective under state law whenever executed) but does require compliance with local delivery, notice, and recordation rules. A disclaimer that is valid under UDPIA on day 275 but delivered after the federal nine-month window will effectively transfer the property under state law while STILL being treated as an inter vivos gift by the disclaimant under §2511 for federal tax purposes. The federal and state tests must both be satisfied.

Do community property states change the disclaimer analysis?

Materially, in nine states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin) plus Alaska and Tennessee's opt-in regimes. In community property states, the surviving spouse already owns one-half of the community property at the moment of the first spouse's death — the survivor cannot disclaim her own one-half because she already owns it and disclaimer only reaches interests received from a transferor. She may disclaim the decedent's one-half community share plus any separate property. The mechanics of severing the community half from the disclaimable half require careful documentation, especially where title records commingle. Louisiana's civil-law forced-heirship regime under La. Civ. Code Art. 1493 further restricts disclaimer of interests going to forced heirs (children under 24 or disabled at any age); a Louisiana disclaimer of a forced portion is void as against the forced heir. Practitioners in community property jurisdictions typically retain local counsel for any disclaimer over $500,000 in value.

Can a surviving spouse disclaim into a trust in which she is a beneficiary?

Yes, under the §2518(c)(3) special surviving-spouse exception. Ordinarily the passage-without-direction requirement of §2518(b)(4) prohibits the disclaimant from receiving any residual interest in the disclaimed property. But IRC §2518(c)(3) carves out a surviving spouse: a surviving spouse may disclaim an interest in property AND retain a life-income interest, a discretionary distribution power under an ascertainable standard, or another beneficial interest in the disclaimed property so long as the disclaimed property passes under a trust structure that was created by the transferor (not the disclaimant). This is the mechanic that lets a surviving spouse disclaim an outright bequest into a decedent-drafted bypass trust or QTIP trust in which she remains a lifetime beneficiary — she gets both the tax benefit of the bypass and the economic benefit of continued access, without violating the §2518(b)(4) passage-without-direction rule.

What is the single most common §2518 mistake in 2026 practice?

Missing the nine-month deadline because a bank, brokerage, insurance company, or transfer agent takes 30-60 days to acknowledge the death and re-title the account, and the beneficiary uses those weeks doing nothing rather than executing the disclaimer against the interest that has already vested. The nine-month clock under §2518(b)(2) starts the day of death, not the day the custodian re-titles or the day the interest reaches the beneficiary. A beneficiary who waits for the custodian to make the first move is spending days off the front of a fixed nine-month window. The cure is to execute the disclaimer against the beneficial interest — which vested at death by operation of the will, trust, or beneficiary designation — as soon as the beneficiary has determined that the disclaimer is desired, without waiting for the custodian's paperwork. Delivery to the executor of the decedent's estate is delivery to the transferor for §2518(b)(2) purposes even if the custodian has not yet re-titled the account.
