1997 Uniform Principal and Income Act §409 in Non-UFIPA States in 2026: The 42-Jurisdiction Field Guide for Trust-Held Inherited IRAs

Forty-two U.S. jurisdictions — including New York, Texas, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Georgia, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, and North Carolina — still operate under the 1997 Uniform Principal and Income Act, not the 2018 UFIPA. Here is how §409's older 10-percent-of-required-distribution rule characterizes annual IRA distributions to a trust, why the SECURE Act 10-year rule breaks the 1997 calibration, and the state-by-state drafting playbook for practitioners and trustees who need to fix the mechanic before the account owner's death.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 17, 2026 24 min read How we research

The August 16, 2026 field guide walked the eight states that have adopted the 2018 Uniform Fiduciary Income and Principal Act — Utah, Kansas, Washington, Colorado, Arkansas, Virginia, California, and Florida — and mapped UFIPA §409's newer 10-percent-of-fair-market-value safe harbor for characterizing annual retirement-plan distributions to a trust.[1] This is the parallel companion for the other 42 U.S. jurisdictions: the states that still operate under the older 1997 Uniform Principal and Income Act, which uses a materially different mechanic — a 10-percent-of-required-distribution allocation, measured against the IRC-mandated RMD portion of the distribution rather than against the plan's fair market value. New York, Texas, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Georgia, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, and North Carolina are the ten highest-population non-UFIPA states, and together they hold roughly half of the U.S. trust population by count.[2]

The 1997 UPIA is not a bad statute. It was carefully drafted at a time when trust-held retirement assets were dominated by defined-benefit annuities and stretched-out lifetime IRA distributions, and the 10-percent-of-required-distribution allocation rule at §409 was a reasonable calibration for that world. What broke the calibration was the December 2019 SECURE Act and the July 2024 final regulations at TD 10001 finalizing the 10-year distribution window for most non-spousal designated beneficiaries.[3] Under the pre-SECURE stretch-IRA framework, a trust receiving annual distributions from an inherited IRA typically received amounts that scaled with the beneficiary's life expectancy — 4 to 6 percent of the balance in early years, growing slowly over decades. The 1997 UPIA §409 allocation applied cleanly to that fact pattern, and the resulting income allocation was small enough to be sensible but large enough to serve the trust's income beneficiaries.

Under the SECURE Act 10-year rule as clarified by TD 10001, the mandated RMD for a non-EDB trust beneficiary of an account owner who died on or after the required beginning date now sits within a hard ten-year window. In years 1 through 9 the trustee takes the pre-death schedule RMD; in year 10 the trustee accelerates the remaining balance to complete distribution by December 31.[4] The 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic still runs — 10 percent of the RMD portion to income, the balance to principal — but the resulting income allocation is now tiny relative to the total distribution the trust receives, especially in the year-10 acceleration. A trust with a $2M inherited IRA distributing $220,000 in year 1 under the 10-year rule might see only $6,000 of that characterized as income by default, with $214,000 defaulting to principal. That default is often the wrong result for the family, and the fix — an express drafting override, a §104 power-to-adjust election, or a state unitrust conversion — is what a trustee holding an inherited IRA in a non-UFIPA state needs to understand before the account owner's death, not after.

The 1997 UPIA framework at the uniform-text level

The Uniform Principal and Income Act was promulgated by the Uniform Law Commission at its July 1997 annual meeting, with technical amendments in 2000, after four years of drafting under a committee chaired by Joel C. Dobris and with substantial input from the American Bar Association Real Property, Trust and Estate Law Section and ACTEC.[5] The 1997 UPIA replaced the older Revised Uniform Principal and Income Act of 1962, which itself replaced the original 1931 Uniform Principal and Income Act. The 1997 iteration runs to seven articles and 505 sections and was adopted in some form by all fifty states plus the District of Columbia over the twelve years following promulgation.

The load-bearing provisions for most inherited-IRA drafting purposes are §§103, 104, 105, 106, 401, 409, 411, and 502. §103 codifies the fundamental principle that the trust instrument controls whenever its terms differ from the default rules. §104 establishes the trustee's discretionary power to adjust between income and principal to achieve impartiality. §106 (adopted as a separate freestanding section in most states) authorizes the trustee to convert an income-only trust into a percentage-of-value unitrust. §401 addresses receipts from business entities. §409 is the load-bearing provision for a trust holding an inherited IRA — the two-part rule that characterizes retirement-plan distributions between income and principal. §411 governs interest on obligations and §502 governs disbursements.

The critical distinction: 10-percent-of-RMD vs 10-percent-of-fair-market-value

The mechanical difference between 1997 UPIA §409 and UFIPA §409 is the single most important thing a practitioner needs to internalize. Under 1997 UPIA §409, the trustee measures the 10 percent against the required-distribution portion of the annual payment — the RMD amount computed under IRC §401(a)(9). Under UFIPA §409, the trustee measures the 10 percent against the plan's beginning-of-year fair market value. The two measurements produce different results in almost every case.

Consider a $2M inherited IRA distributing $220,000 to a trust in year 1 under the SECURE Act 10-year rule (roughly one-tenth of the balance, computed under either the pre-death IRC §401(a)(9) schedule or the year-10 acceleration requirement). Under 1997 UPIA §409, the trustee allocates 10 percent of the $220,000 RMD portion to income — $22,000 — and the remaining $198,000 to principal. Under UFIPA §409, the trustee checks whether the $220,000 total distribution exceeds 10 percent of the $2M fair market value — it does, at 11 percent — so the safe harbor is unavailable and the entire distribution defaults to principal (with only the payor-labeled interest/dividend portion, typically negligible for an IRA, going to income).

Which framework is worse for the income beneficiary?

In a SECURE Act 10-year-rule world, both frameworks tip overwhelmingly toward principal by default. 1997 UPIA §409 typically gives the income beneficiary a small but nonzero share (10 percent of the RMD), while UFIPA §409 typically gives the income beneficiary nothing when the distribution exceeds the safe harbor. Neither result matches the drafter's likely intent for most inherited-IRA see-through trusts. The practitioner fix is the same under both frameworks — an express drafting override displacing the state's default characterization — but the mechanical language of the override differs depending on whether the state uses the 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic or the UFIPA §409 mechanic. In a non-UFIPA state, the override should displace the "10 percent of the required-distribution portion" language; in a UFIPA state, it should displace the "10 percent of the fair-market-value safe harbor" language.

New York — EPTL §11-A-4.9 (2002)

New York enacted the 1997 UPIA as Article 11-A of the Estates, Powers and Trusts Law, effective January 1, 2002, with the enabling legislation at 2001 N.Y. Laws Ch. 243. EPTL §11-A-4.9 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[6] The New York enactment placed the 1997 UPIA in a coordinated Article 11-A within the broader EPTL Chapter 17-B trust-administration framework, and coupled the §409 rule with EPTL §11-2.3 (the Prudent Investor Act, including the §104 power to adjust at EPTL §11-2.3(b)(5)), EPTL §11-2.4 (the optional unitrust conversion at 4 percent of net fair market value), and EPTL §11-1.7 (the trustee's general duty of impartiality).

New York is the highest-search-volume non-UFIPA state on the CalcLeap analytics inventory by a substantial margin, and the single most-searched question in the state's trust-and-estate practice is how EPTL §11-A-4.9 interacts with a see-through trust holding an inherited IRA in the SECURE Act 10-year-rule era. The practical playbook for a New York trustee is a four-step sequence: (1) apply EPTL §11-A-4.9 mechanically to compute the default 10-percent-of-RMD allocation, (2) evaluate whether the resulting split creates a QTIP or reasonable-apportionment problem under Rev. Rul. 2006-26, (3) exercise the EPTL §11-2.3(b)(5) power to adjust to reallocate the balance as needed for the first-year distribution, and (4) if the family wants long-term predictability, consider the EPTL §11-2.4 unitrust conversion at the New York 4-percent default.

New York's practitioner-side complications include the state's own trust income tax framework at N.Y. Tax Law §§601-618, which imposes state income tax on undistributed trust income at rates up to 10.9 percent (top marginal bracket for 2026) if the trust has at least one New York-resident trustee OR at least one New York-resident non-contingent beneficiary. A New York trustee's §11-2.3(b)(5) power-to-adjust decision therefore carries state-tax consequences on top of the trust-accounting characterization — a distribution characterized as principal and retained by the trust is taxed at the trust's compressed federal rate (37 percent at $15,650 in 2026 per Rev. Proc. 2025-32) plus the New York 10.9 percent overlay, while a distribution characterized as income and distributed to a New York-resident beneficiary is taxed at the beneficiary's individual rate.[7]

Texas — Property Code §116.172 (2003, effective 2004)

Texas enacted the 1997 UPIA as Chapter 116 of the Texas Property Code, effective January 1, 2004, with the enabling legislation at 2003 Tex. Gen. Laws Ch. 659. Texas Property Code §116.172 codifies 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[8] The Texas enactment was made compatible with the pre-existing Texas Trust Code at Property Code Chapters 111-115 — Texas did not adopt the Uniform Trust Code in full but has incorporated many UTC principles in its 1984 Texas Trust Code and subsequent amendments. Texas Property Code §116.005 codifies the 1997 UPIA §104 power to adjust; Texas Property Code §116.006 codifies the unitrust-conversion mechanism with a 3-to-5 percent state-defined range.

Texas is a particularly consequential non-UFIPA state because it combines the 1997 UPIA §409 default with the state's zero income-tax framework — Texas imposes no state income tax on trusts, individuals, or beneficiaries. A Texas trustee's §116.005 power-to-adjust decision carries only the federal-tax dimension (the trust's compressed 37-percent rate at $15,650 versus the beneficiary's individual rate), without any state-tax overlay. This makes Texas a favored situs for trust-held inherited IRAs where the family has no strong state-of-residence tie to another jurisdiction — the default 1997 UPIA §409 principal characterization is at most a federal-tax concern in Texas, not a compounded federal-plus-state concern.

Texas also carries the largest concentration of non-family corporate trustees per capita of any non-UFIPA state, with substantial trust-services industries in Dallas, Houston, San Antonio, and Austin. For a family relocating to Texas from a UFIPA state (California in particular) with an inherited-IRA-holding trust, the change-of-situs analysis needs to include the 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic that will apply in Texas going forward. Practitioners handling such relocations should update the trust instrument at the point of situs change to include an express §409 override calibrated to the 1997 UPIA mechanic, and file the state-required documents for the situs change at Texas Property Code §112.038.

Massachusetts — G.L. c. 203D §4.9 (2003, revised 2013)

Massachusetts enacted the 1997 UPIA as Chapter 203D of the General Laws, effective July 1, 2003, then revised the statute substantially in 2013 with the enactment of the Massachusetts Uniform Trust Code at G.L. c. 203E and coordination amendments to Chapter 203D. G.L. c. 203D §4.9 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[9] The Massachusetts enactment includes the §104 power to adjust at G.L. c. 203D §4.4 and a coordinated unitrust-conversion mechanic at G.L. c. 203C (a separately-adopted 2003 unitrust-conversion statute) with a 3-to-5 percent state-defined range.

Massachusetts is the most active non-UFIPA state in terms of proposed UFIPA legislation — UFIPA introduction bills have been filed in every biennial legislative session since 2019, most recently as H. 1467 and S. 1002 in the 2025-2026 session, both currently in committee at the Judiciary Committee. Massachusetts practitioners should draft new inherited-IRA see-through trusts to be operative under both frameworks by including an express §409 override that displaces both the 1997 UPIA "10 percent of the required distribution" mechanic and the potential future UFIPA "10 percent of the fair market value" mechanic — a belt-and-suspenders override protecting the trust from either state law's default.

New Jersey — N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-31 (2001)

New Jersey enacted the 1997 UPIA as N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-1 through 3B:19B-45, effective September 1, 2001. N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-31 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[10] The New Jersey enactment includes the §104 power to adjust at N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-4 and a coordinated unitrust-conversion mechanic at N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-5 with a 4-percent state default (within the 3-to-5 percent uniform range).

New Jersey is significant for inherited-IRA drafting purposes because of the state's unique split-mirror treatment of §401(k) elective deferrals under N.J.S.A. 54A:6-21 — New Jersey taxes §401(k) elective deferrals at contribution rather than at distribution, meaning distributions come with substantial New Jersey basis. This basis interacts with the 1997 UPIA §409 characterization in an unusual way: the portion of the distribution representing recovery of New Jersey basis is not New Jersey-taxable regardless of income/principal characterization, but the earnings portion is New Jersey-taxable at up to 10.75 percent (top marginal for 2026) if the trust retains the amount. Practitioners drafting new inherited-IRA trusts for New Jersey-resident beneficiaries should coordinate the 1997 UPIA §409 override with the N.J.S.A. 54A:6-21 basis-recovery framework — a topic covered in more depth in our July 21, 2026 New Jersey §401(k) piece.

Georgia — O.C.G.A. §53-12-419 (2018)

Georgia enacted the 1997 UPIA as Article 4 of Chapter 12 of Title 53 of the Official Code of Georgia Annotated, effective July 1, 2018. O.C.G.A. §53-12-419 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[11] Georgia was one of the later 1997 UPIA adopters and its enactment coincided with the 2018 Uniform Law Commission's promulgation of UFIPA — Georgia essentially adopted the 1997 UPIA in the same year UFIPA was being promulgated. Georgia includes the §104 power to adjust at O.C.G.A. §53-12-405 and a separately-adopted unitrust-conversion mechanic at O.C.G.A. §53-12-407.

Georgia is a distinctive non-UFIPA state because of its age-tiered retirement income exclusion at Ga. Code §48-7-27, which exempts up to $65,000 per person per year of retirement income for taxpayers age 65 or older. Trust-held inherited IRAs do not qualify for the Georgia retirement income exclusion — the exclusion is available only to individual taxpayers, not to trusts — but distributions from the trust to a Georgia-resident beneficiary age 65 or older may qualify at the beneficiary's individual level. The 1997 UPIA §409 characterization decision therefore has a distinctive Georgia-specific twist: characterizing more of the distribution as income (and thus distributed to the beneficiary) may qualify the amount for the age-65 exclusion at the beneficiary's individual return, producing a state-tax outcome not available at the trust level.

Illinois — 760 ILCS 3/409 (2020)

Illinois enacted the 1997 UPIA as Article 4 of the Illinois Trust Code at 760 ILCS 3/409, effective January 1, 2020. 760 ILCS 3/409 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[12] Illinois was the second-to-last state to adopt the 1997 UPIA and did so as part of a broader 2020 recodification of the state's trust code that also adopted the Uniform Trust Code as 760 ILCS 3. The Illinois enactment includes the §104 power to adjust at 760 ILCS 3/402 (Illinois Trust Code §802 as adopted) and a separately-adopted unitrust-conversion mechanic at 760 ILCS 5/5.3 (a pre-existing 2001 unitrust statute) with a 4-percent state default.

Illinois is unusual among non-UFIPA states because of its unlimited retirement income subtraction at 35 ILCS 5/203(a)(2)(F) — Illinois exempts all §401(a), §403(a), §403(b), §408, §408A, §457(b), and government-plan distributions from state income tax entirely, without age or income tiers. This makes Illinois a favored situs for trust-held inherited IRAs where the income beneficiary is an Illinois resident — the 1997 UPIA §409 characterization decision has zero Illinois state-tax consequence for the beneficiary regardless of income/principal split (because Illinois exempts the distribution at the beneficiary level). At the trust level, Illinois imposes a 4.95 percent flat rate on undistributed trust income under 35 ILCS 5/201, which is materially lower than the New York, New Jersey, or California state trust income tax overlays but higher than the zero-tax Texas or Florida frameworks.

Pennsylvania — 20 Pa.C.S. §8148 (2002)

Pennsylvania enacted the 1997 UPIA as Chapter 81 of Title 20 of the Pennsylvania Consolidated Statutes, effective July 1, 2002. 20 Pa.C.S. §8148 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[13] Pennsylvania was among the earliest 1997 UPIA adopters and coordinated the enactment with the pre-existing Pennsylvania trust code framework. Pennsylvania includes the §104 power to adjust at 20 Pa.C.S. §8104 and a separately-adopted unitrust-conversion mechanic at 20 Pa.C.S. §8105 with a 4-percent state default.

Pennsylvania is the most doctrinally distinctive non-UFIPA state for inherited-IRA purposes because of its contribution-point taxation of §401(k) elective deferrals under 72 P.S. §7301(d) and the associated Pennsylvania basis-recovery mechanics under 61 Pa. Code §101.6. Pennsylvania taxes §401(k) elective deferrals at contribution and does not tax the corresponding distributions at retirement age — meaning §401(k) distributions to a Pennsylvania-resident beneficiary carry substantial Pennsylvania basis. Trust-held inherited IRAs holding pre-Pennsylvania-basis assets (i.e., original account owners who were Pennsylvania residents when contributing) create distinctive drafting complications when the 1997 UPIA §409 characterization interacts with Pennsylvania's basis-recovery framework. Practitioners drafting Pennsylvania-situs trusts should coordinate the 1997 UPIA §409 override with the 72 P.S. §7301(d) basis-recovery framework — a topic covered in depth in our July 17, 2026 Pennsylvania §401(k) piece.

Ohio — Ohio Rev. Code §5812.29 (2002)

Ohio enacted the 1997 UPIA as Chapter 5812 of the Ohio Revised Code, effective January 1, 2002. Ohio Rev. Code §5812.29 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[14] Ohio includes the §104 power to adjust at Ohio Rev. Code §5812.03 and a coordinated unitrust-conversion mechanic at Ohio Rev. Code §5812.05 with a 3-to-5 percent state-defined range. Ohio's enactment is coordinated with the state's Uniform Trust Code adoption at Ohio Rev. Code Chapter 5810 (enacted 2007, effective January 1, 2007) and the state's decanting statute at Ohio Rev. Code §5808.18.

Ohio is a favored non-UFIPA situs for inherited-IRA-holding trusts because of the state's flat 2.75 percent income tax rate (post-2025 rate schedule under Ohio HB 33 and Ohio HB 96), well below the New York, New Jersey, and California overlays. Ohio also carries a mature corporate-trustee industry concentrated in Cleveland, Columbus, and Cincinnati, with substantial multigenerational trust-administration expertise.

Michigan — MCL 555.509 (2003)

Michigan enacted the 1997 UPIA as Public Act 159 of 2003, codified at MCL 555.501 through MCL 555.913, effective September 1, 2004. MCL 555.509 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[15] Michigan includes the §104 power to adjust at MCL 555.504 and a coordinated unitrust-conversion mechanic at MCL 555.505 with a 3-to-5 percent state-defined range.

Michigan is significant for inherited-IRA drafting purposes because of the state's recent Public Act 4 of 2023 (effective January 1, 2026), which restored full state income tax exemption for retirement income received by taxpayers born in 1946 or later — Michigan is now a Category B full-retirement-exemption state (per our July 15, 2026 state retirement income taxation field guide). At the trust level, Michigan imposes a 4.25 percent flat rate under MCL 206.51 on undistributed trust income, similar to Ohio but with a broader base. The distinct Michigan factor is the Public Act 4 of 2023 retirement-income exemption's interaction with the 1997 UPIA §409 characterization at the beneficiary level — as with Illinois, characterizing more of the distribution as income (and thus distributed to the beneficiary) may qualify the amount for the state-level exemption at the beneficiary's individual return.

North Carolina — N.C. Gen. Stat. §37A-4-409 (2004)

North Carolina enacted the 1997 UPIA as Chapter 37A of the General Statutes, effective January 1, 2004. N.C. Gen. Stat. §37A-4-409 codifies the 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim.[16] North Carolina includes the §104 power to adjust at N.C. Gen. Stat. §37A-1-104 and a coordinated unitrust-conversion mechanic at N.C. Gen. Stat. §37A-1-105 with a 3-to-5 percent state-defined range. North Carolina is coordinated with the state's Uniform Trust Code adoption at N.C. Gen. Stat. Chapter 36C.

North Carolina is a growing situs for inherited-IRA-holding trusts because of the state's declining income tax rate (4.25 percent for 2025, scheduled to reach 3.99 percent by 2027 under N.C. HB 259) and its mature corporate-trustee industry concentrated in Charlotte, Raleigh, and Winston-Salem. The state's tax framework at the beneficiary level partially exempts retirement income for beneficiaries under Bailey settlement legislation for government-plan participants, but does not offer a general age-based retirement income exemption; the trust-level 1997 UPIA §409 characterization decision therefore turns primarily on the federal tax mechanics rather than a state-specific interaction.

Interaction with the SECURE Act 10-year rule and see-through trusts

The 1997 UPIA was drafted between 1993 and 1997, well before the December 2019 SECURE Act and even farther in advance of the July 2024 final regulations at TD 10001 that finalized the 10-year distribution window for most non-spousal designated beneficiaries.[3] The 1997 UPIA drafters could not have anticipated either. This creates three doctrinal gaps a practitioner must fix by careful drafting when relying on a non-UFIPA state's 1997 UPIA §409 default rules to govern the annual distributions from an inherited-IRA-holding trust.

First, the 1997 UPIA §409 10-percent-of-required-distribution rule was calibrated to a pre-SECURE world in which the required distribution to an inherited-IRA-holding trust was measured against the beneficiary's remaining life expectancy under IRS Publication 590-B, producing RMDs of roughly 4 to 6 percent of the balance in early years, growing slowly over decades. Under the SECURE Act 10-year rule, the required distribution now sits within a hard ten-year window, and the mandated RMD in the middle years plus the year-10 acceleration together push the total distribution the trust receives from the custodian well above the historical stretch-IRA levels. The 10 percent of the RMD amount is now typically much smaller relative to the total distribution than it was in the pre-SECURE calibration.

Second, TD 10001's July 2024 clarification that non-EDB beneficiaries of an account owner who died on or after the required beginning date must take annual RMDs computed under the pre-death schedule (rather than merely completing distribution by year 10 with no interim RMD requirement) means the 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic runs every year of the 10-year window, not just in year 10. This is different from the pre-TD-10001 practitioner assumption that a trust beneficiary could defer all distributions to year 10 without any annual RMD requirement, and it materially increases the frequency of the §409 calculation the trustee must run.

Third, the SECURE Act's introduction of the applicable multi-beneficiary trust (AMBT) structure at IRC §401(a)(9)(H)(v), and the July 2024 TD 10001 final regulation implementation, created a subdivision option for a trust with mixed-EDB-and-non-EDB beneficiaries. A trust that subdivides into separate accounts under the AMBT framework by September 30 of the year after the account owner's death applies the 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic separately to each sub-trust. This adds administrative complexity but can produce meaningfully different income/principal splits across the sub-trusts depending on the beneficiary's EDB status and remaining life expectancy.

The recommended non-UFIPA state override drafting language

Practitioners drafting a new see-through trust in 2026 in a non-UFIPA state typically include an express §409 override calibrated to the 1997 UPIA mechanic. A representative override reads: "Notwithstanding [applicable state code section codifying 1997 UPIA §409], all distributions from any qualified retirement account (including without limitation any traditional IRA, Roth IRA, §401(k) plan, §403(b) plan, §457(b) plan, or inherited IRA) shall be allocated to trust accounting income to the extent required to (a) satisfy the annual income-distribution requirement of any marital deduction trust that qualifies or is intended to qualify for the federal estate tax marital deduction under IRC §2056(b)(7), (b) preserve the trust's qualification as a see-through trust under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4(f), and (c) provide reasonable apportionment between income and remainder beneficiaries as contemplated by Rev. Rul. 2006-26. The trustee is authorized but not required to allocate additional amounts to income to achieve impartiality between income and remainder beneficiaries as contemplated by [applicable state code section codifying 1997 UPIA §104]." This language displaces the state's 1997 UPIA §409 default, preserves QTIP qualification, and gives the trustee ongoing discretion to reallocate more to income under §104.

Case study: a New York QTIP EPTL §11-A-4.9 election on a $2M inherited IRA

Consider a family with a Manhattan-based deceased account owner, a Westchester-resident 54-year-old surviving spouse as sole income beneficiary of a QTIP marital-deduction trust, and two adult children as remainder beneficiaries. The trust was drafted in 2015 in New York as a see-through QTIP holding the account owner's rollover IRA at Vanguard, with the surviving spouse as income beneficiary for life and the two children taking the remainder at the spouse's death. The account owner died in March 2026 at age 74 (past the required beginning date), leaving a $2M IRA balance. Under the SECURE Act 10-year rule as clarified by TD 10001, the trust must (a) take annual RMDs computed under the account owner's pre-death Uniform Lifetime Table schedule beginning in 2027 and (b) complete distribution of the entire balance by December 31, 2036.[17]

The 2015 trust as drafted is silent on the allocation of retirement-plan distributions between income and principal. Under New York's EPTL §11-A-4.9 default rules, Vanguard's 2027 distribution of approximately $80,000 (roughly the pre-death Uniform Lifetime Table RMD for a 75-year-old on a $2M balance) is measured under the 1997 UPIA §409 two-part rule. Vanguard does not label any portion as interest or dividends. The trustee allocates 10 percent of the $80,000 RMD to income — $8,000 — and the remaining $72,000 to principal. The surviving spouse receives $8,000 as trust accounting income in 2027.

The trustee recognizes that this default characterization creates two problems. First, it defeats the drafter's likely intent that the surviving spouse receive meaningful cash flow from the trust across the 10-year window. Second, and more consequentially, it may create a QTIP qualification problem under IRC §2056(b)(7). The QTIP requires that all trust accounting income be distributed to the surviving spouse annually, and Rev. Rul. 2006-26 clarifies that the state-law characterization framework qualifies for QTIP purposes only if it provides a reasonable apportionment between income and remainder beneficiaries. A characterization that allocates $8,000 out of an $80,000 distribution to income — with the remaining $72,000 going to principal for the children — is arguably not a reasonable apportionment where the spouse is a QTIP income beneficiary with a life interest in the trust.[18]

If the family prefers long-term predictability, the trustee can convert to a 4-percent EPTL §11-2.4 unitrust in 2028 or later — the New York unitrust is a well-established Treas. Six recurring 1997 UPIA §409 drafting mistakes on inherited-IRA trusts

The recurring mistakes practitioners see in non-UFIPA-state 1997 UPIA §409 practice cluster into six categories:

  1. Assuming the state-law characterization is UFIPA-style fair-market-value-based. Practitioners with a national practice who trained on the UFIPA §409 mechanic sometimes forget that non-UFIPA states use the older 1997 UPIA §409 10-percent-of-required-distribution rule. The two mechanics produce different results, and drafting language that displaces the UFIPA safe harbor does not necessarily displace the 1997 UPIA required-distribution allocation. Non-UFIPA-state overrides need to reference the 1997 UPIA mechanic explicitly.
  2. Missing the QTIP qualification interaction under Rev. Rul. 2006-26. A QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA must provide a reasonable apportionment between income and remainder beneficiaries. A trust drafted in a non-UFIPA state that relies on the default 1997 UPIA §409 characterization — allocating only 10 percent of the RMD to income — typically fails the reasonable-apportionment test. The trustee must exercise the state's §104 power to adjust or convert to a state unitrust to preserve QTIP qualification, and should document the exercise in a contemporaneous written memorandum.
  3. Confusing the state's UPIA §106 unitrust range with the trust's own express percentage. Each non-UFIPA state sets a permissible unitrust range (typically 3 to 5 percent). A trustee who elects a percentage outside the state's range creates a voidable conversion. New York's EPTL §11-2.4 sets 4 percent as the exclusive rate (no range); Texas Property Code §116.006 allows 3 to 5 percent within the trustee's discretion; Massachusetts G.L. c. 203C allows 3 to 5 percent.
  4. Failing to update legacy trust language when the trust's situs changes to a UFIPA state. A trust drafted in a non-UFIPA state under the 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic that later changes situs to a UFIPA state (California, Florida, Colorado, and so on) becomes subject to the new state's UFIPA §409 mechanic without any change to the trust instrument. Practitioners handling situs changes should update the trust's income-allocation clause to be operative under both frameworks — either by including a state-neutral express override or by including language that applies the framework of the state of situs on each specific event.
  5. Not documenting the §104 power-to-adjust exercise in a contemporaneous written memorandum. The 1997 UPIA §105 governs judicial control of the §104 power — a beneficiary challenge to the trustee's adjustment decision is reviewed under an arbitrary-and-capricious standard, and the trustee's ability to defend the decision depends heavily on the quality of the contemporaneous documentation. Practitioners should draft a template §104 memorandum for each state's variant of the 1997 UPIA and use it for every year's §409 characterization decision.
  6. Assuming the SECURE Act annual-RMD requirement applies only in year 10. Pre-TD-10001 practitioners often assumed a non-EDB trust beneficiary could defer all distributions to year 10. TD 10001 clarified that annual RMDs apply throughout the 10-year window when the account owner died on or after the required beginning date. Trustees who followed the pre-TD-10001 approach must catch up on the missed RMDs, subject to the SECURE 2.0 Act §302 reduction of the missed-RMD penalty from 50 percent to 25 percent (or 10 percent if timely corrected).

Action checklist for a 1997 UPIA §409 analysis on an inherited-IRA trust

If you are the trustee of a see-through trust holding an inherited IRA in a non-UFIPA state, work through this eight-item checklist before the first annual distribution from the IRA custodian.[19]

  1. Confirm the state's UPIA adoption and the operative statutory citation. Look up the specific state's 1997 UPIA framework in the state code and confirm the state has adopted the 1997 UPIA (not UFIPA). Note the specific §409 retirement-plan citation, the §104 power-to-adjust citation, and the unitrust-conversion citation for use in the trustee's memoranda.
  2. Read the trust instrument for an express §409 override. Look specifically for language that expressly allocates retirement-plan distributions between income and principal — clauses like "all distributions from any qualified retirement account shall be trust accounting income to the extent required to preserve QTIP qualification." An express override displaces the state's 1997 UPIA §409 default.
  3. Compute the §409 default allocation. Take 10 percent of the required-distribution portion of the annual payment (the RMD amount computed under IRC §401(a)(9)). That amount is trust accounting income; the balance is trust principal. Add any payor-labeled interest or dividend amounts to income.
  4. Evaluate the QTIP qualification interaction if the trust is a marital-deduction trust. Rev. Rul. 2006-26 requires reasonable apportionment. A 10-percent-of-RMD allocation typically fails the reasonable-apportionment test — the trustee must use §104 power to adjust or state unitrust conversion to reach a defensible apportionment. Consider the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) unitrust safe harbor.
  5. Consider the state's §104 power to adjust for the first-year distribution. The §104 mechanic requires no beneficiary notice and no formal amendment. It is the least invasive fix and preserves the trustee's flexibility to reallocate differently in later years. Document the exercise in a contemporaneous written memorandum citing the specific reasons for the reallocation.
  6. Consider the state's unitrust conversion if the family wants predictability. The conversion typically requires notice with specific content, is a longer-term mechanic, and applies to the trust as a whole rather than only to the IRA. Choose the state's default or elect within the state's range (New York 4 percent; most others 3-5 percent).
  7. Consider a nonjudicial settlement agreement or trust reformation if a permanent express override is warranted. A one-time permanent fix through a NJSA under UTC §111 (in UTC states) or through judicial reformation produces the cleanest long-term outcome. Costs typically run $9,000 to $15,000 in legal fees and 60 to 90 days of processing. Weigh against the value of avoiding annual §104 elections.
  8. Model the trust's cash-flow profile across the full 10-year window. Use the CalcLeap retirement calculator, the 401(k) withdrawal calculator, the traditional IRA calculator, and the estate tax calculator to project the after-tax outcome under each characterization approach. The right choice depends on the beneficiary's individual bracket, the trust's compressed bracket, the state-tax overlay, and the family's cash-flow preferences.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 1997 Uniform Principal and Income Act and which states still operate under it in 2026?

The 1997 UPIA is a model statute promulgated by the Uniform Law Commission in 1997 to govern how a trustee characterizes trust receipts and disbursements as either trust income (payable to income beneficiaries) or trust principal (held for remainder beneficiaries). Approximately 42 U.S. jurisdictions still operate under some version of the 1997 UPIA in 2026 — the 8 states that adopted the successor 2018 UFIPA (Utah, Kansas, Washington, Colorado, Arkansas, Virginia, California, Florida) have replaced it, leaving 42 jurisdictions governed by the older 1997 framework. New York, Texas, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Georgia, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, and North Carolina are the ten highest-population non-UFIPA states.

What does 1997 UPIA §409 say about IRA distributions to a trust?

1997 UPIA §409 provides a two-part rule: (a) any portion of a retirement-plan distribution the payor labels as interest or dividends is trust income, and (b) if no portion is so labeled, the trustee allocates 10 percent of the required-distribution portion of the annual payment to income and the balance to principal. The 10 percent is measured against the mandated RMD portion, not against the plan's fair market value the way UFIPA §409's newer safe harbor is measured.

How does the SECURE Act 10-year rule interact with 1997 UPIA §409?

The SECURE Act 10-year rule interacts poorly with 1997 UPIA §409 because the older rule was calibrated to a stretch-IRA world with 4-6 percent annual life-expectancy RMDs. Under the SECURE Act 10-year rule and TD 10001's July 2024 clarification, the mandated RMD is now larger and comes within a hard ten-year window. The 10-percent-of-RMD allocation still applies mechanically, but the resulting income allocation is tiny relative to the total distribution — most of the amount defaults to principal. The practitioner fix is an express drafting override, a §104 power-to-adjust election, or a state unitrust conversion.

Can a non-UFIPA state's trust code let the trustee override the 1997 UPIA §409 default?

Yes, three mechanisms are available in most non-UFIPA states: the trust instrument itself (per 1997 UPIA §103), the §104 power to adjust, and a state unitrust-conversion mechanic (typically at 3-5 percent state-defined ranges). New York, Texas, Massachusetts, New Jersey, Georgia, Illinois, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Michigan, and North Carolina all offer at least one of these three overrides.

How does New York's Estates, Powers and Trusts Law implement 1997 UPIA §409?

New York enacted the 1997 UPIA as Article 11-A of the EPTL effective January 1, 2002. EPTL §11-A-4.9 codifies UPIA §409 substantively verbatim. New York couples the rule with EPTL §11-2.3(b)(5) (power to adjust), EPTL §11-2.4 (4-percent unitrust conversion), and EPTL §11-1.7 (duty of impartiality). The practical playbook is: apply §11-A-4.9 mechanically, evaluate QTIP interaction under Rev. Rul. 2006-26, exercise §11-2.3(b)(5) power to adjust for the first-year distribution, and consider §11-2.4 unitrust conversion for long-term predictability.

What about Texas, which is often listed as a non-Uniform Trust Code state?

Texas adopted the 1997 UPIA as Chapter 116 of the Texas Property Code effective January 1, 2004. Texas Property Code §116.172 codifies 1997 UPIA §409 substantively verbatim. Texas Property Code §116.005 codifies the §104 power to adjust; §116.006 codifies the unitrust conversion at 3-5 percent. Texas trustees follow the same four-step playbook as New York, with the additional advantage that Texas imposes no state income tax on trusts, individuals, or beneficiaries.

How does the 1997 UPIA §409 characterization affect a QTIP marital deduction trust holding an IRA?

IRC §2056(b)(7) requires the QTIP trust to distribute all trust accounting income to the surviving spouse annually. Rev. Rul. 2006-26 confirms that the state-law characterization framework qualifies for QTIP purposes only if it provides a reasonable apportionment between income and remainder beneficiaries. Under 1997 UPIA §409, the default 10-percent-of-RMD allocation may fall well below what a reasonable apportionment requires, putting the QTIP marital deduction at risk. The trustee must exercise the state's §104 power to adjust or convert to a state unitrust.

What is the difference between the 1997 UPIA §409 10-percent-of-RMD rule and UFIPA §409's 10-percent-of-fair-market-value rule?

The two rules produce different results in almost every case. Under 1997 UPIA §409, the trustee allocates 10 percent of the mandated RMD to income — the income allocation scales with the RMD amount and is typically small. Under UFIPA §409, the trustee measures 10 percent against the plan's fair market value and may allocate the entire distribution to income if under that threshold. In a SECURE Act 10-year-rule world, both frameworks tip overwhelmingly toward principal by default, but the mechanical path differs.

Should a practitioner drafting a see-through trust in 2026 in a non-UFIPA state use different language than in a UFIPA state?

The core recommendation is the same: include an express override displacing the state's default characterization. Non-UFIPA-state override language should reference the 1997 UPIA §409 mechanic explicitly — for example, "all distributions from any qualified retirement account, whether characterized as required minimum distributions or not, shall be trust accounting income to the extent required to preserve QTIP qualification under IRC §2056(b)(7)." The Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment framework applies identically in both settings.

Are any of the 42 non-UFIPA jurisdictions likely to adopt UFIPA in the near future?

Several UFIPA introduction bills are pending in 2026 legislative cycles. Massachusetts (H. 1467 / S. 1002), New York, and New Jersey have all seen UFIPA introductions in recent sessions but none have passed. Oregon, Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Michigan have had draft UFIPA bills circulated in trust-and-estate section working groups but not introduced. Historical pace of uniform-act adoption in the trust-and-estate area is slow — the 1997 UPIA itself took roughly 12 years to reach majority adoption. Practitioners in non-UFIPA states should plan for the 1997 UPIA to remain in force for the foreseeable future.
