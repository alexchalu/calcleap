IRC §2040 Joint Interests in 2026: The §2040(a) Full-Consideration Rule, §2040(b) Qualified Joint Interest 50/50 Safe Harbor, the Pre-1977 Gallenstein Trap, and the §2056 Marital Deduction Overlay

§2040 is the section that reaches every jointly-titled asset with a right of survivorship — every joint bank account, every jointly-titled residence, every joint brokerage account, every tenancy by the entirety. The starting rule under §2040(a) is 100% inclusion of the date-of-death fair market value in the first-dying joint tenant's gross estate, reduced only by the survivor's affirmatively-documented contribution. §2040(b) carves out a spousal safe harbor at exactly 50/50 regardless of who paid. But the pre-1977 Gallenstein grandfather still preserves the pre-TRA consideration-furnished test for spousal joint tenancies acquired before 1977 — producing a full 100% basis step-up on highly-appreciated pre-1977 marital assets that dwarfs the §2040(b) 50% step-up. This is the 2026 walkthrough — including the joint-bank-account convenience trap that lands 100% of a parent's account in the parent's gross estate when the child was only ever a signatory.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 17, 2026 26 min read How we research

Every estate planner working an estate that includes jointly-titled real estate, a joint bank account, a joint brokerage account, or tenancy-by-the-entirety property has to route the analysis through IRC §2040.[1] The section governs the single most common informal estate-planning technique in the United States — adding a spouse, a parent, or an adult child to the title of an asset — and it governs it in a way that is asymmetric, counter-intuitive, and largely unknown to non-specialist practitioners.[2] The default rule under §2040(a) is not proportionate ownership — it is 100% inclusion of the date-of-death fair market value in the first-dying joint tenant's gross estate, reduced only by the survivor's affirmatively-documented out-of-pocket contribution from separate funds.[1]

§2040 opens the sixth Chapter 11 inclusion-rule pillar beyond the four-pillar §§2035/2036/2038/2042 retained-interest foundation and the §2039 annuity-and-pension-plan pillar we walked in the five earlier pieces of this arc.[3] The retained-interest pillars turn on what powers the decedent held over transferred property at death. §2039 turns on whether the decedent was entitled to periodic payments under a contract that continued after death. §2040 turns on a still-narrower question: at date of death, did the decedent hold title to any property jointly with another person under a form of concurrent ownership that carries a right of survivorship? If yes, the full date-of-death fair market value is included in the decedent's gross estate under §2040(a) unless the survivor can affirmatively prove out-of-pocket contribution, or unless the §2040(b) qualified-joint-interest safe harbor for post-1976 spousal joint interests carves the inclusion down to 50%.[4]

This guide walks §2040 from the statute forward: the operative mechanics of §2040(a) (the general consideration-furnished rule), the §2040(b) qualified-joint-interest safe harbor for spousal joint tenancies acquired after December 31, 1976, the pre-1977 Gallenstein grandfather that still governs marital assets acquired before the TRA §2002(c) enactment, the tenancy-by-the-entirety mechanics, the joint bank and brokerage account analysis, the community-property carve-out under Treas. Reg. §20.2040-1(a)(1), the §1014 basis-step-up trap on §2040(b) property, the §2056 marital-deduction coordination on §2040(b) qualified joint interests, the interaction with §§2033/2036/2038, transfer-on-death and payable-on-death account substitutes, and three worked case studies. The 1954 origin and the 1976 spousal safe harbor

The current §2040 dates from the Internal Revenue Code of 1954, which recodified §811(e) of the 1939 Code without substantive change.[6] The consideration-furnished rule that now sits in §2040(a) originated in Section 402(d) of the Revenue Act of 1918 and was carried forward through §302(e) of the 1926 Code, §811(e) of the 1939 Code, and §2040 of the 1954 Code with only minor drafting revisions.[6] The core rule for six decades was the pure consideration-furnished test: 100% inclusion of the joint-tenancy value in the first-dying joint tenant's gross estate, reduced only by the survivor's affirmatively-documented out-of-pocket contribution.[1]

Congress carved out a spousal safe harbor in the Tax Reform Act of 1976 §2002(c), which enacted the §2040(b) qualified-joint-interest rule effective for joint tenancies created after December 31, 1976.[7] The 1976 rule had several complexities — including a gift-tax election on creation and a threshold-of-contribution test — that Congress simplified in the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 §403(c), which produced the modern §2040(b) rule: an automatic 50% inclusion for a qualified joint interest between spouses regardless of which spouse actually paid.[8] The 1981 amendments applied retroactively to interests created after 1976, so the modern §2040(b) rule governs all spousal jointly-held property created between January 1, 1977, and the present.[8]

The rule has been substantively unchanged since 1981 in its §2040(a) and §2040(b) subsections. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 (Public Law 119-21) did not amend §2040 directly, but the OBBBA §70106 permanent $15,000,000 basic-exclusion-amount indexation changes the economic pressure on §2040 planning: the increased exemption means most §2040 inclusions produce no cash tax at the first death but still affect the surviving spouse's DSUE portability and the §1014 basis-step-up computation.[5]

§2040(a) — the general consideration-furnished rule

The current text of §2040(a) provides that the value of the gross estate shall include the value of all property to the extent of the interest therein held as joint tenants by the decedent and any other person, or as tenants by the entirety by the decedent and spouse, or deposited, with any person carrying on the banking business, in their joint names and payable to either or the survivor, except such part thereof as may be shown to have originally belonged to such other person and never to have been received or acquired by the latter from the decedent for less than an adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth.[1] The section closes with the reciprocal rule that where property has been acquired by gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance by the decedent and any other person as joint tenants or tenants by the entirety, there shall be excluded from the value of the gross estate a fractional part determined by the number of joint tenants.[1]

Treas. The Peters, Endicott Trust, and Fratini paper-trail cases

The classical §2040(a) paper-trail cases establish the evidentiary bar for the survivor's contribution defense.[10] Estate of Peters v. Commissioner, 386 F.2d 404 (4th Cir. 1967), rejected the survivor's testimony that she had contributed to a jointly-owned residence when she could produce no contemporaneous banking records to substantiate the contribution — the Fourth Circuit held that the taxpayer's burden under §2040(a) requires documentary evidence, not just credible testimony, and affirmed the Tax Court's finding of 100% inclusion in the deceased husband's gross estate.[11] Endicott Trust Co. v. United States, 305 F. Supp. 943 (N.D.N.Y. 1969), applied the same rule to a joint bank account funded partially by the surviving joint tenant's separate wages — the district court allowed the pro rata reduction where the wage records were produced but denied credit for unlabeled deposits the survivor could not trace to separate funds.[12] Estate of Fratini v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 1998-308, applied the rule to a modern brokerage joint tenancy where the surviving joint tenant had contributed proceeds from a prior sale of her separate real property; the Tax Court allowed the contribution credit only for the traceable proceeds.[13]

The "burden on the survivor" trap

The §2040(a) consideration-furnished test places the burden of proof on the taxpayer (the executor of the deceased joint tenant's estate, whose interests are aligned with reducing the estate's tax exposure) to affirmatively document the survivor's contribution. The IRS's default position on audit is 100% inclusion. The executor's failure to produce contemporaneous banking records, deeds of trust, or mortgage payment history over the entire history of the joint tenancy will produce a full 100% inclusion — even where the survivor unquestionably made substantial contributions but cannot documentarily prove them. On a decades-old joint tenancy where the underlying records are lost, the 100% inclusion is often the only defensible position.

§2040(b) — the qualified joint interest 50/50 safe harbor

§2040(b), enacted by TRA §2002(c) and refined by ERTA §403(c), provides an automatic 50% inclusion rule for a "qualified joint interest" between spouses.[7] A qualified joint interest is defined in §2040(b)(2) as any interest in property held by the decedent and the decedent's spouse as (A) tenants by the entirety, or (B) joint tenants with right of survivorship if the decedent and the decedent's spouse are the only joint tenants.[1] When the qualified-joint-interest rules apply, exactly 50% of the date-of-death fair market value is included in the first-dying spouse's gross estate — regardless of whether one spouse paid 100% of the purchase price, regardless of whether the property was acquired before or during the marriage (subject to the pre-1977 grandfather discussed below), and regardless of the source of any improvements.[1]

Qualified Joint Interest Inclusion = Date-of-Death FMV × 50%

The §2040(b) rule eliminates the consideration-furnished paper-trail exercise for spousal jointly-held property acquired after December 31, 1976. The 50% inclusion is then offset by the §2056 marital deduction if the property passes outright to the surviving spouse under the survivorship feature — which is the default result for a qualified joint interest, because the survivorship feature carries 100% of the property to the surviving spouse by operation of state property law.[14]

Key rule — automatic 50/50 for post-1976 spousal joint interests

For any qualified joint interest between spouses created after December 31, 1976, the first-dying spouse's gross-estate inclusion is exactly 50% of the date-of-death fair market value — no consideration-furnished test, no documentary burden, no allocation dispute. The 50% is then offset by the §2056 marital deduction on outright pass-through to the surviving spouse. Federal estate tax at the first death is zero (subject to the surviving spouse being a U.S. citizen; a non-citizen surviving spouse requires a §2056A qualified domestic trust or the marital deduction is lost).

The pre-1977 Gallenstein grandfather and the double-basis-step-up windfall

The pre-1977 Gallenstein doctrine is one of the most consequential grandfather provisions in the U.S. transfer-tax code — and one of the most underused planning opportunities available to surviving spouses in their 70s, 80s, and 90s.[16] The doctrine grew from Gallenstein v. United States, 975 F.2d 286 (6th Cir. 1992), Patten v. United States, 116 F.3d 1029 (4th Cir. 1997), Baszto v. United States, 80 F. Supp. 2d 969 (M.D. Fla. 1999), and Hahn v. Commissioner, 110 T.C. 140 (1998), which held that the §2040(b) qualified-joint-interest 50/50 rule does not apply retroactively to spousal jointly-held property acquired before its January 1, 1977 effective date.[17]

For pre-1977 spousal joint tenancies, the pre-TRA §2040(a) consideration-furnished test still governs. The doctrinal consequence is enormous for a surviving spouse where the deceased spouse paid the full purchase price of a jointly-titled asset before 1977: 100% of the date-of-death fair market value is included in the deceased spouse's gross estate under §2040(a), which pushes 100% of the pre-death appreciation into the §1014 basis-step-up rule.[18]

The Gallenstein result The pre-1977 spousal joint tenancy where one spouse funded the entire purchase produces a full 100% basis step-up at the first spousal death — dwarfing the §2040(b) 50% step-up available for post-1976 marital joint tenancies. On a $2M pre-1977 residence with a $50,000 original basis, the Gallenstein 100% step-up saves the surviving spouse ~$487,000 in future capital-gains tax at the current 23.8% federal rate compared to the §2040(b) 50% step-up.

Gallenstein applies only to pre-1977 joint tenancies where the deceased spouse contributed 100%

The Gallenstein result is favorable only where the deceased spouse contributed 100% of the pre-1977 purchase price. Where the surviving spouse contributed a documentable portion, the §2040(a) consideration-furnished test reduces the deceased spouse's inclusion by that portion — and the surviving spouse's continuing separate share carries its original cost basis into the survivorship-succession posture with no step-up.[13] The optimal Gallenstein fact pattern is one where (1) the joint tenancy was acquired before January 1, 1977; (2) the deceased spouse (typically the historically-higher-earning spouse) paid 100% of the purchase price and any improvements; (3) the surviving spouse can affirmatively concede zero contribution; and (4) the property has appreciated substantially since the pre-1977 acquisition.

The IRS's 1998 Chief Counsel Advice acquiescence

The IRS initially litigated Gallenstein by arguing that the §2040(b) rule superseded the §2040(a) rule for all spousal joint tenancies regardless of the date of creation. After losses in Gallenstein, Patten, Baszto, and Hahn, the IRS acquiesced by non-appeal (Rev. Rul. 98-8, 1998-1 C.B. 541, formalizing the position) and now accepts the Gallenstein analysis on audit and in Form 706 preparation.[19] The current administrative position of the IRS is that §2040(a) governs pre-1977 spousal joint tenancies and §2040(b) governs post-1976 spousal joint tenancies, with the basis-step-up consequences flowing accordingly.[19]

The §1014 basis-step-up trap on jointly-held property

§1014(a) generally provides that the basis of property in the hands of a person acquiring it from a decedent shall be its fair market value at the date of the decedent's death (or the §2032 alternate-valuation-date value if the executor elects).[20] §1014(b)(9) confirms that jointly-held property to the extent included in the decedent's gross estate under §2040 qualifies for the §1014 step-up on the included portion. The trap is the asymmetry between §2040(a) and §2040(b):[21]

Practical planning implication — the Gallenstein-vs-2040(b) tradeoff

For a marital residence acquired in 1968 for $50,000 and now worth $2,000,000, the Gallenstein 100% step-up saves ~$487,000 in future capital-gains tax versus the §2040(b) 50% step-up. Estate planners of surviving spouses in their 70s and 80s should systematically ask about pre-1977 acquisitions and preserve Gallenstein-eligible fact patterns rather than dissolving pre-1977 joint tenancies into modern trust arrangements that would forfeit the grandfather.

Joint bank and brokerage accounts — the convenience-account trap

Joint bank accounts and joint brokerage accounts are the most common §2040 fact pattern outside marital real estate. A typical adult-child-plus-elderly-parent scenario runs like this: parent adds child to the parent's checking account "so the child can help pay the parent's bills when the parent gets sick." The account holds the parent's Social Security check, the parent's pension, and any parental savings. The child never deposits a dollar. Then the parent dies with $200,000 in the joint account.[22]

Under §2040(a), the entire $200,000 is included in the parent's gross estate because the child cannot document any out-of-pocket contribution — the account was funded entirely by the parent's separate income. The 100% inclusion applies regardless of what state law says about the child's post-death survivorship rights, regardless of the parent's intent that the account be a "convenience" arrangement, and regardless of the fact that the child never treated the account as the child's own during the parent's life.[22]

The convenience-account failure mode

A joint bank account created solely for the parent's convenience — where the child was added for signature authority but never contributed funds — produces 100% §2040(a) gross-estate inclusion on the parent's death, followed by 100% pass-through to the child by the survivorship feature (bypassing the parent's will, bypassing the parent's other beneficiaries, and bypassing any probate). The result is a double-whammy: the account is fully taxed in the parent's gross estate under §2040(a), and the account is fully inherited by the joint-tenant child under state property law rather than under the parent's estate plan. Estate planners should retitle such accounts as convenience accounts under the Uniform Multiple Person Accounts Act or as transfer-on-death (TOD) accounts under UPC §6-306 to avoid both the §2040 inclusion trap and the state-property-law bypass problem.

The Uniform Multiple Person Accounts Act — the convenience-account safe harbor

Most states have adopted some version of the Uniform Multiple Person Accounts Act (UMPAA), which permits a bank account to be titled explicitly as (1) a JTWROS account (survivorship intended), (2) a POD/TOD account (no lifetime interest in the second party — the second party takes only at the owner's death), or (3) a "convenience" account (no survivorship — the second party is a signatory for the owner's convenience during life but has no post-death interest).[23] A convenience-account titling avoids the §2040 inclusion entirely because there is no survivorship feature — the account passes on the owner's death to the owner's estate under §2033 and is distributed under the owner's will or under intestacy. The convenience-account titling also avoids the state-property-law bypass problem — the child does not automatically inherit the account balance by virtue of being a signatory.

Payable-on-death and transfer-on-death substitutes

Payable-on-death (POD) accounts for bank deposits and transfer-on-death (TOD) accounts for brokerage holdings are non-probate transfer arrangements in which the account owner retains sole lifetime rights to the account and designates a beneficiary who takes at death.[24] POD/TOD accounts are outside §2040 because there is no lifetime joint tenancy — the beneficiary has zero rights until the owner's death. POD/TOD accounts are included in the owner's gross estate under §2033 as owned property (the owner had 100% rights during life) and pass to the designated beneficiary at death outside probate. The §1014 basis step-up applies to the entire account at date of death (100%, not 50%), because 100% is included in the owner's gross estate.[24] The POD/TOD structure is thus the modern estate-planning replacement for a convenience-account joint tenancy — it produces the same intended result (child inherits the account outside probate) without the §2040(a) inclusion mess.

Community property is outside §2040 entirely

Community property is expressly outside the §2040 net. Treas. Reg. §20.2040-1(a)(1) confirms that §2040 applies only to property held as "joint tenants with right of survivorship," "tenants by the entirety," or joint owners of a bank or brokerage account with a survivorship feature.[9] Community property is a separate form of concurrent ownership governed by state community-property law in the nine classical community-property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin) plus the elective community-property regimes offered by Alaska (2015), South Dakota (2019), Tennessee (2010), Florida (2021), and Kentucky (2020).[25]

Community property passes at death under state law without a common-law survivorship feature — each spouse owns a one-half interest that passes by will or intestacy, not by right of survivorship. The §2033 general-inclusion rule pulls the decedent's one-half community-property share into the gross estate, and the §1014(b)(6) double-basis-step-up rule then steps up the basis on both the decedent's one-half share and the surviving spouse's one-half share to date-of-death fair market value.[25] The double-basis-step-up under §1014(b)(6) is the single most valuable tax feature of the community-property regime — it produces a full 100% basis reset on all community property at the first death, versus the 50% partial reset available under §2040(b) for common-law-state spousal joint tenancies.

Community property vs §2040(b) qualified joint interest

The community-property double-step-up under §1014(b)(6) produces a 100% basis reset on community property at the first spousal death. The §2040(b) qualified joint interest under common-law property law produces only a 50% basis reset on the deceased spouse's included share; the surviving spouse's continuing 50% carries original cost basis. For a highly-appreciated marital residence in a common-law state, the difference is often hundreds of thousands of dollars in future capital-gains tax exposure. States including Alaska, South Dakota, Tennessee, Florida, and Kentucky offer elective community-property regimes that allow common-law couples to opt into community-property treatment for specifically-titled assets — capturing the §1014(b)(6) double-step-up for property that would otherwise be governed by §2040(b).

§2056 marital-deduction coordination

For a §2040(b) qualified joint interest between spouses, the 50% gross-estate inclusion is fully offset by the §2056 marital deduction because the property passes outright to the surviving spouse by operation of the survivorship feature — meaning no terminable-interest disqualification under §2056(b)(1)(A) and no need for a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election.[26] The result at the first spousal death is zero cash federal estate tax on the jointly-held property, with the 50% included value increasing the deceased spouse's aggregate taxable-estate line but wholly offset by the §2056 deduction.

The surviving spouse then owns 100% of the property (50% under continuation of the surviving spouse's original share plus 50% by survivorship), and the surviving spouse's §1014 basis-step-up applies to the 50% that came from the deceased spouse. At the surviving spouse's later death, 100% of the property is included in the surviving spouse's gross estate under §2033 as owned property, offset by the surviving spouse's own §2010(c) $15M exclusion plus any DSUE portability from the first spousal death under §2010(c)(4).[27]

The non-citizen surviving spouse trap under §2056(d)

The §2056 marital deduction is disallowed where the surviving spouse is not a U.S. citizen at the time of the decedent's death — a bright-line rule under §2056(d)(1) that captures the tax-avoidance concern that a non-citizen surviving spouse can leave the U.S. and take the un-taxed marital property beyond U.S. jurisdiction.[28] The §2056A qualified domestic trust (QDOT) is the workaround: a QDOT is a trust that meets specific requirements (a U.S. trustee, distributions subject to withholding, and specific bond or letter-of-credit requirements for QDOTs holding more than $2 million of assets) and preserves the marital deduction for property placed in the trust for the benefit of the non-citizen surviving spouse.[28] For a §2040(b) qualified joint interest passing by survivorship to a non-citizen surviving spouse, the marital deduction is disallowed absent affirmative planning to place the surviving spouse's post-survivorship share in a QDOT — the property passes to the surviving spouse outside probate by operation of state law, so a QDOT arrangement must be pre-planned rather than post-death remediation.

Overlap with §§2033, 2036, and 2038

§2040 does not operate in isolation. A single jointly-held asset can trigger multiple Chapter 11 sections, and the overlap analysis is critical to correct gross-estate valuation.[3]

Three worked case studies

Case study 1 — Post-1976 spousal residence, both spouses on title from acquisition

Married couple in Ohio (a common-law property state) acquires a residence in 1998 titled as JTWROS. Husband pays the entire $250,000 purchase price from his separate premarital funds. Wife contributes no separate funds to the acquisition or improvements. Husband dies in 2026 when the residence is worth $850,000. Under §2040(b), exactly 50% of the $850,000 date-of-death FMV = $425,000 is included in Husband's gross estate — regardless of the fact that Husband paid 100% of the purchase price. The $425,000 is offset in full by the §2056 marital deduction because the property passes outright to Wife by survivorship. Zero cash federal estate tax at the first death. Wife's cost basis in the residence at Husband's death: $125,000 original share (half of the $250,000 purchase) plus $425,000 stepped-up share = $550,000. Wife owns 100% of the residence. On Wife's later death in 2036 when the residence is worth $1,200,000, 100% of the residence is included in Wife's gross estate under §2033, offset by Wife's own $15M §2010(c) exclusion.

Case study 2 — Pre-1977 spousal residence, Gallenstein double-step-up

Married couple in Virginia acquires a residence in 1968 titled as tenants by the entirety. Husband pays the entire $52,000 purchase price from his premarital savings. Wife contributes no separate funds to the acquisition or improvements. Husband dies in 2026 when the residence is worth $1,950,000. Under the Gallenstein doctrine, §2040(a) applies (not §2040(b)) because the tenancy was created before January 1, 1977. Because Husband contributed 100% and Wife contributed nothing documentable, 100% of the $1,950,000 date-of-death FMV = $1,950,000 is included in Husband's gross estate. The full $1,950,000 is offset in full by the §2056 marital deduction because the property passes outright to Wife by survivorship. Zero cash federal estate tax at the first death. Wife's cost basis in the residence: full $1,950,000 stepped-up basis under §1014(a) — the full 100% Gallenstein basis step-up. If Wife later sells the residence for $2,050,000 shortly after Husband's death, Wife's federal capital gain is $100,000 (the $2,050,000 sale minus the $1,950,000 stepped-up basis) — versus $1,050,000 of gain that would have resulted under §2040(b) 50/50 treatment (only $975,000 of stepped-up basis plus $25,000 of continuing Wife-share basis).

Case study 3 — Parent-child joint bank account with convenience-account intent

78-year-old Widow in Michigan adds her 52-year-old adult son to her checking account "so he can help pay bills when I get sick." Widow's Social Security check and pension are direct-deposited to the account. Widow's utility bills, property taxes, and grocery expenses are paid from the account. Son never deposits any of his separate funds and treats the account solely as Widow's account for signature-authority purposes. Widow dies in 2026 with $185,000 in the account. Under §2040(a), 100% of the $185,000 is included in Widow's gross estate because Son cannot document any out-of-pocket contribution. Son inherits 100% of the $185,000 by the survivorship feature outside probate. Federal estate tax consequence: Widow's aggregate gross estate is well below the $15M §2010(c) exclusion so no cash tax is due, but the $185,000 is nonetheless a §2040(a) inclusion item on Widow's Form 706 (if a return is required for portability-election purposes). Son's cost basis in the $185,000: full $185,000 stepped-up basis under §1014(a) — the 100% §2040(a) inclusion carries a 100% basis step-up. The convenience-account trap here is not the tax result (the $15M exclusion absorbs the inclusion easily) but the estate-plan-bypass problem: Widow's will leaves everything to her three children in equal thirds, but the $185,000 checking account bypasses the will and goes 100% to Son under state survivorship law. Widow's actual family-transfer plan is dis-served by the account structure. Retitling the account as a POD account to Widow's estate before Widow's death would have preserved the estate-plan distribution and eliminated any §2040(a) analysis.

Practitioner action checklist

Before filing Form 706 for any decedent with jointly-titled property, joint bank accounts, joint brokerage accounts, or tenancy-by-entirety real estate, run this eight-item checklist:

  1. Inventory every jointly-titled asset. Identify every JTWROS deed, every tenancy-by-entirety deed, every joint bank account, every joint brokerage account, every joint safe-deposit-box arrangement, and every jointly-titled vehicle. Do not overlook informal jointly-titled accounts the decedent set up during life for convenience purposes — the survivorship feature invokes §2040 regardless of the decedent's subjective intent.
  2. Determine whether §2040(a) or §2040(b) governs. For each spousal joint tenancy, determine the date of creation. If created after December 31, 1976, §2040(b) 50/50 applies automatically. If created before January 1, 1977, the Gallenstein doctrine preserves §2040(a) consideration-furnished analysis — which can produce a favorable 100% basis step-up if the deceased spouse contributed the full purchase price. For non-spousal joint tenancies, §2040(a) always applies regardless of the date of creation.
  3. Assemble the survivor's contribution paper trail. For every §2040(a) joint tenancy where the survivor may have contributed separate funds, assemble contemporaneous banking records, mortgage payment history, separate-property titling deeds, and any inheritance or gift documentation. Where the decedent gifted the contribution to the survivor before the joint tenancy was created, the survivor's contribution is disregarded and 100% inclusion is restored under the money's-worth test.
  4. Compute the §2040 inclusion on the Form 706 Schedule E. For §2040(b) qualified joint interests, the inclusion is exactly 50% of the date-of-death FMV. For §2040(a) joint tenancies, the inclusion is 100% of the FMV minus the survivor's documented contribution percentage. Complete Schedule E with the full property description, the date of creation of the joint tenancy, and the contribution analysis.
  5. Coordinate with §§2033, 2036, and 2038. For each jointly-held asset, run the overlap analysis. Where the decedent retained a life interest in the property beyond the joint tenancy itself, §2036 may reach the entire property. Where the decedent retained the sole power to sever the joint tenancy, §2038 may reach the entire property. Apply the section producing the larger inclusion under Treas. Reg. §20.2040-1(a).
  6. Compute the §1014 basis step-up for the surviving joint tenant. The §1014 step-up applies to the portion of the property included in the decedent's gross estate under §2040. For §2040(b) qualified joint interests, 50% of the basis is stepped up. For §2040(a) Gallenstein-eligible pre-1977 spousal joint tenancies with 100% deceased-spouse contribution, the full 100% basis is stepped up. For §2040(a) non-spousal joint tenancies with mixed contributions, only the included portion is stepped up.
  7. Analyze the §2056 marital deduction on §2040(b) qualified joint interests. For a spousal joint interest passing to a U.S.-citizen surviving spouse by survivorship, the §2056 marital deduction is automatic and offsets the 50% §2040(b) inclusion. For a non-citizen surviving spouse, the deduction is disallowed absent a §2056A QDOT arrangement — which cannot easily be established post-death for property passing outside probate.
  8. Consider prospective retitling for convenience-account failure modes. Where the decedent is elderly and the family relies on a joint-signatory arrangement for bill-paying purposes, retitle the account under the Uniform Multiple Person Accounts Act as a convenience account (no survivorship feature) or as a POD/TOD account (survivorship replaced by a beneficiary designation). Both alternatives avoid the §2040(a) 100% inclusion trap and preserve the decedent's estate-plan intent for post-death distribution.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2040 do in 2026?

IRC §2040 is the joint-tenancy inclusion rule in Chapter 11. It pulls into the decedent's gross estate the value of any property held as JTWROS, tenancy by the entirety, or a joint bank/brokerage account with a survivorship feature. Under §2040(a) the starting point is 100% inclusion of the date-of-death fair market value, reduced only by the survivor's affirmatively-documented contribution. Under §2040(b) a qualified joint interest between spouses (created after 1976) is exactly 50% regardless of who paid. The section reaches jointly-titled real estate, joint bank accounts, joint brokerage accounts, joint safe-deposit-box contents, and jointly-owned vehicles. It does not reach tenancy in common (§2033) or community property (§1014(b)(6)).

How does the §2040(a) consideration-furnished test work?

§2040(a) provides that the gross estate includes the value of jointly-held property except such part as may be shown to have originally belonged to the survivor and never to have been received from the decedent for less than full consideration. The burden is on the survivor to affirmatively document contribution — the default is 100% inclusion. The mechanical formula: Inclusion = Date-of-Death FMV × (1 − survivor's proportionate contribution). Peters v. Commissioner, Endicott Trust Co. v. United States, and Estate of Fratini establish the paper-trail requirements. Where the survivor cannot document contribution, the full value is included.

What is the §2040(b) qualified joint interest safe harbor?

§2040(b), enacted by TRA §2002(c) and refined by ERTA §403(c), provides an automatic 50% inclusion rule for a qualified joint interest between spouses. A qualified joint interest is any spousal tenancy by the entirety or spousal JTWROS if the spouses are the only joint tenants. When it applies, 50% of the FMV is included regardless of who paid. The 50% is offset by the §2056 marital deduction on outright pass-through to the surviving spouse. Effective for interests created after December 31, 1976.

What is the pre-1977 Gallenstein doctrine and does it still matter in 2026?

Yes — Gallenstein v. United States (975 F.2d 286), Patten v. United States (116 F.3d 1029), Baszto v. United States, and Hahn v. Commissioner held that §2040(b) does not apply retroactively to pre-1977 spousal joint tenancies. The §2040(a) consideration-furnished test still governs. For a pre-1977 spousal tenancy where the deceased spouse paid 100%, the result is a full 100% inclusion plus a full 100% §1014 basis step-up — the double-basis-step-up windfall. The IRS acquiesced in Rev. Rul. 98-8 and now accepts Gallenstein on audit. The doctrine is still live for surviving spouses in their late 70s, 80s, and 90s who acquired their marital residence before 1977.

How is a joint bank or brokerage account taxed under §2040?

A spousal JTWROS account created after 1976 is 50% included under §2040(b), offset by the §2056 marital deduction. A non-spousal JTWROS account is 100% included under §2040(a) unless the surviving joint tenant can prove contribution from separate funds. The most common failure is a parent-child convenience account funded entirely by the parent's income — 100% included in the parent's gross estate on death regardless of the child's post-death survivorship rights. The Uniform Multiple Person Accounts Act permits explicit convenience-account titling (no survivorship) or POD/TOD titling (no lifetime joint interest) — both avoid the §2040 inclusion trap.

Does §2040 apply to community property?

No. Treas. Reg. §20.2040-1(a)(1) limits §2040 to JTWROS, tenancy by the entirety, and joint bank/brokerage accounts. Community property in the nine classical community-property states (AZ, CA, ID, LA, NV, NM, TX, WA, WI) plus five elective-regime states (AK, SD, TN, FL, KY) is governed by §2033 (50% inclusion of the decedent's community share) plus §1014(b)(6) (full 100% basis step-up on both halves — the community-property double-step-up). The double-step-up is the most valuable feature of the community-property regime and dwarfs the §2040(b) 50% partial reset.

How does §2040 interact with the §2056 marital deduction?

For a §2040(b) qualified joint interest, the 50% inclusion is fully offset by the §2056 marital deduction because the property passes outright to the surviving spouse by survivorship — no terminable-interest disqualification, no need for QTIP election. Zero cash federal estate tax at first death. Surviving spouse receives 50% basis step-up on the deceased spouse's share; own 50% carries original basis. At surviving spouse's death, 100% is in the surviving spouse's gross estate under §2033, offset by the surviving spouse's own $15M exclusion plus DSUE portability. For non-spousal §2040(a) joint tenancies, no marital deduction applies.

What is the §1014 basis-step-up trap on jointly-held property?

§1014(b)(9) provides that jointly-held property gets basis step-up on the portion included under §2040. The asymmetry: §2040(a) pre-1977 spousal Gallenstein produces 100% inclusion and 100% step-up; §2040(b) post-1976 spousal produces 50% inclusion and 50% step-up. On a $2M marital residence with $50K original basis, the Gallenstein 100% step-up saves ~$487K in future capital-gains tax at the 23.8% federal rate versus the §2040(b) 50% step-up. Estate planners of pre-1977-marital surviving spouses should preserve Gallenstein-eligible fact patterns rather than dissolving pre-1977 joint tenancies into trust arrangements.

Can the survivor argue partial contribution to reduce a §2040(a) inclusion?

Yes — the §2040(a) test is partial-contribution, not all-or-nothing. Inclusion is reduced dollar-for-dollar by the survivor's proportionate contribution. Example: parent and child buy a $500K rental, parent pays $300K, child pays $200K from documented separate funds. On parent's death when the property is worth $800K, the §2040(a) inclusion is $800K × 60% = $480K. Documentation is critical — Treas. Reg. §20.2040-1(a)(2) requires contemporaneous banking and mortgage records. Where the survivor's contribution was itself gifted by the decedent, the money's-worth test restores 100% inclusion.

How does §2040 interact with the $15 million §2010(c) basic exclusion amount under OBBBA?

OBBBA §70106 (Public Law 119-21, July 2025) permanently set the §2010(c)(3) exclusion at $15,000,000 per person for post-2025 decedents. §2040 operates independently: the §2040(a) or §2040(b) inclusion is added to the aggregate taxable estate, and the $15M unified credit offsets tentative tax. For estates below $15M, §2040 inclusions produce no cash tax. For estates near or above, a $6M jointly-titled residence produces a $3M §2040(b) inclusion (offset by §2056 marital deduction on outright pass-through, so no cash tax at first death but full basis step-up on the 50% included). A $6M non-spousal joint account with no documented survivor contribution produces a $6M §2040(a) inclusion. Planning focuses on retitling non-spousal joint accounts to convenience or TOD formats before the death that triggers §2040(a) inclusion.

Conclusion — §2040 as the sixth Chapter 11 inclusion pillar

§2040 opens the sixth Chapter 11 inclusion-rule pillar beyond the four-pillar §§2035/2036/2038/2042 retained-interest foundation and the §2039 annuity-and-pension-plan pillar we walked in the five earlier pieces of this arc. The retained-interest pillars govern property that a decedent transferred during life while retaining control or economic benefit. §2039 governs periodic-payment arrangements — annuities and pensions — with survivor features. §2040 governs a still-narrower class: property held at date of death under a form of concurrent ownership with a right of survivorship. The three frameworks work together to reach the three most common estate-planning informalities in the United States: retained-interest gifts to family members, retirement-plan and annuity survivor arrangements, and jointly-titled real estate and financial accounts.

For estates below the $15M §2010(c) threshold, §2040 inclusions produce increases in the taxable-estate line but no cash tax. For estates above the threshold, the §2040(a) full-consideration test on non-spousal joint accounts can transform an informally-arranged parent-child convenience account into a substantial gross-estate inclusion, and the pre-1977 Gallenstein grandfather on marital residences can produce a double-basis-step-up windfall that shelters hundreds of thousands of dollars of capital-gains tax. The estate planner's job at every death is to inventory the jointly-titled arrangements, determine whether §2040(a) or §2040(b) governs, assemble the survivor's contribution paper trail where §2040(a) applies, run the §1014 basis-step-up computation on the included portion, coordinate the §2056 marital-deduction analysis for spousal joint interests, and — prospectively for the surviving spouse or surviving joint tenant — analyze whether the jointly-titled arrangements should be retitled before the second death to convenience-account, POD/TOD, or trust-based alternatives that better serve the family's transfer objectives.
