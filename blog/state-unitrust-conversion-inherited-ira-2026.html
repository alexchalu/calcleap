State Unitrust Conversion in 2026: The Non-UFIPA §106 Field Guide for Trust-Held Inherited IRAs

In the 42 non-UFIPA U.S. jurisdictions, the state unitrust conversion statute is the permanent-conversion alternative to the annual §104 power-to-adjust. It delivers a fixed 3-5 percent of net fair market value each year, falls squarely inside the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) QTIP marital-deduction safe harbor, and — for a trust holding an inherited IRA subject to the SECURE Act 10-year rule — provides the smoothest cash-flow profile the state-law toolkit offers. Here is the full mechanic, the state-by-state percentage variation, the notice-content requirements, the reconversion procedure, and a worked $2M New York QTIP case study.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 19, 2026 25 min read How we research

The August 18, 2026 §104 power-to-adjust deep-dive walked the load-bearing annual-discretion mechanic that lets a trustee reallocate between trust accounting income and principal in a non-UFIPA state.[1] That piece named the alternative that a substantial minority of inherited-IRA trustees ultimately choose: the state unitrust conversion statute — modeled on 1997 Uniform Principal and Income Act §106 and enacted with state-specific variations in the 42 non-UFIPA jurisdictions — which converts the trust's income accounting regime from a classical income-versus-principal split into a fixed percentage of the trust's net fair market value distributed to the income beneficiary each year.[2] This is the deep-dive on that mechanic.

The unitrust conversion is the trustee's tool of first resort when three conditions coincide. First, the trust holds a large enough IRA that the §409 default characterization — 10 percent of the required minimum distribution to income, 90 percent to principal — produces a material impartiality problem year over year. Second, the trust's beneficiary constellation includes an income beneficiary who values cash-flow predictability more than year-by-year flexibility (a surviving spouse in a QTIP trust is the paradigm case). Third, the trustee prefers a one-time procedural burden — a notice-and-objection cycle at conversion — over the recurring per-year burden of a §104 factor-by-factor memorandum. All three conditions describe the modal trust-held inherited IRA in a non-UFIPA state under the SECURE Act 10-year rule.[3]

§106 at the uniform-text level

1997 UPIA §106 is the sibling provision to §104 — where §104 grants annual discretion to reallocate, §106 grants the authority to make a permanent conversion. The uniform text is longer than §104's compact five-subsection structure because §106 must also specify the conversion procedure, the ongoing operational rules, the reconversion procedure, and the interaction with the trust instrument. The state adaptations retain most of §106's structure but modify the specific percentage (fixed 4 percent versus 3-5 percent discretionary range), the notice period (30 versus 60 days), and the objection procedure.[4]

The §106(a) grant is procedural: subject to the trust instrument and the state's specific rules, a trustee may convert an income trust to a total return unitrust as provided in the statute. The trust instrument controls if it expressly prohibits conversion or expressly authorizes only a specified different distribution regime; absent such language, the statute applies.

The §106(b) percentage rule is where the state variation is most pronounced. The uniform text at §106(b) states a 4 percent default, with an optional trustee discretion in a 3-5 percent range in states that adopted the "power to select" variant. New York locked in the mandatory 4 percent with no trustee discretion; Texas adopted the 3-5 percent range with express trustee discretion; Illinois adopted the 4 percent default with a modified discretion mechanic; most other states track the uniform text closely.

The §106(c) valuation rule requires the trustee to determine the trust's net fair market value at least annually using a reasonable valuation method, and specifies that the fixed percentage is applied to a smoothed average — typically the three-year rolling average of the trust's net fair market value on the first business day of each of the three preceding calendar years. The three-year smoothing serves two purposes: it dampens year-to-year cash-flow volatility that the raw value would produce, and it partially cushions the SECURE Act year-10 acceleration by including pre-drawdown balances in the year-10 denominator.[5]

The §106(d) reconversion procedure mirrors the initial conversion mechanic — 30 to 60 days advance notice to all current and remainder beneficiaries stating the intended reconversion, the effective date, the reasons, and each beneficiary's right to object. The reconversion procedure exists because circumstances change: the initial reasons that justified the unitrust conversion may no longer apply if the trust's asset composition changes, if the income beneficiary's cash-flow needs change, or if the tax landscape shifts.

The §106(e) beneficiary-objection procedure is the mechanic's most important procedural constraint. If any beneficiary — including a remainder beneficiary or a contingent beneficiary — objects within the notice period, the conversion cannot proceed without either (i) the trustee obtaining judicial approval through a court proceeding, or (ii) all beneficiaries entering into a written consent overriding the objection. This is the point at which unitrust conversion most differs from a §104 power-to-adjust election, which requires no notice and no beneficiary participation.

The notice-content requirements and the 30-60 day objection window

Every state unitrust conversion statute contains a notice-content requirement that is legally load-bearing. A conversion effected without compliant notice is voidable at a beneficiary's option, and a trustee who converts without proper notice risks personal liability for lost income or lost investment opportunity to a beneficiary who successfully challenges the conversion. The notice-content specifications are statutory and specific; the trustee cannot substitute a general "I'm converting this trust" letter.

The notice must state, at minimum: (1) the trustee's identity and address; (2) the identity of the trust being converted (name, date, and any trust identification number); (3) the intended effective date of the conversion; (4) the specific percentage that will apply after conversion (4 percent, 3-5 percent, or the state-specific rate); (5) the valuation methodology that will be used, including whether the state's three-year smoothing formula applies; (6) the trustee's reasons for the conversion, tied to the trust's specific facts; (7) each beneficiary's right to object within the notice period; and (8) the address at which objections must be delivered. Some states — New York, Texas, Illinois — require additional specific content; the trustee must consult the specific state statute.

The notice must go to all current beneficiaries and all remainder beneficiaries, including contingent remainder beneficiaries whose interests are not yet vested. This is a wider notice list than the trustee might initially assume; a trust with a life-tenant surviving spouse and a class of remainder beneficiaries "to my then-living descendants per stirpes" requires notice to every currently-living descendant identified with reasonable diligence. Notice to the trustee's own file is not notice. Notice to a lawyer for a beneficiary is not notice unless the lawyer has authority to receive notice; the safer default is to serve the beneficiary directly and copy the lawyer.

The notice-content trap

A frequent conversion error is a notice that recites the state's specific-percentage rule but does not attempt to explain the trustee's reasons for converting on the specific trust. A generic "the trustee proposes to convert this trust to a state unitrust with a 4 percent annual distribution rate" fails the notice-content requirement in every non-UFIPA state that requires the notice to state the reasons for the conversion (which is most of them). The reasons must be trust-specific: the trust's income beneficiary has cash-flow needs that the §409 default does not meet, the trust holds a growing inherited IRA whose fair market value has increased substantially since the account owner's death, the trustee wishes to preserve the QTIP marital deduction under Rev. Rul. 2006-26 and Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f), and so on. A notice reciting these trust-specific reasons materially reduces the risk of a beneficiary objection and provides the trustee's record for judicial review if an objection nonetheless occurs.

New York EPTL §11-2.4 — the mandatory 4 percent

New York adopted the unitrust conversion mechanic in 2001 as EPTL §11-2.4, effective January 1, 2002.[7] The New York rule fixes the unitrust amount at 4 percent of the smoothed fair market value with no trustee discretion to select a different rate. The trade-off the New York legislature made was clarity over flexibility: the 4 percent rate provides beneficiaries with a certain expectation and eliminates the trustee's exposure to a "wrong rate" claim, at the cost of preventing the trustee from responding to trust-specific facts that might argue for a different rate.

For an inherited-IRA trust in New York, the 4 percent rate produces an annual distribution that typically matches the required minimum distribution under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4(f) reasonably well in years 1-9 of the 10-year window. In year 10, the residual acceleration exceeds the 4 percent smoothed amount substantially; the trustee's typical response is to combine the unitrust conversion with a supplemental §104 power-to-adjust adjustment in year 10 specifically, capturing the acceleration as an income distribution rather than sending the residual to principal at year-end.

Texas Property Code §116.006 — the 3-5 percent discretionary range

Texas adopted the unitrust conversion mechanic in 2003 as Property Code §116.006, part of the Texas Uniform Principal and Income Act (Chapter 116).[8] The Texas rule provides a 3-5 percent discretionary range, with the trustee selecting the specific rate at the time of conversion. The trustee's rate selection is documented in the conversion notice to beneficiaries and is a permanent aspect of the trust's ongoing accounting regime — changing the rate after conversion requires a reconversion under §116.006(d) followed by a new conversion at the new rate.

The Texas discretion is valuable for inherited-IRA trusts in specific fact patterns. A trust with a high-net-worth income beneficiary already in the top individual federal bracket may benefit from a lower rate (say 3 percent) that reduces the income-tax drag on the beneficiary's after-tax cash flow. A trust with a modest-net-worth income beneficiary in a lower federal bracket may benefit from a higher rate (say 5 percent) that captures more of the trust's compressed-bracket arbitrage. The trustee's rate selection should be documented with the same rigor as a §104 factor analysis — the trustee's reasons for the specific rate become part of the record on judicial review.

Illinois 760 ILCS 3/106 — the 4 percent default with modified discretion

Illinois adopted the Illinois Trust Code in 2019, effective January 1, 2020, with 760 ILCS 3/106 as the unitrust conversion provision.[9] The Illinois rule uses a 4 percent default but permits the trustee to select a different rate with a specific justification tied to the trust's facts. Illinois's twist is procedural: the trustee's justification for a non-default rate must be delivered to beneficiaries in the notice, giving beneficiaries the specific information they need to evaluate whether to object.

The Illinois modification is well-suited to inherited-IRA trusts because it lets the trustee tie the rate selection to the specific SECURE Act 10-year rule fact pattern the trust faces. A trust with a decedent who died in 2020 and is in year 6 of the drawdown may reasonably select a rate above 4 percent for the remaining years of the drawdown; a trust in year 1 with 10 full years ahead may reasonably select a rate at or below 4 percent to preserve principal for the year-10 acceleration.

Ohio §5812.05 and Pennsylvania 20 Pa.C.S. §8106 — the 3-5 percent variants

Ohio's unitrust conversion statute at Rev. Code §5812.05 also uses a 4 percent default with trustee discretion in the 3-5 percent range, mirroring the Texas approach with the 4 percent starting point as the default.[10] Ohio's ancillary §5808.18 decanting statute — as flagged in the August 18 §104 piece — provides a natural alternative path for trustees who conclude the unitrust mechanic is not the right long-term solution: decanting to a new trust with an express distribution formula eliminates the annual valuation-plus-percentage exercise.

Pennsylvania's unitrust conversion statute at 20 Pa.C.S. §8106 uses a 4 percent default with trustee discretion to depart within a "reasonable" range with justification.[11] Pennsylvania is unique among the surveyed states in using a 30-day notice period rather than the 60-day norm; trustees should confirm the shorter period at the specific state's current statute before publishing the notice. Pennsylvania's PIT retirement-distribution exclusion continues to create a state-income-tax quirk: unitrust distributions from an inherited IRA to a Pennsylvania resident retain the retirement-distribution character for PIT purposes, so the state-tax analysis differs from the classical income-tax analysis in most other jurisdictions.

Massachusetts, New Jersey, Georgia, Michigan, and North Carolina — the 4 percent defaults

Five of the ten surveyed states use a straightforward 4 percent default with no trustee discretion to depart. Massachusetts G.L. c. 203D §5 (effective 2003), New Jersey N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-6 (effective 2002), Georgia O.C.G.A. §53-12-346 (effective 2018 as part of Georgia's UPIA modernization), Michigan MCL 555.506 (effective September 2004), and North Carolina §37A-1-106 (effective January 2004) all track the 1997 UPIA §106 uniform text at the 4 percent default with 60-day advance notice.[12]

For inherited-IRA trusts in these jurisdictions, the operational mechanic is nearly identical to the New York model: the trustee gives 60 days advance notice, waits out the objection period, converts on the effective date, and thereafter distributes 4 percent of the three-year smoothed fair market value to the income beneficiary each year. The absence of trustee rate-selection discretion simplifies the procedural burden but also constrains the trustee's ability to calibrate to specific trust facts; in practice, most trustees in these states use the 4 percent default without incident because the rate falls squarely within the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor.

The Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) QTIP marital-deduction safe harbor

The single most important federal-tax reason a trustee chooses unitrust conversion over §104 power-to-adjust is the Treasury Regulation §20.2056(b)-5(f)(1) safe harbor for state-law unitrust distributions in QTIP trusts. Under IRC §2056(b)(7), a QTIP trust must distribute "all the income" to the surviving spouse annually to qualify for the marital deduction on the deceased spouse's estate. The classical trust-accounting definition of "income" is a fixed dollar amount driven by the income-versus-principal characterization rules; a unitrust's percentage-of-value distribution is not "income" in that classical sense. Absent regulatory guidance, a unitrust distribution to a QTIP surviving spouse would arguably disqualify the QTIP election.[13]

Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f)(1) resolves the tension. The regulation establishes that a state-law unitrust conversion satisfying certain conditions qualifies as producing a stream of "income" for QTIP purposes, notwithstanding the classical accounting mismatch. The safe harbor requires four conditions:

  1. The unitrust rate must fall between 3 percent and 5 percent of the fair market value of the trust assets. All ten non-UFIPA state unitrust conversion statutes surveyed in this guide fall within this range at their default rates (4 percent) and — for the states that allow trustee rate selection — the discretionary range (3-5 percent) is coextensive with the safe harbor's range.
  2. The fair market value must be determined at least annually using a reasonable valuation method. The three-year rolling average smoothing formula used by every surveyed state satisfies this requirement, so long as the underlying single-year valuations use a reasonable methodology.
  3. The unitrust must be authorized under applicable state law. All ten surveyed states expressly authorize unitrust conversion under their §106-style statute.
  4. The state law's requirements must be met. This condition brings the safe harbor into direct alignment with state-law compliance: the notice-content requirements, the objection procedure, and the trustee's factor analysis must all be satisfied under the specific state's statute for the safe harbor to apply.

The practical consequence is significant. A trustee converting to unitrust in a non-UFIPA state can preserve the QTIP marital deduction without a per-year Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment analysis of whether the trust's income distribution meets the "all the income" standard. The safe harbor eliminates the annual reasonable-apportionment question and replaces it with a one-time procedural check at conversion. That is a substantial reduction in ongoing administrative burden and audit risk over the trust's remaining life.

The safe harbor's four conditions are conjunctive

The safe harbor requires all four conditions to be met. Missing any one condition — a rate outside 3-5 percent, a valuation methodology that fails the reasonable-valuation standard, an unauthorized conversion under state law, or a state-law procedural defect — takes the trust out of the safe harbor and back into the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment analysis. Trustees converting a QTIP trust to unitrust should confirm all four conditions in the conversion memorandum and re-confirm the state-law compliance annually as part of the trust's fiduciary income-tax return preparation.

The SECURE Act 10-year rule interaction and the year-10 acceleration

The SECURE Act 10-year rule creates a specific fact pattern that the classical unitrust mechanic was not originally designed to handle. Under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4(f), as amended by TD 10001 (Final Regulations, July 19, 2024), a non-EDB designated beneficiary of a decedent who died after the applicable required beginning date must take annual RMDs in years 1-9 of the 10-year window and must fully distribute the residual balance by December 31 of year 10.[14] The annual RMDs in years 1-9 typically run 4-8 percent of the balance; the year-10 residual can be 25-40 percent of the balance depending on market performance and the trustee's year-10 planning.

A 4 percent unitrust conversion produces an annual distribution to the income beneficiary that closely tracks the annual RMD in years 1-9. The mismatch appears in year 10. The 4 percent unitrust distribution based on the smoothed three-year average may be $80,000 while the year-10 required residual distribution from the IRA is $500,000. The trustee has three options for handling the mismatch.

First, the trustee can rely on the three-year smoothing formula to partially cushion the year-10 acceleration. The three-year average denominator for year 10's unitrust distribution includes the substantial pre-drawdown balances from years 8 and 9, which pulls the denominator up and thus pulls the numerator up. This partially closes the gap but does not close it entirely; even with maximum smoothing effect, the year-10 unitrust distribution typically covers only 40-60 percent of the year-10 residual.

Second, the trustee can retain the year-10 residual in principal at year-end 10 and pay it out in subsequent years under the ongoing unitrust regime. This approach preserves the unitrust mechanic's smoothness but delays the beneficiary's receipt of the full year-10 acceleration by several years — not a beneficiary-friendly outcome unless the beneficiary specifically prefers the smoothed stream over the acceleration.

Third, the trustee can combine the unitrust conversion with a supplemental §104 power-to-adjust adjustment in year 10 specifically, capturing the acceleration as an income distribution rather than sending the residual to principal at year-end. This is the option most inherited-IRA trustees choose because it preserves the smoothness of the unitrust mechanic in years 1-9 while addressing the year-10 acceleration cleanly. The §104 adjustment in year 10 requires the standard contemporaneous-memorandum documentation described in the August 18 piece.[15]

The §106(d) reconversion procedure

Every state unitrust conversion statute contains a §106(d)-style reconversion procedure that mirrors the initial conversion mechanic. The reconversion procedure exists because circumstances change: the initial reasons that justified the unitrust conversion may no longer apply if the trust's asset composition changes materially, if the income beneficiary's cash-flow needs change, or if the tax landscape shifts.

The reconversion procedure requires 30 to 60 days advance written notice to all current and remainder beneficiaries stating the intended reconversion, the effective date, the reasons for the reconversion, and each beneficiary's right to object. The notice period matches the state's initial conversion notice period — 30 days in Pennsylvania, 60 days in every other surveyed state. The objection procedure also mirrors the initial conversion: if any beneficiary objects, the reconversion cannot proceed without judicial approval or unanimous beneficiary consent.

Reconversion is not undoable retroactively. Distributions made during the unitrust years remain characterized under the unitrust regime, and only prospective distributions revert to the classical income-versus-principal accounting. This means a trustee who reconverts must plan for a transition period in which the trust's accounting shifts from percentage-of-value to income-versus-principal; the trust's tax return preparer must be alerted to the shift and the trust's investment strategy may need to be revisited to align with the new distribution regime.

Six practical differences distinguish the two mechanics, and the choice between them depends on which differences matter most for the specific trust's facts.

The choice framework can be reduced to three questions the trustee asks at the outset of the analysis.

First, is the trust a QTIP trust for a surviving spouse? If yes, unitrust conversion is usually the better tool because the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor eliminates the per-year Rev. Rul. 2006-26 analysis burden. If the trust is a non-QTIP trust — a discretionary trust for adult children, a bypass trust, a charitable-remainder trust — the QTIP safe harbor advantage is not available and the analysis reduces to the other five dimensions.

Second, does the trust need year-over-year flexibility? If the trust's beneficiary constellation, asset composition, or tax situation is likely to change over the drawdown window, §104 is usually the better tool because it lets the trustee recalibrate each year. If the trust is stable — a single surviving spouse income beneficiary, an inherited IRA as the dominant asset, a predictable state of affairs — unitrust conversion is usually the better tool because it eliminates the annual re-analysis burden.

Third, is beneficiary opposition to unitrust conversion likely? If a remainder beneficiary is likely to object to the notice — because the unitrust reduces the remainder beneficiary's expected share by increasing the income beneficiary's share — §104 avoids the objection cycle entirely. If beneficiary opposition is unlikely (all beneficiaries share the settlor's expressed preference for income-beneficiary primacy, or the trust involves a single dominant beneficiary), the notice cycle is a manageable one-time cost.

Worked case study: $2M inherited IRA in a New York QTIP trust

A concrete case study illustrates the mechanic. Consider a New York QTIP trust holding a $2,000,000 inherited IRA. Robert died December 15, 2025 at age 74, past his required beginning date. The IRA is titled to his revocable trust which contained a QTIP marital-deduction subtrust for his surviving spouse Elena (age 72). The QTIP trust names Elena as the income beneficiary and Robert's children from his first marriage — David (age 45) and Jennifer (age 43) — as remainder beneficiaries. The 10-year window ends December 31, 2035. Elena's other income sources are Social Security ($40,000/year), Robert's pension ($30,000/year), and a modest bank account.

Under §409 default characterization, the year-1 annual RMD of approximately $85,000 (4.25 percent of the balance under the life-expectancy table) is characterized 10 percent as income ($8,500) and 90 percent as principal ($76,500). Elena receives $8,500. That amount is patently below the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment threshold and puts the QTIP election at risk on Robert's estate.

The trustee has three primary remedies: an express drafting amendment (requires reformation and court order), a §104 power-to-adjust reallocation (per-year memorandum plus factor analysis), or a state unitrust conversion under EPTL §11-2.4 (60-day notice, 4 percent mandatory rate, three-year smoothing). Assume the trustee elects the unitrust conversion.

The unitrust conversion delivers a family after-tax total ($60,800) approximately at parity with the §104 reallocation ($64,600), but wins on three other dimensions the tax number does not capture. Rul. 2006-26 analysis burden and reduces the estate-tax audit risk on Robert's estate. Second, the per-year administration burden is materially lower — a one-time notice cycle at conversion instead of an annual nine-factor memorandum for the next ten years. Third, Elena receives an excellent predictability of cash flow — the 4 percent of net fair market value under the three-year smoothing formula produces distributions in the $75,000-$85,000 range each year with only modest year-over-year variation. Elena can plan her personal finances around a known cash flow rather than waiting each year to see what the trustee's §104 analysis produces.

The unitrust conversion's principal disadvantage — the year-10 acceleration mismatch — appears in this fact pattern as an estimated $400,000-$500,000 residual balance at year-end 2035 that the smoothed 4 percent formula would distribute over the following several years rather than in year 2035 itself. The trustee's typical response is to plan for a supplemental §104 power-to-adjust adjustment in year 2035 specifically, capturing the acceleration as a year-2035 income distribution rather than deferring it. That supplemental §104 adjustment in year 10 requires the standard nine-factor contemporaneous memorandum, but it is a single-year exercise rather than a ten-year exercise — the administrative-burden savings from the unitrust conversion in years 1-9 remain intact.

Six recurring state unitrust conversion mistakes

The fiduciary-litigation record surfaces six recurring mistakes on state unitrust conversion for inherited-IRA trusts. Each is avoidable with straightforward attention at conversion time.

Mistake 1 — converting without checking the trust instrument. The state statute applies except where the trust instrument expressly prohibits unitrust conversion or expressly authorizes only a different distribution regime. A trustee who converts without checking the trust instrument may find, after the fact, that the trust contained a prohibition clause the trustee overlooked. The check is a five-minute review at conversion time and eliminates a category of error entirely.

Mistake 2 — inadequate notice content. The notice must state the trustee's reasons for the conversion tied to the trust's specific facts, not merely recite the state's percentage rule. A notice that reads as boilerplate materially raises the risk of a beneficiary objection and provides a weak record on judicial review if an objection proceeds to court. The notice-drafting effort is proportionate to the trust's size; a notice on a $2M trust deserves the same specificity as a notice on a $20M trust.

Mistake 3 — converting outside the 3-5 percent safe harbor in a QTIP trust. Every one of the ten surveyed states falls within the 3-5 percent range at its default rate. But a Texas trustee choosing 6 percent under the Texas discretionary range would fall outside the safe harbor and risk QTIP disqualification on the deceased spouse's estate. The trustee's rate selection in a QTIP trust must stay within 3-5 percent regardless of what the state statute otherwise permits.

Mistake 4 — no year-10 acceleration plan. The year-10 residual acceleration exceeds what the smoothed unitrust formula can distribute even with maximum smoothing effect. A trustee who converts without a year-10 plan will find, in year 10, that a substantial residual sits at year-end 10 with no distribution mechanism. The plan is straightforward — a supplemental §104 power-to-adjust adjustment in year 10 specifically — but it must be built into the conversion memorandum at conversion time so the trustee remembers to execute it in year 10.

Mistake 5 — inadequate valuation methodology. The safe harbor requires a reasonable annual valuation methodology. Most inherited-IRA trustees use custodian statements for valuation, which is generally acceptable, but the trustee should confirm the custodian's valuation methodology satisfies the reasonable-valuation standard — particularly for any IRA assets other than publicly-traded securities (private-fund interests, closely-held business interests, real estate, and so on).

Mistake 6 — converting a grantor trust. A grantor trust for federal income tax purposes has all income and gain taxed to the grantor regardless of the trust's accounting regime. Converting a grantor trust to unitrust has no federal-tax effect during the grantor period; the conversion is a procedural exercise that produces no tax benefit for the trust. Most inherited-IRA trusts are non-grantor because the account owner died, terminating any grantor-trust status; but a trust that was a revocable trust during the account owner's lifetime and continued as a grantor trust for the surviving spouse under a §678 rule would still be a grantor trust and would not benefit from conversion until the grantor's death.

Pre-conversion action checklist

The 8-item pre-conversion checklist for a trust-held inherited IRA

Complete each item before delivering the conversion notice. Retain the completed checklist in the trust's records for the statute of limitations plus a reasonable margin.

  1. Read the trust instrument. Check for an express prohibition on unitrust conversion or an express authorization of a specific different distribution regime. If either exists, the state statute is displaced and the conversion cannot proceed under the statute.
  2. Confirm the state statute and the specific percentage rule. Identify the trust's state of situs, pull the state's §106-style statute, and confirm the specific percentage (fixed 4 percent, 3-5 percent range, or another state-specific rate). Confirm the notice period (30 days in Pennsylvania; 60 days elsewhere).
  3. Analyze the QTIP marital-deduction implications. If the trust is a QTIP trust, confirm the intended unitrust rate falls within the 3-5 percent Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor. If the trust is not a QTIP trust, this step is skipped.
  4. Model the year-10 acceleration. Project the year-10 residual IRA balance under reasonable investment assumptions. Confirm the smoothed unitrust distribution formula does not distribute the full year-10 residual and identify the mechanic — supplemental §104 adjustment, deferred distribution, or another approach — for handling the residual at year-end 10.
  5. Prepare a trust-specific conversion memorandum. Document the specific reasons for the conversion tied to the trust's facts: the trust's income beneficiary's cash-flow needs, the trust's asset composition, the QTIP marital-deduction preservation, the SECURE Act 10-year rule timeline, and the trustee's rationale for the specific rate. The memorandum becomes the trustee's record on judicial review if a beneficiary objects.
  6. Identify all current and remainder beneficiaries. Include contingent remainder beneficiaries whose interests are not yet vested. For a trust with a class of "then-living descendants per stirpes" remainder beneficiaries, identify every currently-living descendant with reasonable diligence.
  7. Draft and deliver compliant notice. Prepare notice with the state-specific required content and deliver it to every current and remainder beneficiary at least 30-60 days (per state rule) before the intended effective date. Retain proof of delivery for each beneficiary.
  8. Wait out the objection period. If no objection is received, proceed with the conversion on the effective date. If an objection is received, either (i) obtain judicial approval, (ii) obtain unanimous beneficiary consent, or (iii) withdraw the conversion notice and pursue an alternative remedy such as a §104 power-to-adjust reallocation.

Frequently asked questions about state unitrust conversion

What is state unitrust conversion and why does it matter for a trust holding an inherited IRA?

State unitrust conversion is a statutory procedure — modeled on 1997 UPIA §106 and adopted with state-specific variations in the 42 non-UFIPA jurisdictions — that permanently converts a trust from an income-only accounting regime into a percentage-of-value distribution regime. Once converted, the trust distributes to the income beneficiary a fixed percentage (typically 3-5 percent, most commonly 4 percent) of the trust's net fair market value each year. For a trust holding an inherited IRA subject to the SECURE Act 10-year rule, unitrust conversion is the primary state-law tool for delivering predictable annual cash flow to the income beneficiary while satisfying the impartiality standard. It is the permanent-conversion alternative to a per-year §104 power-to-adjust reallocation, and its 3-5 percent range aligns with the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment threshold that must be satisfied for a QTIP trust to preserve the IRC §2056(b)(7) marital deduction.

What percentage does each state use for its unitrust conversion default?

The state variation is meaningful. New York EPTL §11-2.4 fixes 4 percent mandatory with no trustee discretion. Illinois 760 ILCS 3/106 uses 4 percent default with trustee discretion. Texas Property Code §116.006 provides a 3-5 percent discretionary range. Pennsylvania 20 Pa.C.S. §8106 uses 4 percent default with trustee discretion in a reasonable range. Ohio Rev. Code §5812.05 uses 4 percent default with trustee discretion in the 3-5 percent range. Massachusetts G.L. c. 203D §5, Michigan MCL 555.506, New Jersey N.J.S.A. 3B:19B-6, Georgia O.C.G.A. §53-12-346, and North Carolina §37A-1-106 all use 4 percent defaults with no or minimal trustee discretion. Trusts with situs-selection flexibility should compare state rules before locking situs.

How does unitrust conversion interact with the SECURE Act 10-year rule?

The 10-year rule compresses the inherited IRA's drawdown into a 10-year window. Annual RMDs typically run 4-8 percent of the balance in years 1-9, with the full residual balance in year 10. A 4 percent unitrust distribution tracks the annual RMD closely in years 1-9 and materially undershoots the year-10 acceleration. The trustee has three options for year 10: rely on the three-year smoothing formula to partially cushion the acceleration (partial fix only), retain the residual in principal for post-year-10 distribution under the ongoing unitrust regime (delays beneficiary receipt), or combine the unitrust conversion with a supplemental §104 power-to-adjust adjustment in year 10 specifically (the most common choice). The year-10 plan should be documented at conversion time.

What is the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) QTIP safe harbor?

The regulation establishes that a state-law unitrust conversion satisfying four conditions qualifies as producing a stream of "income" for QTIP marital deduction purposes under IRC §2056(b)(7), notwithstanding that a percentage-of-value distribution is not "income" in the classical trust-accounting sense. The four conditions: (1) the unitrust rate must fall between 3 percent and 5 percent; (2) the fair market value must be determined at least annually using a reasonable valuation method; (3) the unitrust must be authorized under applicable state law; and (4) the state law's requirements must be met. All ten surveyed non-UFIPA state statutes fall within the safe harbor at default rates. The practical consequence is that a trustee converting to unitrust in a non-UFIPA state can preserve the QTIP marital deduction without a per-year Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment analysis.

Does the trustee need to give beneficiaries notice before converting?

Yes in every non-UFIPA state that adopted the §106-style mechanic. The notice period is 60 days in most states and 30 days in Pennsylvania. The notice-content requirements are statutory: the notice must identify the trustee, identify the trust, state the effective date, state the specific percentage, state the valuation methodology, state the trustee's reasons for the conversion tied to trust-specific facts, and state each beneficiary's right to object. The notice must go to all current beneficiaries and all remainder beneficiaries, including contingent remainder beneficiaries. A conversion without compliant notice is voidable at a beneficiary's option.

Can a trustee reconvert the trust back to income-only after unitrust conversion?

Yes. Every state unitrust conversion statute contains a §106(d)-style reconversion procedure that mirrors the initial conversion mechanic — 30 to 60 days advance notice to all current and remainder beneficiaries with a right to object. The reconversion is prospective only; distributions made during the unitrust years remain characterized under the unitrust regime. Reconversion should be documented with the same contemporaneous-memorandum rigor as the initial conversion. Common reconversion triggers include a change in the trust's asset composition, a change in the income beneficiary's cash-flow needs, or a change in the tax landscape that alters the trust's optimal accounting regime.

How does the 3-year smoothed valuation formula work?

Most state statutes apply the fixed percentage to a smoothed multi-year average — typically the average of the trust's net fair market values on the first business day of each of the three preceding calendar years. New York EPTL §11-2.4(b)(2) uses the three-year average. Texas Property Code §116.006(f) uses a three-year average. The smoothing dampens year-to-year swings in market value that would otherwise translate into cash-flow volatility. For an inherited-IRA trust drawing down under the SECURE Act 10-year rule, the smoothing partially cushions the year-10 acceleration by including pre-drawdown balances in the year-10 denominator, though the acceleration is large enough that smoothing alone typically leaves substantial residual principal at year-end 10.

What happens if the trust instrument prohibits unitrust conversion?

If the trust instrument expressly prohibits unitrust conversion or unitrust distributions, the trustee cannot convert regardless of state law. Most 1997 UPIA §106-style statutes contain an override provision stating that the statute applies except to the extent the trust instrument expressly provides otherwise. A settlor who wanted to preserve classical accounting can do so with a single sentence. Trusts drafted before the state statute existed (pre-2001 for New York, pre-2003 for most other states) generally do not contain such a prohibition; trusts drafted after should be checked. If the trust instrument is silent, the state statute applies and the trustee may convert following the statutory procedure.

How does unitrust conversion compare with §104 power-to-adjust?

Six differences: (1) permanence — unitrust is permanent, §104 is per-year; (2) predictability — unitrust delivers a known percentage of value, §104 delivers a variable dollar amount; (3) notice — unitrust requires 30-60 days advance notice, §104 requires none; (4) annual documentation — unitrust requires no per-year factor memo, §104 requires a nine-factor memorandum every year; (5) QTIP — unitrust falls within the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor, §104 requires Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment analysis; (6) beneficiary objection — unitrust conversion can be blocked by beneficiary objection during the notice period, §104 cannot. The choice depends on the trust's specific facts.

What are the most common unitrust conversion mistakes on inherited-IRA trusts?

Six recurring mistakes: (1) converting without checking the trust instrument for an express prohibition; (2) inadequate notice content that recites the percentage rule without stating trust-specific reasons; (3) converting in a QTIP trust to a rate outside the 3-5 percent safe harbor; (4) no plan for the year-10 acceleration mismatch; (5) inadequate valuation methodology that fails the reasonable-valuation safe-harbor requirement; (6) converting a grantor trust where the conversion has no federal-tax effect during the grantor period.
