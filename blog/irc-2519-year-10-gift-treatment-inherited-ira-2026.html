IRC §2519 Year-10 Gift-Treatment Mechanics for QTIP-Held Inherited IRAs in 2026: The Federal-Tax Trap That Closes the QTIP Lifecycle

The August 20 field guide walked the IRC §2056(b)(7) qualifying-income-interest test that lets a QTIP election defer estate tax on an inherited IRA payable to a marital trust. That was the front end of the QTIP lifecycle. This piece is the back end. IRC §2519 treats any disposition by the surviving spouse of her qualifying-income-interest — even a small partial disposition, even a consent, even a failed disclaimer — as a constructive transfer of the ENTIRE underlying property. Post-SECURE, the year-10 residual of a QTIP-held inherited IRA is the fact pattern where §2519 most often springs unnoticed, and the tax exposure regularly runs into seven figures. Here is the full statutory mechanic, the Rev. Rul. 98-8 doctrine, PLR 200030030 and the ownership-line authority, the IRC §2207A(b) reimbursement right that partially cures the tax, the IRC §2044 coordination that prevents double taxation, and a worked $5M inherited-IRA case study.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 21, 2026 27 min read How we research

Every practitioner who drafts, administers, or advises on a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA needs to understand two federal-tax provisions with equal fluency. The first is IRC §2056(b)(7), which lets the executor defer estate tax by electing marital-deduction treatment for property passing to a trust in which the surviving spouse holds a qualifying income interest for life.[1] The second is IRC §2519, which converts any disposition by that surviving spouse of her qualifying income interest into a constructive transfer of the entire underlying property.[2] Both provisions are load-bearing. The first without the second reads as an unlimited deferral vehicle; the second without the first reads as an obscure gift-tax curiosity. Together, they define the actual lifecycle of every QTIP trust: a QTIP election at the deceased spouse's death defers tax on the marital transfer, and the tax comes back — either as an inclusion under IRC §2044 at the surviving spouse's death, or as a constructive gift under IRC §2519 if the surviving spouse disposes of any part of her income interest during her lifetime.[3]

This piece walks the second provision end to end. It reads best as the sixth in the state-law-plus-federal-tax toolkit arc that closes the retirement-inheritance sub-cluster's practitioner cycle: 2026-08-17 non-UFIPA §409 field guide, 2026-08-18 §104 power to adjust, 2026-08-19 §106 unitrust conversion, 2026-08-20 IRC §2056(b)(7) QTIP election, and today's IRC §2519 gift-treatment mechanics. A practitioner who understands all five pieces can walk the full trust-held-inherited-IRA QTIP mechanic from the deceased spouse's Form 706 preparation through the year-10 SECURE Act acceleration and on to the surviving spouse's own death, without hitting either a §2056(b)(7) qualification defect at the front or a §2519 gift-tax trap at the back.[4]

The IRC §2519 statutory framework

IRC §2519(a) reads in full: "For purposes of this chapter and chapter 12, any disposition of all or part of a qualifying income interest for life in any property to which this section applies shall be treated as a transfer of such property other than the qualifying income interest."[2] Section 2519(b) defines the trigger property as any property in which the transferor has a qualifying income interest for life, and cross-references IRC §§2044 and 2056(b)(7) for the definition of the qualifying interest. The statutory rule is short — two subsections — but its practical effect is very large. A "disposition" under §2519 is not limited to a sale or gift in the ordinary sense; it includes any commutation, court-approved settlement, exchange, redemption, release, transfer, assignment, sale, or other alienation of any part of the qualifying income interest. Rev. Rul. 98-8 and the Treasury Regulations at §25.2519-1 walk the breadth of the term in detail.[5]

The mechanic operates in two parallel steps. First, §2519 treats the disposition of the income interest as a deemed transfer of the underlying property (net of the value that would be included in the surviving spouse's gross estate under §2044 if she had died on the day of the disposition — mechanically, this net-out equals the actuarial value of the retained income interest, if any). Second, IRC §2511 separately treats the actual disposition of the income interest itself as an outright gift of the income-interest value. The two components together sum to the entire fair market value of the QTIP property, so a full disposition of the income interest triggers a constructive transfer under §2519 plus §2511 equal to 100 percent of the trust corpus.[6]

Why §2519 exists

Congress enacted §2519 in the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 as part of the same statutory package that created the QTIP marital deduction under §2056(b)(7). The two provisions were designed to work together as a coordinated deferral-and-eventual-tax structure. Congress feared that without §2519, a surviving spouse could receive the marital deduction at her spouse's death and then defeat the eventual tax by disposing of her income interest during her lifetime — leaving the QTIP property in the hands of the remainder beneficiaries without any federal transfer tax having been paid. Section 2519 closes that loop: any lifetime disposition triggers a gift-tax event at the full value of the underlying property.[7] The legislative history to §2056(b)(7) makes the coordination explicit: the QTIP deferral is available "only if the surviving spouse's interest is such that the property will be subject to federal transfer tax at her death or upon disposition of her interest." H.R. Rep. No. 97-201, at 160 (1981); S. Rep. No. 97-144, at 128 (1981).

Why §2519 is dangerous in QTIP practice

Section 2519 is dangerous for three structural reasons that no practitioner unfamiliar with the mechanic would intuit. First, the constructive-transfer measure is the full fair market value of the underlying property, not the amount of the actual disposition. A surviving spouse who consents to a $100,000 principal distribution to a remainder beneficiary while retaining her income interest in the balance can trigger a §2519 transfer of many multiples of $100,000 — the entire portion of the underlying property associated with the disposed-of income interest. Rev. Rul. 98-8 walks this partial-disposition mechanic in detail.[5]

Second, the "disposition" concept is far broader than practitioners typically assume. A failed disclaimer under IRC §2518 is a §2519 disposition. A court-approved commutation is a §2519 disposition. A sale of the income interest to a remainder beneficiary is a §2519 disposition. A gift of the income interest to a charity is a §2519 disposition (though it may qualify for a charitable deduction under §2522). Even a settlement of a will contest that alters the surviving spouse's income entitlement can be a §2519 disposition. The breadth of the concept turns any ambiguity in the surviving spouse's action into a §2519 risk.[5]

Third, the trap disproportionately affects sophisticated planning fact patterns — blended-family situations, second-marriage QTIPs, situations where the surviving spouse and remainder beneficiaries are in overtly friendly relationships and see themselves as coordinating a shared plan rather than as parties on opposite sides of a fiduciary boundary. In each of these situations, a well-intentioned surviving spouse acting collaboratively with the remainder beneficiaries can trigger §2519 without any adversarial precipitating event and without warning from a general-purpose estate-planning attorney who is not steeped in the QTIP mechanic.[3]

Rev. Rul. 98-8 and the constructive-gift doctrine

Rev. Rul. 98-8, 1998-1 C.B. 541, is the controlling revenue ruling on the practical breadth of §2519 in QTIP practice. The ruling walked three fact patterns, each involving a court-supervised commutation of a QTIP trust for the benefit of both the surviving spouse and the remainder beneficiaries. In each fact pattern, the surviving spouse's income interest was actuarially valued under IRC §7520 tables, the remainder interest was actuarially valued, and the trust corpus was divided between the surviving spouse and the remainder beneficiaries in proportion to those actuarial values.[8]

The IRS held that in each fact pattern, the commutation triggered two separate federal gift-tax events. First, the surviving spouse made a §2519 constructive transfer of the entire remainder value (the underlying property less her income-interest actuarial value). Second, the surviving spouse made a §2511 gift of her actuarial income-interest value to the third-party recipients (whoever received the portion of the corpus attributable to the income interest — typically herself, but even a receipt of her own income-interest actuarial value can trigger a §2511 gift if the mechanic of the commutation causes her to give up her income interest before receiving her actuarial share).

The two gift-tax events together sum to the full fair market value of the trust corpus at the moment of commutation. A $5 million QTIP trust commuted between a 72-year-old surviving spouse and her adult children, with the surviving spouse's income interest valued actuarially at (say) $1.7 million and the remainder valued at $3.3 million, produced a §2519 constructive transfer of $3.3 million (the remainder value) plus a §2511 transfer of $1.7 million (the income-interest value) — a full $5 million of taxable transfers.[8]

The commutation trap in QTIP practice

Practitioners who counsel a surviving spouse considering commutation of a QTIP trust — for asset-consolidation reasons, to eliminate trustee-administration expenses, or to accelerate the remainder beneficiaries' access — must recognize that Rev. Rul. 98-8 makes any such commutation a full-value gift-tax event. The commutation itself typically causes NO income-tax event and NO estate-tax event, so it presents as tax-neutral to the untrained eye. But it triggers a gift-tax event equal to the entire trust corpus, and the 40 percent gift-tax rate above the surviving spouse's remaining $15M lifetime exemption can produce out-of-pocket gift taxes exceeding the value of any administrative savings from the commutation. Every QTIP commutation requires §2519 analysis before the surviving spouse signs the commutation document.

PLR 200030030 and the ownership-line authority

Private Letter Ruling 200030030 (April 27, 2000) is the leading downstream authority on the §2519 constructive-gift mechanic in the specific context of a QTIP trust holding a retirement account.[9] The PLR involved a surviving spouse who wanted to sever the QTIP trust into two separate trusts — a marital trust holding the IRA and a second trust holding non-IRA assets — and to have the two trusts hold beneficial ownership as tenants-in-common of certain remaining assets. The IRS held that the severance itself would not trigger §2519 provided (i) the surviving spouse's aggregate qualifying-income-interest was preserved across the two trusts, (ii) the severance was performed under a state-law severance statute that permitted the transaction without altering the surviving spouse's income entitlement, and (iii) no portion of the beneficial ownership passed from the surviving spouse to any other party in the severance transaction.

The PLR's importance is not in the specific holding — which was favorable to the taxpayer — but in the analytical framework it establishes. The IRS distinguished a §2519-triggering "disposition" from a mere §2519-neutral "restructuring" of the qualifying-income-interest arrangement on three tests: (a) the aggregate income entitlement must be preserved without material change; (b) the transaction must operate under state law as a severance rather than a distribution; and (c) no beneficial ownership may pass from the surviving spouse to any other party in the transaction. Any restructuring failing all three tests is a §2519 disposition triggering the constructive-gift mechanic. A restructuring passing all three tests is not.

Downstream PLRs and the ownership-line taxonomy

Subsequent private letter rulings — including PLRs 200234017, 200522004, and 200832010 — have applied the PLR 200030030 framework to various QTIP-restructuring fact patterns. The pattern that emerges is a two-category taxonomy. Category A transactions preserve the surviving spouse's income entitlement without diminishment and do not shift beneficial ownership — these do not trigger §2519. Category B transactions either alter the surviving spouse's income entitlement (in amount, timing, or scope) OR shift beneficial ownership to another party. These do trigger §2519, with the constructive transfer measured at the full underlying-property value.

Note that PLRs are not precedent under IRC §6110(k)(3) and cannot be cited or used by taxpayers other than the requesting party. But the PLR 200030030 framework is the accepted analytical starting point in practitioner guidance and appears in ACTEC materials, the Bloomberg BNA Estate Planning portfolios, and the leading Warren Gorham & Lamont treatises on federal estate and gift taxation. A prudent practitioner starts a §2519 analysis by identifying which of the two categories the contemplated transaction falls into.[10]

The two-step §2519 computation

Applied to any real fact pattern, the §2519 computation proceeds in two mechanical steps that a practitioner should walk on paper before executing any transaction involving the surviving spouse's income interest.

Step 1 — measure the full fair market value of the QTIP property immediately before the disposition. For a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA, this is the sum of (i) the IRA balance at the date of disposition, valued at the custodian's date-of-disposition statement, and (ii) any other assets held in the trust (marketable securities, real estate, cash equivalents), each valued at date-of-disposition fair market value. The valuation date is the date of the §2519 disposition, not the date of the deceased spouse's death and not the date of the original QTIP election.

Step 2 — subtract the value of the surviving spouse's qualifying income interest. The income-interest value is measured actuarially under IRC §7520 tables at the §7520 rate for the month of the disposition (or one of the two prior months if the taxpayer elects under §7520(a) — this election is a §7520 election, not a §2519 election, and the taxpayer must consistently apply the elected rate). The actuarial computation uses the surviving spouse's age at the date of disposition, the §7520 rate for the applicable month, and the standard Table S life-estate factors published by the IRS in Publication 1457 (as updated for each new §7520 mortality table publication). Beginning January 1, 2021, the applicable mortality table is the Table 2010CM published in Treas. Reg. §20.2031-7.[11]

§2519 constructive transfer = FMVQTIP property at disposition − Income-interest actuarial value

The result of Step 2 is the §2519 constructive transfer. This amount is a lifetime taxable gift reportable on the surviving spouse's Form 709 for the calendar year of the disposition. The gift is a taxable gift, meaning it uses lifetime exemption first (the $15 million OBBBA exemption for 2026, reduced by any prior taxable gifts) and only the excess is subject to the 40 percent gift-tax rate under IRC §2502.[12]

In parallel, IRC §2511 applies to the surviving spouse's actual disposition of her income interest. If she disposed of the income interest to a third party, the disposition is a separate §2511 gift equal to the actuarial income-interest value. If she disposed of the income interest to herself (which is the operative pattern in a full or partial commutation), no §2511 gift arises with respect to the income-interest value — but the §2519 constructive transfer still stands with respect to the remainder value.

The IRC §2207A reimbursement right and the tax-cost cure

IRC §2207A(b) grants the surviving spouse a statutory right of reimbursement from the recipients of the constructively-transferred QTIP property, equal to the incremental federal gift tax attributable to the §2519 constructive transfer.[13] The reimbursement mechanic works as follows.

The surviving spouse first pays the federal gift tax attributable to the §2519 constructive transfer out of her own resources — the tax is a personal gift-tax liability under IRC §2502(c). She then asserts the §2207A(b) reimbursement right against the recipients of the QTIP property (the remainder beneficiaries or the QTIP property itself). The reimbursement amount is equal to the incremental federal gift tax that arose because of the §2519 transfer (essentially, the total gift tax due minus the gift tax that would have been due if the §2519 transfer had not been made).

For federal gift-tax purposes, the reimbursement is treated as if the reimbursed amount had never been transferred by the surviving spouse. The mechanic essentially unwinds the portion of the gift equal to the reimbursement, leaving the surviving spouse with no net gift-tax cost with respect to the reimbursed portion. IRC §2207A(b) coordinates with IRC §2036(a) to prevent the reimbursement mechanic from being treated as a retained interest in the transferred property for estate-tax purposes.

The §2207A(b) reimbursement is not automatic

The surviving spouse must affirmatively assert the reimbursement right, in writing, within the state-law limitation period (which varies by state — typically the statute of limitations on trust distributions, in the range of 2 to 6 years). Failure to assert the reimbursement right within the limitation period is itself a §2511 gift equal to the un-recovered reimbursement amount. Every QTIP commutation or disposition that triggers §2519 requires a contemporaneous §2207A(b) assertion, in writing, delivered to the remainder beneficiaries or the trustee, before the limitation period expires. The safe practice is to include the §2207A(b) assertion in the commutation or disposition instrument itself, so the assertion is contemporaneous with the disposition and cannot be later characterized as a delayed election that resulted in a separate gift.

Practical effect of §2207A(b) on the §2519 tax cost

The §2207A(b) mechanic is often described as "eliminating" the §2519 gift-tax cost. This is not quite accurate. The mechanic eliminates the surviving spouse's out-of-pocket gift-tax expense with respect to the reimbursed portion, and it eliminates the corresponding reduction in her lifetime exemption. But it does not eliminate all of the §2519 tax consequences: the §2519 transfer still uses the surviving spouse's lifetime exemption to the extent the exemption absorbs the transfer without triggering the 40 percent rate. And it does not eliminate the transaction-cost overhead of preparing Form 709, computing the actuarial income-interest value, tracking the §2207A(b) reimbursement assertion, and coordinating the reimbursement's actual payment. In sophisticated practice, the §2207A(b) mechanic is best understood as a partial cure that reduces but does not eliminate the §2519 tax cost.[13]

IRC §2044 coordination and the anti-double-tax rule

IRC §2044(a) includes in the surviving spouse's gross estate at her death any property in which she held the qualifying income interest at her death, provided a QTIP election was made with respect to the property at the deceased spouse's death.[14] The §2044 inclusion is the normal end-of-lifecycle mechanic — the QTIP property comes back into the transfer-tax base at the surviving spouse's death, having been deferred at the deceased spouse's death by the QTIP election.

But if the surviving spouse disposed of her income interest during her lifetime under §2519, the §2519 disposition is a lifetime constructive transfer at the time of disposition. The §2044 inclusion does not then apply to the disposed-of property at her later death, because at her death she no longer holds the qualifying income interest in the disposed-of property. Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(a) and §20.2044-1 make this coordination explicit: "Any property with respect to which a section 2519 transfer has been made shall not thereafter be included in the transferor's gross estate under section 2044."[15]

The result is a coordinated one-tax-event rule: the QTIP property is subject to a federal transfer tax exactly once — either the §2519 gift tax during the surviving spouse's lifetime, or the §2044 estate-tax inclusion at her death, but not both. Practitioners occasionally worry about a §2519-plus-§2044 double-inclusion scenario. The Treasury Regulations foreclose it. The rare-but-possible edge case is a partial §2519 disposition where the surviving spouse retains the income interest in a portion of the QTIP property. In that case, the disposed-of portion is subject to §2519 during her lifetime, and the retained portion is subject to §2044 at her death. The two portions are independently tracked.

The §2519-versus-§2044 tax-planning election

The one-tax-event rule creates a practical tax-planning question that few surviving spouses consider explicitly but that every sophisticated practitioner should raise. Given a choice between paying §2519 gift tax now (at the disposition) or letting the QTIP property come back under §2044 at the surviving spouse's death, which produces the lower total tax? The answer depends on four variables: (i) the surviving spouse's own estate at her death, (ii) the state estate-tax rules that apply at her death (some states tax QTIP-includable property, others do not), (iii) the time-value of money between the §2519 date and the §2044 date (paying tax now versus later at the same rate favors later), and (iv) whether the §2207A(b) reimbursement right is enforceable and whether the reimbursement source is available. In most fact patterns, deferring to §2044 is the tax-neutral or tax-favored answer, because the surviving spouse's own estate is unlikely to exceed the $15M OBBBA exemption in her lifetime absent unusual growth. But blended-family and second-marriage fact patterns where the surviving spouse's own estate is projected to exceed the exemption can flip the analysis and favor accelerating the §2519 disposition to trigger the reimbursement mechanic and shift the tax cost to the remainder beneficiaries.

The SECURE Act 10-year rule and the year-10 §2519 trap

The IRC §401(a)(9)(H) 10-year rule, enacted in the SECURE Act (Pub. L. 116-94, December 20, 2019) and implemented in Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-3 as amended by TD 10001 (July 19, 2024), forces a non-eligible-designated-beneficiary of an IRA to fully distribute the inherited account within ten years of the account owner's death.[16] For a QTIP trust that is a see-through trust holding an inherited IRA, the 10-year rule applies to the trust in the same way it would apply to an individual non-EDB beneficiary — the entire IRA must be distributed to the trust within ten years, and the trust then distributes to the surviving spouse and the remainder beneficiaries according to the trust's own terms and the state-law characterization rules.

Pre-SECURE, the QTIP-held IRA distributed over the surviving spouse's remaining life expectancy — typically 20 to 35 years for a spouse in her 60s or 70s at the deceased spouse's death. The year-by-year distribution was small (roughly 4 to 6 percent of the balance per year for a 65-to-75-year-old spouse), and the residual after the surviving spouse's death was often 40 to 60 percent of the original balance — flowing to the remainder beneficiaries under IRC §2044 as an estate-tax inclusion event at the surviving spouse's death. The §2519 mechanic rarely came into play because the surviving spouse rarely had occasion to consent to a large principal distribution during her lifetime.

Post-SECURE, the year-by-year distribution is much larger (10 to 15 percent of the initial balance under the "greater of" rule from Rev. Rul. 2000-2), and the year-10 residual — after nine years of forced annual distributions plus the year-10 acceleration — is often 25 to 40 percent of the initial balance for a normally-invested IRA. On a $3M IRA compounding at 5 percent with steady annual distributions, the year-10 residual is roughly $1.5M to $2M. On a $10M IRA under the same assumptions, the year-10 residual is roughly $5M to $7M. That residual, distributed all at once in year 10, presents the surviving spouse with a specific question she likely has not faced before: does she keep the residual (as trust income under the state-law characterization rules) or consent to letting the residual flow directly to the remainder beneficiaries as principal?[17]

The year-10 QTIP-with-IRA fact pattern that triggers §2519

The surviving spouse consents in writing to a year-10 principal distribution from the QTIP trust to the remainder beneficiaries (typically the deceased spouse's children from a first marriage). She sees the consent as a coordinated family decision — she has enough for her own needs, and the residual belongs by intent to her deceased husband's remainder beneficiaries. The consent is well-intentioned. But the consent is a §2519 disposition of her qualifying-income-interest with respect to the residual. Under §2519(a), she is treated as having made a constructive transfer of the entire residual value (net of any actuarial income-interest value she retains in any remaining trust property). At $1.5M–$5M residual values, the constructive transfer consumes lifetime exemption dollar-for-dollar and, above the exemption, is taxed at 40 percent. On a $5M residual, if the surviving spouse has already used $12M of her $15M lifetime exemption on prior gifts, the excess $2M is taxed at 40 percent — a $800,000 out-of-pocket gift-tax cost that neither the surviving spouse nor the remainder beneficiaries expected.

Six recurring §2519 mistakes in QTIP-with-inherited-IRA practice

The following mistakes account for the vast majority of §2519 fact patterns that reach practitioner attention post-disposition. Each is preventable with pre-disposition analysis.

Mistake 1 — surviving-spouse consent to a year-10 principal distribution without recognizing the §2519 consequence. This is the modal post-SECURE mistake. A surviving spouse in her 70s, having already received nine years of QTIP-trust income distributions, agrees to let the year-10 residual flow directly to her deceased husband's children as remainder beneficiaries. The consent is documented in a trust distribution instrument prepared by the trustee's counsel. Nothing in the instrument warns the surviving spouse that her consent triggers §2519. The gift-tax consequence surfaces months or years later when a CPA prepares her Form 709. Prevention: every year-10 principal-distribution consent from a QTIP surviving spouse must be preceded by a §2519 analysis and, if a §2519 transfer would result, either a §2207A(b) reimbursement mechanic in the instrument or an alternative structure that avoids the disposition.

Mistake 2 — court-supervised commutation of the QTIP trust for administrative convenience. Rev. Rul. 98-8 is directly on point. A commutation, whether court-supervised or private, is a §2519 disposition. Even when the commutation is structured to preserve the surviving spouse's actuarial share, the §2519 constructive transfer of the entire remainder value stands. Prevention: commutations of QTIP trusts require §2519 analysis before the surviving spouse signs any commutation document, and generally require §2207A(b) reimbursement mechanics built into the commutation itself.

Mistake 3 — failing to assert the IRC §2207A(b) reimbursement right within the state-law limitation period. The §2207A(b) reimbursement is not automatic; the surviving spouse must affirmatively assert the right in writing, delivered to the remainder beneficiaries or trustee, within the state-law limitation period. Failure to assert is itself a §2511 gift of the un-recovered reimbursement amount. Prevention: build the §2207A(b) assertion into the disposition instrument itself, so the assertion is contemporaneous with the disposition and cannot be later characterized as a delayed election that produced a separate gift.

Mistake 4 — partial disposition without measuring the associated underlying-property transfer. Rev. Rul. 98-8 and Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(c) confirm that a partial disposition of the income interest triggers a constructive transfer of the entire underlying property associated with the disposed-of income interest — not proportional to the actual cash disposition. A surviving spouse who consents to a $50,000 principal distribution can trigger a §2519 transfer of $500,000 or more, depending on the actuarial values. Prevention: any partial disposition requires a Step-1-plus-Step-2 §2519 computation before execution, and the surviving spouse should sign only after seeing the constructive-transfer number.

Mistake 5 — failed disclaimer under IRC §2518. If the surviving spouse purports to disclaim her qualifying-income-interest but the disclaimer fails IRC §2518's requirements (nine-month time limit, no acceptance of benefits, unqualified refusal, disclaimed interest passes without direction), the failed disclaimer is a §2519 disposition. The surviving spouse becomes liable for gift tax on the entire underlying property that was the subject of the failed disclaimer. Prevention: every QTIP-related disclaimer must satisfy §2518's four-part test, and prudent practice includes obtaining a private letter ruling before executing any disclaimer of a significant QTIP interest — the letter-ruling fee is trivial next to the §2519 exposure.

Mistake 6 — IRA in-kind distribution redirected to a remainder beneficiary through the surviving spouse's account. A subtle variant: the trustee distributes an in-kind IRA balance to the surviving spouse (as trust income under state characterization rules), and the surviving spouse then transfers the received in-kind assets to the remainder beneficiary — either as an outright gift or as an inheritance-planning move to accelerate the remainder beneficiary's economic receipt. This variant escapes §2519 if the surviving spouse actually receives and then re-gifts the assets (in which case §2511 applies to the re-gift at fair market value). But if the trustee's distribution is a coordinated redirection at the surviving spouse's direction, the IRS may recharacterize the redirection as a §2519 disposition of the income interest, triggering the full constructive-transfer mechanic. Prevention: separate the trust-distribution step from any re-gift step in time and in documentation. The surviving spouse must actually receive the trust distribution as income and hold it in her own name for a meaningful period before executing any subsequent gift.[18]

Worked case study: $5 million inherited IRA in a QTIP trust

Consider Robert and Elena, a blended-family second-marriage couple. Robert (age 78) dies in June 2026, survived by his second wife Elena (age 72) and by two adult children from his first marriage, David (age 48) and Jennifer (age 45). Robert's largest asset is a $5,000,000 IRA, which he had designated payable to a QTIP marital trust for Elena's benefit during her life, with the remainder passing to David and Jennifer in equal shares at Elena's death. Robert's Form 706 makes a full QTIP election with respect to both the IRA and the QTIP trust (following Rev. Rul. 2000-2 format).[17]

The trust's state situs is New York, and the trust instrument authorizes the trustee to invoke EPTL §11-2.4's 4-percent unitrust conversion. The trustee has done so effective January 1, 2027 (the year following Robert's death). Under the 4-percent unitrust, the surviving spouse Elena receives 4 percent of the trust's beginning-of-year fair market value each year as a unitrust amount, characterized as income for QTIP purposes under Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f).

The IRA is subject to the SECURE Act 10-year rule under IRC §401(a)(9)(H). The 10-year window ends December 31, 2036. Assume the IRA earns 5 percent per year during the window, and the trustee distributes the required minimum distribution each year under the "greater of" rule from Rev. Rul. 2000-2 (comparing the unitrust amount to the actual RMD each year and distributing the greater).

The year-10 §2519 trap fact pattern

Now vary the fact pattern. Suppose in year 10 (2036), Elena is 82 years old, in reasonable health, and does not need the year-10 $897,000 residual for her own living expenses. David (now 58) and Jennifer (now 55) have their own tax situations that are less demanding — they are in the 32-percent federal bracket versus Elena's 37 percent, and they live in Florida rather than New York (so no state tax on the receipt). Elena, coordinating with David and Jennifer through a family meeting, agrees to sign a written consent letting the year-10 $897,000 residual flow directly from the QTIP trust to David and Jennifer, bypassing Elena's own receipt-and-re-gift path.

The written consent is a §2519 disposition of Elena's qualifying-income-interest with respect to the year-10 residual. Under §2519, Elena is treated as having made a constructive transfer of the underlying property associated with the disposed-of income interest. Because Elena's income interest applies only to the year-10 residual (she retained her income interest in prior years' distributions), the constructive transfer is measured on the residual itself.

Step 1: FMV of the year-10 residual at the date of disposition = $897,000.
Step 2: Elena's income-interest actuarial value in the residual = negligible (the residual is being distributed in the same year, so the income interest has substantially zero remaining actuarial duration).
§2519 constructive transfer = $897,000 − ~$0 = $897,000.

Assume Elena has previously used $14 million of her $15 million lifetime exemption on prior gifts (a plausible fact pattern for a wealthy blended-family surviving spouse). The residual $1 million of exemption absorbs $897,000 of the §2519 transfer without triggering the 40 percent rate — but reduces Elena's remaining exemption to $103,000. If Elena has any further estate or lifetime gift-tax exposure, the reduction becomes real: her heirs will face 40 percent tax on the incremental amount above the reduced exemption at her death.

Now vary again. Suppose the IRA at Robert's death was $10 million rather than $5 million (a plausible fact pattern for a wealthy retiree). The year-10 residual then scales to roughly $1.8 million. Elena's §2519 constructive transfer would be $1.8 million. If Elena's remaining exemption at the time of the disposition is $500,000, the excess $1.3 million is taxed at 40 percent — a $520,000 out-of-pocket gift-tax cost that Elena did not anticipate. If Elena asserts the §2207A(b) reimbursement right against David and Jennifer, they must reimburse her $520,000 out of the year-10 residual they received. If Elena fails to assert the reimbursement right within the state-law limitation period, the un-recovered reimbursement is itself a §2511 gift compounding the tax exposure.

The correct pattern in this fact pattern

The correct pattern for Elena in the year-10 fact pattern is one of three alternatives. Alternative A: distribute the residual to Elena as trust income (following the "greater of" rule from Rev. Rul. 2000-2, which requires distribution to her), then Elena either keeps the residual or makes a separate gift to David and Jennifer using her annual exclusion ($19,000 per donee per donor in 2026 under Rev. Proc. 2025-32) and her remaining lifetime exemption. Alternative B: if Elena and the remainder beneficiaries want to accelerate the remainder receipt, structure the transaction as a §2207A(b)-cured §2519 disposition with a documented reimbursement mechanic — the reimbursement neutralizes the surviving spouse's out-of-pocket cost. Alternative C: retain the residual in the trust past the year-10 accounting deadline via a proper trust reformation that preserves the qualifying-income-interest structure — this option requires state-court approval and is only available where state trust law permits. In all three alternatives, the surviving spouse's consent to a bare year-10 distribution to remainder beneficiaries without §2207A(b) mechanics is the wrong answer.

Pre-disposition action checklist

The 8-item pre-§2519-disposition checklist for a QTIP-trust surviving spouse

Complete each item before the surviving spouse signs any consent, commutation instrument, disclaimer, or distribution direction that could be characterized as a disposition of her qualifying-income-interest. Retain the completed checklist and the executor's contemporaneous notes for the statute of limitations plus a reasonable margin.

  1. Identify the contemplated transaction as a §2519 disposition. Apply the PLR 200030030 two-category test: does the transaction (a) alter the surviving spouse's aggregate income entitlement, or (b) shift beneficial ownership from the surviving spouse to any other party? If yes to either, the transaction is a §2519 disposition. If no to both, the transaction is a §2519-neutral restructuring. Document the analysis in writing.
  2. Compute the §2519 constructive transfer amount. Perform the Step 1 (fair market value of the QTIP property at disposition) plus Step 2 (subtract actuarial income-interest value under §7520) computation. Use the §7520 rate for the month of disposition (or the two prior months if elected under §7520(a)) and the current mortality table (Table 2010CM as of 2026). The result is the §2519 constructive transfer to be reported on the surviving spouse's Form 709.
  3. Compute the incremental federal gift-tax exposure. Determine the surviving spouse's remaining lifetime exemption ($15,000,000 for 2026 under Notice 2025-67, less any prior taxable gifts). Compute the incremental gift tax at 40 percent on the excess of the §2519 transfer over remaining exemption. The resulting figure is the surviving spouse's out-of-pocket gift-tax cost absent a §2207A(b) reimbursement.
  4. Assess the §2207A(b) reimbursement mechanic. Determine whether the surviving spouse has a legally enforceable reimbursement right against the remainder beneficiaries or the QTIP property, and whether the reimbursement source is available. Draft the §2207A(b) assertion into the disposition instrument itself, so the assertion is contemporaneous with the disposition and cannot be later challenged as a delayed election.
  5. Compare against the §2044 alternative. Model the total transfer tax under (a) §2519 today with §2207A(b) reimbursement versus (b) §2044 inclusion at the surviving spouse's projected death. Consider the surviving spouse's own gross estate at projected death, applicable state estate tax rules, and the time-value of money. If (b) produces lower total tax, restructure the transaction to avoid the §2519 disposition.
  6. Check for state gift tax and state estate tax coordination. Some states (Connecticut, Minnesota) impose their own gift or estate taxes with lower exemptions than federal. A §2519 transfer that is federal-tax-neutral because of the $15M exemption can trigger meaningful state gift or estate tax. Review the state-tax exposure separately.
  7. Consider a private letter ruling. Any §2519 disposition with material tax exposure — over $500,000 in constructive-transfer value, or over $100,000 in projected gift tax — warrants consideration of a private letter ruling before execution. The PLR fee (currently in the $30,000 range for a §2519 ruling) is trivial next to the potential exposure of an erroneous execution.
  8. Document the surviving spouse's informed consent. Prepare a written memorandum, signed by the surviving spouse, acknowledging the §2519 constructive-transfer amount, the projected gift-tax cost, the §2207A(b) reimbursement mechanic, and the surviving spouse's understanding that the transaction is a lifetime taxable gift for federal gift tax purposes. The memorandum becomes the record if the surviving spouse or her heirs later challenge the transaction.

Frequently asked questions about IRC §2519 and QTIP-held inherited IRAs

What is IRC §2519 and why does it matter for a QTIP-held inherited IRA?

IRC §2519(a) treats any disposition by the surviving spouse of all or part of her qualifying income interest in QTIP property as a transfer by her of ALL of the underlying property, less the portion that would be included in her gross estate under IRC §2044 if she had died on the day of the disposition. For a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA under the SECURE Act 10-year rule, the year-10 residual is often $1M–$5M or more, and a well-intentioned surviving-spouse consent to letting the remainder beneficiaries receive the residual directly triggers a 40 percent federal gift tax on the entire residual value less her remaining $15M OBBBA lifetime exemption.

What did Rev. Rul. 98-8 hold?

Rev. Rul. 98-8 held that a court-supervised commutation of a QTIP trust — even one that preserved the surviving spouse's actuarial share — triggers two separate gift-tax events: a §2519 constructive transfer of the entire remainder value, and a §2511 transfer of the surviving spouse's actuarial income-interest value. The two components together equal 100 percent of the trust corpus. The ruling is the controlling authority on the practical breadth of §2519 and confirms that ANY commutation, sale, settlement, or consent to premature principal distribution triggers the rule.

What is the two-step §2519 computation?

Step 1: measure the full fair market value of the QTIP property immediately before the disposition. Step 2: subtract the actuarial value of the surviving spouse's qualifying income interest (measured under IRC §7520 tables at the applicable rate for the month of disposition, using the current mortality table Table 2010CM). The result is the §2519 constructive transfer, reported on Form 709 as a lifetime taxable gift. In parallel, IRC §2511 applies to the actual disposition of the income interest itself — the two provisions together capture 100 percent of the trust corpus in a full disposition.

How does IRC §2207A(b) cure the double-tax problem?

IRC §2207A(b) grants the surviving spouse a statutory right of reimbursement from the recipients of the constructively-transferred QTIP property, equal to the incremental federal gift tax attributable to the §2519 constructive transfer. The reimbursement is treated for federal gift tax purposes as if the reimbursed amount had never been transferred, so the surviving spouse's out-of-pocket cost is reduced dollar-for-dollar. But the right is not automatic — she must affirmatively assert it in writing, within the state-law limitation period, or the failure is itself a §2511 gift.

Is there a §2044 double-inclusion risk if the surviving spouse later dies?

No. The Treasury Regulations (§25.2519-1(a) and §20.2044-1) coordinate §2519 and §2044 explicitly: property that was the subject of a §2519 transfer during the surviving spouse's lifetime is not included in her gross estate under §2044 at her later death. The QTIP property is subject to a federal transfer tax exactly once — either §2519 during her lifetime, or §2044 at her death, but not both.

How does the SECURE Act 10-year rule create the §2519 trap?

Pre-SECURE, a QTIP-held IRA distributed over the surviving spouse's remaining life expectancy (20–35 years), so the year-by-year distribution was small and year-10 residual questions rarely arose. Post-SECURE, the entire IRA must distribute within 10 years, meaning a $3M IRA leaves roughly $1.5M–$2M as a year-10 residual. That residual, if characterized as principal and payable to remainder beneficiaries, tempts the surviving spouse to consent to letting it flow past her — a consent that is a §2519 disposition triggering a constructive gift of the entire residual value.

Can the surviving spouse make a partial §2519 disposition?

Yes, but §2519(a) treats the partial disposition as a constructive transfer of the entire portion of the QTIP property associated with the disposed-of income interest, not merely the amount transferred. A surviving spouse consenting to a $100,000 principal distribution can trigger a §2519 transfer of $500,000+ depending on actuarial values. Rev. Rul. 98-8 and Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(c) walk the partial-disposition mechanic and confirm that the constructive transfer is not proportional to the cash amount that changed hands.

Does §2519 apply if the surviving spouse takes an IRA distribution directly?

No. §2519 applies only to a disposition of the surviving spouse's qualifying income interest — a transfer of the entitlement itself, not the receipt of income under the entitlement. When the surviving spouse receives an IRA distribution as trust income under state characterization rules, no §2519 event occurs. The §2519 trap only springs when the surviving spouse consents to a distribution of principal to a remainder beneficiary bypassing her own receipt.

What are the most common §2519 mistakes in QTIP-with-inherited-IRA practice?

Six recurring mistakes: (1) surviving-spouse consent to a year-10 principal distribution to remainder beneficiaries without §2519 analysis; (2) court-supervised commutation of the QTIP trust triggering Rev. Rul. 98-8 on point; (3) failing to assert IRC §2207A(b) reimbursement within the state-law limitation period; (4) partial disposition without measuring the associated underlying-property transfer under Treas. Reg. §25.2519-1(c); (5) surviving-spouse disclaimer that fails IRC §2518's four-part test — the failed disclaimer is a §2519 disposition; (6) IRA in-kind distribution redirected to a remainder beneficiary through the surviving spouse's account with insufficient separation between the two steps.

How does the $15M OBBBA exemption affect §2519 exposure?

The OBBBA (Pub. L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) permanently increased the unified estate and gift tax exemption to $15 million per person for 2026 decedents and gifts, indexed thereafter. The §2519 constructive transfer uses the surviving spouse's remaining lifetime exemption first, with excess taxed at 40 percent. For most surviving spouses the $15M cushion absorbs the §2519 exposure without out-of-pocket tax — but it still uses lifetime exemption dollar-for-dollar, reducing the exemption available at her own later death. In fact patterns where the surviving spouse's own estate is projected to exceed the exemption, the §2519 transfer is not tax-free even under the $15M ceiling.
