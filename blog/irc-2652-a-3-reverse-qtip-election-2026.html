IRC §2652(a)(3) Reverse-QTIP Election in 2026: The Form 706 Election That Preserves the Deceased Spouse's §2631 GST Exemption Across a Marital-Deduction QTIP Trust and Locks In a Zero Inclusion Ratio for the Grandchildren

The DSUE portability mechanic under §2010(c)(5) ports the deceased spouse's Applicable Exclusion Amount but does not port the §2631 GST exemption — which means an executor who elects §2056(b)(7) QTIP treatment on the marital trust and does nothing else has permanently wasted the first decedent's $15,000,000 GST exemption. §2652(a)(3) is the escape hatch: a Form 706 Schedule R-1 election that treats the first decedent as the GST transferor of the QTIP corpus (overriding the default §2652(a)(1) rule that would make the surviving spouse the transferor at her later §2044 inclusion), allocates the first decedent's $15M GST exemption at the first death, and locks in a zero inclusion ratio under §2642(a)(1) that carries through the surviving spouse's life-income interest to the remainder beneficiaries — grandchildren, great-grandchildren, or a dynasty trust — twenty or forty years later. CalcLeap Editorial Published September 8, 2026 26 min read How we research

The §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election is the highest-leverage Form 706 election an executor can make when the first-to-die spouse's estate flows through a §2056(b)(7) QTIP marital trust and the family's estate-planning goal reaches beyond the surviving spouse to a generation-skipping remainder — grandchildren, great-grandchildren, or a multi-generation dynasty trust. The election preserves the first decedent's $15,000,000 §2631 GST exemption (2026 OBBBA-amended figure) inside the QTIP trust, locks in a zero inclusion ratio under §2642(a)(1) via the executor's allocation on Schedule R and Schedule R-1, and prevents the surviving spouse from becoming the GST transferor at her later death under the default §2652(a)(1) transferor-attribution rule.[1]

The stakes are large. GST exemption does not port under §2010(c)(5) — only the Applicable Exclusion Amount (AEA) ports. An executor who elects §2056(b)(7) QTIP treatment on a $20,000,000 marital trust with no reverse-QTIP election and no other GST allocation loses the first decedent's entire $15M GST exemption at the first death. When the surviving spouse dies twenty years later, her own $15M GST exemption (indexed to her death year) is what applies to the QTIP corpus — and if she has used any part of her exemption on lifetime gifts to grandchildren, or if the QTIP corpus has appreciated beyond her available exemption, every generation-skipping distribution to the remainder beneficiaries pays 40% GST tax on the shortfall.[2] On a $30M QTIP that has doubled to $60M over twenty years, the differential between "reverse-QTIP election made, first decedent's $15M allocated, inclusion ratio 0" and "no reverse-QTIP election, surviving spouse becomes transferor, only $8M of her exemption remaining" is on the order of $12M–$18M of avoided GST tax on the eventual generation-skipping distribution.

This piece is the practitioner-grade deep dive on §2652(a)(3) as an executor's Form 706 election. It complements the §2056(b)(7) partial-QTIP election piece (which walks the marital-deduction side of the same trust), the §2519 disposition-trap piece (which walks what happens if the surviving spouse gives up her income interest during her lifetime), and the §2010(c) DSUE portability piece (which walks the AEA-portability mechanic that reverse-QTIP does not replicate for GST purposes). Here the focus is the GST-transferor mechanic under §2652 — how the reverse election works, the §2654(b) severance move for QTIP trusts that exceed the first decedent's remaining exemption, the Rev. Proc. 2016-49 90-day cure for a missed election, the §9100 relief path for a missed cure, and the three worked case studies that cover the highest-frequency reverse-QTIP fact patterns in modern practice.

The §2652(a)(1) default transferor rule

The GST transferor rule under IRC §2652(a)(1) is the numerical center of the entire generation-skipping transfer tax. Every generation-skipping transfer — a taxable distribution under §2612(b), a taxable termination under §2612(a), or a direct skip under §2612(c) — is measured against the identity and the §2631 exemption of the "transferor" of the property. §2652(a)(1) provides the default rule: the transferor is the individual with respect to whom the property was most recently subject to Federal estate tax OR Federal gift tax.[3]

For property that passes at death under §2001 (federal estate tax), the transferor is the decedent — the individual whose death triggered the estate-tax measurement. For property that passes during life under §2501 (federal gift tax), the transferor is the donor — the individual who made the gift. For property that passes via a marital-deduction transfer under §2056 (either an outright bequest to a surviving spouse or a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election), a critical subrule applies: the marital deduction eliminates the transfer from the first-decedent's federal-tax base, so the first decedent is not "the individual with respect to whom the property was most recently subject to Federal estate tax." When the surviving spouse later dies and the QTIP property is included in her gross estate under §2044, the surviving spouse becomes the transferor because her §2044 inclusion is the most recent federal-tax measurement.

The result: for QTIP trusts under §2056(b)(7), the default §2652(a)(1) transferor rule attributes the transferor identity to the surviving spouse, not the first decedent. When the surviving spouse dies twenty years later and the QTIP corpus flows to the remainder beneficiaries — often the first decedent's children from a prior marriage, grandchildren, or a dynasty trust — the applicable GST exemption is the surviving spouse's, not the first decedent's. The first decedent's $15M GST exemption expires unused at the first death because the marital deduction (via QTIP or outright) prevented any federal-tax measurement of the property at the first death.

The GST-transferor default in one line

Under §2652(a)(1) as it operates against a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election, the surviving spouse becomes the GST transferor of the QTIP corpus at her later §2044 inclusion — the first decedent's $15M GST exemption is wasted at the first death unless the executor makes the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election. GST exemption does not port under §2010(c)(5). Once the first-decedent's Form 706 filing window closes without a reverse-QTIP election, the exemption is gone permanently.

The §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election

IRC §2652(a)(3) provides the executor's escape hatch from the §2652(a)(1) default. The statute reads: "In the case of any trust with respect to which a deduction is allowed to the decedent under section 2056(b)(7), the estate of the decedent may elect to treat all of the property in such trust for purposes of this chapter as if the election to be treated as qualified terminable interest property had not been made." "This chapter" is Chapter 13 of the Code — the GST tax provisions. The election is available only where a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election has been made; it overrides the default §2652(a)(1) transferor rule only for GST purposes, leaving the §2056(b)(7) marital deduction intact for estate-tax purposes.[4]

Once the reverse-QTIP election is made, the property inside the QTIP trust is treated for GST purposes "as if" the §2056(b)(7) election had not been made. That fiction produces two operative results. First, the first decedent is treated as the GST transferor of the property under §2652(a)(1) — the "individual with respect to whom the property was most recently subject to Federal estate tax" is the first decedent, because absent the §2056(b)(7) election the property would have been fully includible in the first decedent's gross estate and taxed under §2001. Second, the first decedent's executor may allocate the first decedent's §2631(a) GST exemption to the QTIP corpus on Schedule R (Notice of Allocation) and Schedule R-1 (Direct Skips from Trusts) of the timely-filed Form 706, producing an inclusion ratio of 0 on the fully-exempted portion under the §2642(a)(1) formula.

The mechanics on Form 706

The §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election is made in Part 3, Line 4 of Schedule R-1 of Form 706, with a supporting statement identifying the QTIP trust (by trust name, trustee identity, and if severed, the specific sub-trust or trust portion) against which the election is made. The election is irrevocable once made and once the return-filing deadline (nine months plus any six-month extension) has passed. The executor must contemporaneously make three coordinated Form 706 entries: (i) the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on Schedule M identifying the trust and the electing portion; (ii) the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R-1 identifying the trust or severed sub-trust; and (iii) the §2631(a) GST exemption allocation on Schedule R identifying the amount of exemption allocated and confirming the resulting inclusion ratio.[5]

Treas. Reg. §26.2632-1(b)(2) provides that an executor's allocation of GST exemption on Schedule R is effective as of the date of death of the transferor, valued at the estate-tax value under §2032 (or §2032A special-use value, where elected). This is critical for the reverse-QTIP mechanic because the exemption is allocated at the first death's valuation — before any appreciation over the surviving spouse's remaining life — locking in the inclusion ratio at the low pre-appreciation figure. A $15M exemption allocated to a $15M reverse-QTIP-elected trust produces inclusion ratio 0 regardless of how much the trust grows over the surviving spouse's remaining life; the same allocation to the same trust twenty years later against a $60M appreciated corpus produces inclusion ratio 0.75 — a 30x worse GST outcome for the same dollars of exemption.[6]

The formal inclusion ratio computation

Inclusion Ratio = 1 − (GST Exemption Allocated ÷ Trust Value at Allocation Date)

The inclusion ratio (IR) is the numerical measure of a trust's GST-tax exposure. An IR of 0 means the trust is fully GST-exempt and any generation-skipping distribution pays $0 GST tax regardless of amount. An IR of 1.0 means the trust is fully GST-exposed and any generation-skipping distribution pays 40% × distribution amount (40% is the maximum federal rate under IRC §2641(a) referring to the §2001(c) rate; the "applicable rate" for GST is the product of the maximum federal rate and the inclusion ratio). A fractional IR applies the maximum rate proportionately: a $1,000,000 taxable termination from a trust with IR 0.25 pays $1,000,000 × 40% × 0.25 = $100,000 of GST tax.

For the reverse-QTIP election to produce IR = 0, the executor's GST exemption allocation must equal or exceed the trust's estate-tax value at the first death. Where the QTIP corpus exceeds the first decedent's remaining §2631 exemption (a common problem for large estates that have consumed exemption on lifetime gifts, or for surviving spouses of first decedents whose $15M was reduced by pre-2025 gifting under the pre-OBBBA exemption regime), the executor must use §2654(b) severance to split the trust into a fully-exempt sub-trust and a fully-taxable sub-trust — a mechanic covered in detail below.

The §2654(b) severance mechanic

IRC §2654(b) authorizes the executor to sever a trust into two or more separate trusts, provided the severance is effective under state law and the several trusts are established with the same beneficial interests as the original trust in identifiable severed portions.[7] Treas. Reg. §26.2654-1(b) elaborates: the severance may be qualified for §2654(b) purposes only if (i) the several trusts are treated as separate trusts under state law from the date of severance, (ii) each severed trust's beneficial interests match a specific fractional or pecuniary portion of the original trust (either based on the value of the trust at the time of severance or on a permissible pecuniary funding formula), and (iii) the trustee has authority to sever under the trust instrument or under state law.

The reverse-QTIP severance move is the single most important use of §2654(b). Where the QTIP corpus at the first death exceeds the first decedent's remaining §2631 GST exemption, the executor severs the QTIP into two sub-trusts:

  • The GST-exempt sub-trust. Value equals the first decedent's remaining §2631 exemption. Reverse-QTIP election made on this sub-trust under §2652(a)(3). First decedent's full remaining GST exemption allocated. Inclusion ratio 0. All future generation-skipping distributions from this sub-trust are GST-tax-free forever, regardless of appreciation, and regardless of when the surviving spouse dies.
  • The non-GST-exempt sub-trust. Value equals the excess of the QTIP corpus over the first decedent's remaining GST exemption. No reverse-QTIP election. No GST exemption allocated. Inclusion ratio 1.0. Any generation-skipping distribution from this sub-trust pays 40% GST tax on the distribution amount.

The severance is made on Schedule R-1 of Form 706 by identifying the two severed sub-trusts, the fractional or pecuniary allocation formula, and (for each) the QTIP-election and reverse-QTIP-election posture. The executor's discretion on the severance ratio is broad; the standard practice for a $20M QTIP with $15M of first-decedent exemption remaining is a $15M / $5M severance with the reverse-QTIP election made only against the $15M sub-trust. The trustee thereafter administers the two sub-trusts as separate trusts for state-law purposes and for federal GST purposes, and can preferentially route generation-skipping distributions from the GST-exempt sub-trust and intra-generation distributions from the non-GST-exempt sub-trust to capture the full economic benefit of the allocated exemption.

The Rev. Proc. 2016-49 90-day supplemental-election window

Rev. Proc. 2016-49 provides a limited automatic-relief window for executors who filed Form 706 and made the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election but inadvertently omitted the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election.[8] The procedure applies where (i) the Form 706 was timely filed (nine months plus any six-month extension), (ii) the QTIP election under §2056(b)(7) was properly made on Schedule M, (iii) the reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) was omitted inadvertently, and (iv) the estate identifies the omission and files a supplemental Form 706 within 90 days of the original filing deadline (or, if longer, 90 days after the actual filing date).

The supplemental Form 706 must include a statement titled "Filed pursuant to Rev. Proc. 2016-49" at the top of the return, a corrected Schedule R-1 with the reverse-QTIP election properly marked, the corrected Schedule R with the §2631 exemption allocation, and a copy of the original Schedule M confirming the underlying §2056(b)(7) QTIP election. The IRS does not require a private letter ruling under this procedure and the relief is automatic — the supplemental election is treated as if it had been made on the original filing date, and the inclusion ratio is computed at the date-of-death valuation under §26.2632-1(b)(2).

Rev. Proc. 2016-49 supersedes the earlier Rev. Proc. 2001-38 supplemental-election framework for a wider set of estate elections, including specifically the reverse-QTIP omission. The 90-day window is measured strictly and cannot be extended by administrative discretion — an executor who identifies the omission on day 91 must proceed under §9100 relief instead. Practitioners handling any estate with QTIP-election filings should build the Rev. Proc. 2016-49 review into their post-706 checklist within 60 days of filing to preserve the supplemental-election opportunity.

§9100 relief for a missed cure

Where the Rev. Proc. 2016-49 90-day window has closed and the reverse-QTIP election was not made, the executor's only remaining path is discretionary relief under Treas. Reg. §301.9100-3. The regulation permits IRS discretionary relief for a missed regulatory election if the taxpayer establishes (i) the taxpayer acted reasonably and in good faith, and (ii) the grant of relief will not prejudice the interests of the government.[9]

§9100 relief requires a private letter ruling request under the annual Rev. Proc. 2026-1 (or successor) with a $38,000 user fee for estate-tax matters (as of 2026, subject to annual adjustment), an affidavit from the executor and from the drafting attorney establishing the reasonable-and-good-faith standard, supporting evidence that the omission was inadvertent (attorney malpractice affidavit, communication records demonstrating that the executor believed the election had been made, or a change in professional advisor between preparation and filing), and a wait of 6 to 18 months for the IRS to rule.

The IRS has granted §9100 relief for missed reverse-QTIP elections in a series of published private letter rulings (e.g., PLR 200629017, PLR 201040001, PLR 201409005), typically where the drafting attorney or estate accountant provides a malpractice affidavit and the estate promptly seeks relief upon discovery of the omission. Each ruling is fact-specific and does not create precedent. §9100 relief for a purely statutory election (as opposed to a regulatory election) is NOT available — but the reverse-QTIP election is treated as regulatory because it is authorized by §2652(a)(3) with mechanics prescribed by Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2, so it qualifies for §9100 relief in principle.

Three worked case studies for 2026 reverse-QTIP elections

Case 1: $30M QTIP with full §15M reverse-QTIP severance — Robert and Elena

Robert died on April 8, 2026 in New York, leaving a $30M estate to his surviving spouse Elena (age 68) in a testamentary marital trust drafted with a §2056(b)(7) QTIP-election clause and a Robert-drafted contingent bypass sub-trust. Robert had made no taxable lifetime gifts. His remaining §2010(c) Applicable Exclusion Amount at death is $15M; his remaining §2631 GST exemption at death is $15M. The remainder beneficiaries under Robert's marital trust are his three grandchildren (Robert and Elena's grandchildren, taken through their two adult daughters — the grandchildren range from ages 12 to 22 at Robert's death).

The strategic choice. Robert's executor faces an allocation puzzle: $15M of AEA to use for the bypass trust vs $15M of GST exemption for the reverse-QTIP allocation. Both are $15M. The executor's job is to route each dollar to the account with the higher future tax value. For a family in which the grandchildren are the ultimate recipients, the GST exemption is preferentially allocated to the QTIP trust (via reverse-QTIP election) because the QTIP corpus will be §2044-included in Elena's later estate and could be exposed to Elena's later GST rate on the way to the grandchildren; the AEA is preferentially allocated to the bypass trust because the bypass trust is NOT §2044-included in Elena's estate and its estate-tax posture is fixed at Robert's death regardless of what happens to §2010(c) thresholds by 2046.

The 2046 outcome. Elena dies in 2046 at age 88 with the QTIP-A sub-trust valued at $40M (having grown at 5% real over 20 years from $15M) and the bypass trust valued at $40M (same growth). The QTIP-A corpus is included in Elena's gross estate under §2044 for federal estate-tax purposes but Elena's own $22M inflation-indexed AEA (or her AEA plus Robert's DSUE) offsets a portion; the residual $18M is estate-taxed at 40% for a $7.2M federal estate-tax cost on the QTIP-A portion. But the GST posture is Robert-transferor with IR 0 — every dollar that passes to the grandchildren is GST-tax-free. Without the reverse-QTIP election, the QTIP-A distribution to the grandchildren at Elena's death would have been an Elena-transferor generation-skipping direct skip at 40% GST rate on the entire $40M (less Elena's residual $0-8M GST exemption at her death) — a $12M-16M additional cost.

Case 2: $12M QTIP with §2654(b) severance — Marcus and Sophia

Marcus died on July 15, 2026 in Florida, leaving a $12M estate to his surviving spouse Sophia (age 63) in a marital trust with a QTIP-election clause. Marcus made $7M of taxable lifetime gifts to a family GST-exempt dynasty trust in 2024 under the pre-OBBBA §2010(c)/§2631 exemption schedule, consuming $7M of both his AEA and his §2631 GST exemption. Marcus's remaining AEA at death is $15M − $7M = $8M; his remaining §2631 GST exemption at death is $15M − $7M = $8M. The QTIP corpus is $12M — greater than Marcus's remaining $8M of §2631 exemption, requiring severance under §2654(b).

The trustee's post-severance job is to preserve the tax-preferential characteristics of each sub-trust while satisfying Sophia's income and principal needs. The trustee's default routing is (i) mandatory income to Sophia is drawn pro rata from both sub-trusts (Sophia is entitled to all income under §2056(b)(7)(B)); (ii) discretionary principal distributions to Sophia (where the trust permits) are drawn preferentially from the QTIP-Non-Exempt sub-trust to reduce its value at Sophia's death and thereby reduce the amount that will pass to skip persons at IR 1.0; (iii) at Sophia's death, the corpora of both sub-trusts pass under the marital trust's remainder provisions to the grandchildren, but at IR 0 for the QTIP-Exempt residual and at IR 1.0 for the QTIP-Non-Exempt residual.

The 2046 outcome. Sophia dies in 2046 at age 83. Twenty years of trustee-preferential principal-distribution routing from the QTIP-Non-Exempt sub-trust have reduced its balance from $4M to $1.5M (Sophia consumed $2.5M of principal over the 20 years) while the QTIP-Exempt sub-trust has grown from $8M to $21M (5% real growth, no principal distributions). At Sophia's death, both sub-trusts are §2044-included in her gross estate for federal estate-tax purposes (Sophia's own $22M AEA plus Marcus's $8M DSUE = $30M total exemption offsets the full $22.5M combined corpus). The GST outcome at Sophia's death: the $21M QTIP-Exempt corpus flows to the grandchildren with Marcus as GST transferor at IR 0 — $0 GST tax on the $21M. The $1.5M QTIP-Non-Exempt corpus flows to the grandchildren with Sophia as GST transferor at IR 1.0 — Sophia's own residual GST exemption (her $15M as adjusted by prior lifetime uses) offsets the $1.5M. Effective GST tax at Sophia's death: $0.

Why the severance beats a "single reverse-QTIP with fractional inclusion ratio" alternative. Had Marcus's executor instead made the reverse-QTIP election against the entire $12M QTIP trust with $8M of exemption allocated, the inclusion ratio would be 1 − ($8M ÷ $12M) = 0.3333. Every distribution to the grandchildren at Sophia's death would pay 40% × 0.3333 = 13.3% GST tax on the distribution amount. On $22.5M of corpus at Sophia's death, that would be a $3M GST-tax hit — compared to the $0 GST hit under the severance strategy. The severance is $3M more efficient.

Case 3: $8M QTIP with a missed election and a Rev. Proc. 2016-49 cure — the Fitzgerald estate

Andrew Fitzgerald died on February 20, 2026 in Illinois, leaving an $8M estate to his surviving spouse Nancy (age 71) in a marital trust with a §2056(b)(7) QTIP-election clause. Andrew had made no taxable lifetime gifts. His remaining AEA and §2631 GST exemption are both $15M. The remainder beneficiaries under Andrew's marital trust are Andrew's two grandchildren from his son Daniel.

Andrew's executor (Nancy, acting through counsel) files Form 706 on November 20, 2026 (nine months after death), electing §2056(b)(7) QTIP treatment on Schedule M for the entire $8M marital trust. Counsel inadvertently omits the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R-1 and does not allocate any of Andrew's $15M §2631 GST exemption on Schedule R. The omission is discovered on January 15, 2027 (56 days after the filing) when Nancy's new accountant reviews the return during pre-tax-season planning.

The Rev. Proc. 2016-49 cure. Because the omission is discovered within 90 days of the November 20 filing deadline (i.e., by February 18, 2027), the estate qualifies for automatic supplemental-election relief. Counsel files a supplemental Form 706 on January 30, 2027 (71 days after original filing) captioned "Filed pursuant to Rev. Proc. 2016-49" and including (i) a corrected Schedule R-1 with the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election properly marked against the entire $8M QTIP trust; (ii) a corrected Schedule R allocating $8M of Andrew's §2631 GST exemption to the QTIP trust; and (iii) a copy of the original Schedule M confirming the underlying QTIP election.[10]

The outcome. The supplemental election is automatically effective as of the November 20, 2026 original filing date. Andrew is the GST transferor of the QTIP corpus (IR = 0). At Nancy's later death, the $8M corpus (adjusted for growth) flows to Andrew's grandchildren estate-tax-included in Nancy's estate (offset by her AEA + Andrew's DSUE) and GST-tax-free under the reverse-QTIP + full-exemption-allocation combination. Cost of the cure: attorney and CPA fees for the supplemental filing (~$8,000-$12,000). Cost of a §9100 relief request had the 90-day window been missed: user fee $38,000 + attorney/CPA fees $20,000-$40,000 + 6-18 month wait + uncertain outcome.

The Case 3 lesson

Every executor filing a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on Form 706 should build a 60-day post-filing review into the file to confirm the reverse-QTIP election and the corresponding GST exemption allocation were made. A five-minute checklist review during the 60-90-day window preserves a Rev. Proc. 2016-49 cure that costs $10K; missing the window forces a $60K-$80K §9100 path with no guaranteed outcome.

Interactions with §2519, §2044, and the partial-QTIP election

Interaction with §2519 disposition trap

The §2519 disposition trap applies during the surviving spouse's lifetime and treats any disposition by the surviving spouse of her income interest in QTIP property as a constructive gift of the entire remainder interest under §2519(a).[11] The reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) is independent of §2519 — the two operate on different sections of the QTIP lifecycle. If the reverse-QTIP election was made at the first death and the surviving spouse then disposes of her income interest during her lifetime, the §2519 constructive-gift analysis applies for gift-tax purposes (the surviving spouse is treated as making a gift of the QTIP remainder to the remainder beneficiaries under §2001), but the GST posture of the property is unaffected — the reverse-QTIP-elected corpus flows to the remainder beneficiaries under Robert's transferor identity (from Case 1) or Marcus's transferor identity (from Case 2) with the same IR = 0 that the original allocation locked in.

The narrow exception: if the surviving spouse's §2519 disposition converts the QTIP into a direct skip transfer to grandchildren (rather than a transfer to the surviving spouse's own remainder beneficiaries), the GST tax on the direct skip is measured under the original transferor's IR — 0 for a reverse-QTIP-elected corpus, so no GST tax. But the gift-tax cost under §2519 is real: the surviving spouse consumes her own §2010(c) AEA and, if the remaining AEA is exhausted, pays 40% gift tax on the constructive transfer of the remainder. Practitioners running any §2519 disposition analysis on QTIP-elected trusts must confirm both the reverse-QTIP status and the surviving spouse's residual AEA before advising the disposition.

Interaction with §2044 gross-estate inclusion

IRC §2044 requires that at the surviving spouse's death, the value of the QTIP-elected property is included in her gross estate for federal estate-tax purposes.[12] This inclusion is independent of the reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3): the reverse-QTIP election affects only the GST transferor identity and does NOT eliminate §2044 estate-tax inclusion at the surviving spouse's death. A reverse-QTIP-elected QTIP is still §2044-included in the surviving spouse's gross estate, taxed at 40% estate-tax rates against her own AEA (plus any DSUE from the first-decedent under §2010(c)(5)).

The critical mismatch: estate tax and GST tax are two independent tax bases on the same property, and the reverse-QTIP election addresses only the second. The estate-tax exposure at the surviving spouse's death is unaffected. A $30M QTIP that has grown to $80M at the surviving spouse's death produces $80M of §2044 gross-estate inclusion at the second death, offset by her AEA + DSUE, and estate-taxed at 40% on any excess — regardless of whether reverse-QTIP was elected. But the GST tax on the passage of the residual (post-estate-tax) corpus to the grandchildren depends entirely on the reverse-QTIP status: IR 0 (reverse-QTIP elected, $0 GST tax) or IR up to 1.0 (no reverse-QTIP, up to 40% GST tax on the residual).

Interaction with §2056(b)(7) partial-QTIP election

The §2056(b)(7) partial-QTIP election permits the executor to elect QTIP treatment for a specific portion of the marital trust — a common allocation between a bypass trust (using the first-decedent's AEA) and a QTIP marital trust (deferring estate tax to the second death). The reverse-QTIP election is available only against the QTIP-elected portion; the bypass-trust portion is not subject to §2652(a)(1) transferor-shifting at the second death (it is not §2044 property and the surviving spouse is not the transferor). For the bypass portion, the first decedent is the GST transferor by default under §2652(a)(1), and the executor allocates GST exemption to the bypass trust on Schedule R the same way. The practitioner's typical allocation for a $30M estate with $15M of first-decedent GST exemption is: $15M to bypass (first-decedent transferor by default; $15M GST exemption allocated), and up to $15M of QTIP with reverse-QTIP election and remaining GST exemption allocated — the exact split depends on state estate-tax exposure and expected asset appreciation.

Six most-expensive reverse-QTIP mistakes

The pattern of reverse-QTIP failures in 2026 practice is consistent:

  1. Not making the election at all. The single-most-common error — an executor who makes the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on Schedule M and stops there, without the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R-1 and without any §2631 exemption allocation on Schedule R. The first-decedent's $15M GST exemption expires unused. The cure: Rev. Proc. 2016-49 supplemental election within 90 days of the original filing deadline; otherwise, §9100 relief for six-figure fees and an uncertain outcome.
  2. Making the election but not allocating exemption. The §2652(a)(3) election alone does not allocate GST exemption — it merely designates the first decedent as the transferor. An election without a corresponding Schedule R allocation of the first-decedent's §2631 exemption produces IR = 1.0 (no exemption allocated), a fully GST-exposed trust with the first decedent as transferor. The cure: allocate exemption via Schedule R at the time of the reverse-QTIP election. Post-filing allocation is possible under §2632(a) but at the trust's later value, not the date-of-death value — potentially a much worse outcome.
  3. Not severing when the QTIP corpus exceeds the exemption. An executor who makes the reverse-QTIP election on the full QTIP trust with an exemption allocation less than the trust value produces a fractional IR that is worse than a severed-and-fully-exempted approach. On a $20M QTIP with $15M of exemption allocated, IR = 0.25 (single-trust) vs 0 on a $15M severed sub-trust (severance approach). The cure: use §2654(b) severance at the first death to split into GST-exempt and non-GST-exempt sub-trusts.
  4. Missing the 90-day Rev. Proc. 2016-49 window. An executor who identifies the missed election on day 91 loses the automatic supplemental-election path and must proceed under §9100 relief — $38,000 user fee, malpractice affidavits, 6-18 month wait. The cure: build a 60-day post-filing review into every §2056(b)(7) filing to identify the omission inside the 90-day window.
  5. Confusing DSUE portability with GST portability. DSUE under §2010(c)(5) ports the first decedent's AEA; GST exemption does NOT port. A practitioner who assumes that "if we port DSUE we've got the surviving spouse covered on both estate and GST" is wrong. The reverse-QTIP election is the only mechanic that preserves the first decedent's GST exemption for the eventual generation-skipping distribution. Practitioner training on the AEA / GST distinction is a persistent gap.
  6. Ignoring state-law severance formalities. §2654(b) severance is effective for federal-tax purposes only if the several trusts are treated as separate trusts under state law. Some states (California, Massachusetts, Illinois) require probate-court approval for a testamentary trust severance; others (Delaware, Florida, Texas) permit trustee-initiated severance under the trust instrument. The cure: verify state-law severance authority and complete any required state-law formalities before filing the Form 706 severance election.

The eight-item pre-Form-706 action checklist for reverse-QTIP

Every 2026 executor filing Form 706 for an estate with a §2056(b)(7) QTIP-election posture should walk this checklist before submitting the return:

  1. Compute the first-decedent's remaining §2631 GST exemption. Starting figure is $15,000,000 (2026 OBBBA-amended). Subtract any exemption allocated to lifetime transfers under §2632(a) (automatic allocation to direct skips) or §2632(b) (elected allocation to indirect skips via gift-tax return Form 709). The residual is the GST exemption available for allocation on Schedule R at the first death.
  2. Identify the QTIP corpus value at date of death. Determine whether the QTIP-elected portion of the marital trust exceeds the first-decedent's remaining §2631 exemption. If yes, §2654(b) severance is required to avoid a fractional inclusion ratio.
  3. Determine the severance ratio if required. Standard practice is a pecuniary severance: GST-exempt sub-trust value equals the first-decedent's remaining §2631 exemption; non-GST-exempt sub-trust value equals the residual. Verify state-law severance authority under the trust instrument or state statute.
  4. Make the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on Schedule M. Identify the trust name, trustee, and the electing portion (full or partial) on Schedule M. If severed, identify each severed sub-trust separately.
  5. Make the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R-1. Mark the appropriate box on Schedule R-1 identifying the QTIP trust (or the GST-exempt severed sub-trust) as the trust against which the reverse-QTIP election is made. Attach a supporting statement identifying the trust and confirming the linkage to the Schedule M QTIP election.
  6. Allocate §2631 GST exemption on Schedule R. Complete Schedule R with the exemption amount allocated to the reverse-QTIP-elected trust (or GST-exempt sub-trust). Confirm the resulting inclusion ratio equals 0. Where partial exemption is allocated (severance approach with sub-trust smaller than exemption), confirm the sub-trust's fractional IR under §2642(a)(1).
  7. Coordinate the AEA allocation between bypass and QTIP. Where the estate uses a bypass-and-QTIP structure, model the AEA allocation between the bypass trust (§2010(c) AEA absorbed at first death, not §2044-included at second death) and the QTIP portion (defers estate tax to §2044 inclusion at second death). The bypass trust preserves AEA for §2010(c); the QTIP portion defers estate tax but permits DSUE portability of unused AEA to the surviving spouse.
  8. Build a 60-day post-filing review into the file. Schedule a review 60 days after the Form 706 filing date to confirm all three elections (§2056(b)(7), §2652(a)(3), Schedule R allocation) are properly reflected in the filed return and to identify any omission inside the Rev. Proc. 2016-49 90-day supplemental-election window.

Trust-drafting checklist for new estate plans

Estate plans drafted in 2026 for testators who intend a reverse-QTIP-election-enabled marital trust structure should include the following provisions in the will or revocable trust:

  • Explicit §2056(b)(7) QTIP-election clause. The will or trust should authorize (not require) the executor to make the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on all or any portion of the marital trust. Mandatory-QTIP language reduces post-mortem flexibility.
  • Explicit §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP-election authority. The trust should authorize the executor to make the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on all or any severed portion of the QTIP trust, and should authorize the executor to allocate any portion of the decedent's §2631 GST exemption on Schedule R.
  • Explicit §2654(b) severance authority. The trust should authorize the trustee (and the executor via the trust instrument) to sever the marital trust into two or more sub-trusts, and should specify a permissible severance formula (pecuniary based on first-decedent's remaining GST exemption; fractional based on QTIP value; or discretionary as the executor determines).
  • Remainder-beneficiary specification. Identify the remainder beneficiaries with generation-skipping intent explicitly — grandchildren by representation, a multi-generation dynasty trust, or a specific dynasty-trust beneficial structure. Silent-remainder language routes to the surviving spouse's disposition, which may not accomplish the intended GST leverage.
  • Trustee routing authority. Authorize the trustee to route mandatory income and discretionary principal distributions preferentially between the GST-exempt and non-GST-exempt sub-trusts post-severance to preserve the tax-preferential characteristics of each.
  • Coordination with DSUE portability. Provide that the executor may elect DSUE portability under §2010(c)(5) independently of the QTIP and reverse-QTIP elections, so the surviving spouse receives the first-decedent's unused AEA for her own gift-and-estate purposes.

Frequently asked questions

What is the reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3)?

The reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3) is an irrevocable election that the executor of the first-to-die spouse makes on Form 706 (Schedule R-1) to override the general §2652(a)(1) transferor rule for generation-skipping transfer tax purposes. Under the default §2652(a)(1) rule, when property qualifies for the marital deduction under §2056(b)(7) (the QTIP election), the surviving spouse — not the first decedent — is treated as the GST transferor at her later death because her §2044 gross-estate inclusion is what triggers the transfer for GST purposes. The reverse-QTIP election reverses that treatment: the first decedent is treated as the GST transferor, which lets the first decedent's executor allocate the first decedent's §2631(a) GST exemption to the QTIP corpus at the first death and lock in a zero inclusion ratio under §2642(a)(1) that carries through the surviving spouse's life-income interest to the remainder beneficiaries.

Why does the reverse-QTIP election matter — doesn't GST exemption port under §2010(c)(5)?

No. The DSUE portability mechanic under IRC §2010(c)(5) ports only the deceased spouse's unused Applicable Exclusion Amount (AEA) for estate and gift tax purposes. GST exemption under §2631 does NOT port. If the first-decedent's executor does not allocate the first decedent's $15,000,000 GST exemption during life or at death, the exemption is lost forever at the first death — the surviving spouse cannot inherit or use it. For a first decedent whose estate flows through a QTIP marital trust that is expected to hold grandchildren as remainder beneficiaries, the reverse-QTIP election is the only mechanic that preserves the first decedent's GST exemption for the eventual generation-skipping distribution. Without the reverse-QTIP election, the surviving spouse becomes the GST transferor at her death (under §2652(a)(1)) and her own GST exemption (indexed to her death year) is what applies — a suboptimal outcome that wastes the first decedent's $15M.

When must the reverse-QTIP election be made and can it be made late?

The reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) must be made on Schedule R-1 of the first-decedent's timely-filed Form 706 (United States Estate Tax Return), which is due nine months after death with a permitted six-month extension for filing (not payment) under Treas. Reg. §20.6081-1. The election is irrevocable once made and once the return-filing deadline passes. However, Rev. Proc. 2016-49 provides a limited 90-day supplemental-election window: if the executor filed Form 706 and made the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election but did NOT make the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election, the executor may file a supplemental Form 706 within 90 days of the original filing deadline adding the reverse-QTIP election, provided the omission was inadvertent and the estate promptly identifies and cures the omission. Beyond that 90-day window, only a §9100 relief request under Treas. Reg. §301.9100-3 (reasonable and good faith, no prejudice to the government) can potentially cure the omission — an expensive and uncertain private letter ruling path with no guaranteed outcome.

What is the §2654(b) severance mechanic and how does it interact with reverse-QTIP?

IRC §2654(b) permits the executor to sever a QTIP trust into two or more separate trusts on Schedule R-1, with the reverse-QTIP election made only against the severed portion that the executor intends to fully protect with the first decedent's §2631 GST exemption. For a $20M QTIP trust with $15M of first-decedent GST exemption available, the executor severs the trust into (i) a $15M GST-exempt sub-trust (reverse-QTIP election made, first decedent's $15M GST exemption allocated, zero inclusion ratio under §2642(a)(1)) and (ii) a $5M non-GST-exempt sub-trust (no reverse-QTIP election, no GST exemption allocated, inclusion ratio of 1.0 for the eventual generation-skipping distribution). The severance is done at the first death via the executor's Form 706 election and produces two separately-administered trusts thereafter with different GST postures — a critical mechanic when the QTIP corpus exceeds the first decedent's remaining §2631 GST exemption.

What is the inclusion ratio and how does the reverse-QTIP election affect it?

The inclusion ratio under §2642(a)(1) is the numerical measure of the GST-tax exposure of a trust. Formally, inclusion ratio = 1 − (GST exemption allocated / value of property transferred, both at the time of allocation). A trust with inclusion ratio 0 is fully GST-exempt — no GST tax on any generation-skipping distribution. A trust with inclusion ratio 1.0 is fully GST-exposed — the maximum federal rate of 40% (under IRC §2641(a) referring to the §2001(c) rate) applies to any generation-skipping distribution. Fractional inclusion ratios apply proportionately. The reverse-QTIP election, combined with allocation of the first decedent's §2631 GST exemption on Schedule R, drops the inclusion ratio to 0 on the GST-exempt severed portion — the highest-value outcome and the reason the reverse-QTIP mechanic exists at all.

How does the reverse-QTIP election interact with the §2519 disposition trap?

The two mechanics operate on different sections of the QTIP lifecycle. The reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) is a Form 706 election made at the first death that fixes the GST-transferor identity as the first decedent. The §2519 disposition trap applies during the surviving spouse's lifetime and treats any disposition by the surviving spouse of her income interest in QTIP property as a constructive gift of the entire remainder interest under §2519(a). If the reverse-QTIP election was made and the QTIP corpus is GST-exempt (inclusion ratio 0), a §2519 disposition still triggers the constructive-gift consequence for gift-tax purposes under §2001, but the GST posture of the property is unaffected — the reverse-QTIP-elected corpus flows to the remainder beneficiaries under the first decedent's transferor identity regardless of §2519 gift-tax treatment. A §2519 disposition on non-GST-elected corpus (or the non-GST-exempt severed portion) triggers both a §2519 gift and, if the eventual remainder passes to a skip person, a full GST tax at inclusion ratio 1.0.

What happens if the executor makes a partial QTIP election under §2056(b)(7) — how does reverse-QTIP interact?

The §2056(b)(7) partial-QTIP election permits the executor to elect QTIP treatment for a specific portion of the marital trust — for example, electing QTIP for $8M of a $20M marital trust and leaving the other $12M to fund a testamentary bypass trust that consumes the first decedent's Applicable Exclusion Amount. The reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) is available only against the QTIP-elected portion (the portion that received the marital deduction under §2056(b)(7)) because §2652(a)(3) only applies where QTIP treatment was elected. The bypass-trust portion is not subject to §2652(a)(1) at the second death (it is not §2044 property and the surviving spouse is not the transferor) — the first decedent is the transferor of the bypass-trust portion by default under §2652(a)(1), and the executor allocates GST exemption to the bypass trust on Schedule R the same way. For a $20M estate with $15M of first-decedent GST exemption, the practitioner typically models the split to allocate $12M to bypass (first-decedent transferor by default; $12M GST exemption allocated) and $8M to reverse-QTIP-elected QTIP ($8M GST exemption allocated). The severance choice — bypass vs QTIP with reverse election — depends on state estate-tax exposure, expected asset appreciation, and the surviving spouse's income needs.

Can the reverse-QTIP election be made retroactively via 9100 relief?

In principle, yes; in practice, the outcome is uncertain and expensive. Under Treas. Reg. §301.9100-3, the IRS may grant discretionary relief for a missed regulatory election (as opposed to a statutory election, which cannot be cured under §9100) if the taxpayer establishes (i) the taxpayer acted reasonably and in good faith, and (ii) the grant of relief will not prejudice the interests of the government. §9100 relief requires a private letter ruling request under Rev. Proc. 2026-1 (or the successor annual revenue procedure), a $38,000 user fee for estate-tax matters as of 2026, and a wait of 6-18 months for the IRS to rule. Where the executor's omission of the reverse-QTIP election was truly inadvertent and the record supports the reasonable-and-good-faith standard — often supported by a professional-malpractice affidavit from the drafting attorney — 9100 relief has been granted in a series of published private letter rulings, but each ruling is fact-specific and does not create precedent. The Rev. Proc. 2016-49 90-day supplemental-election window is faster, cheaper, and more certain.

What is the effect of the reverse-QTIP election at the surviving spouse's death?

At the surviving spouse's death, the QTIP corpus is included in her gross estate under IRC §2044 for estate-tax purposes and passes to the remainder beneficiaries named in the first-decedent's QTIP trust. Under the default §2652(a)(1) rule, this §2044 inclusion would make the surviving spouse the GST transferor — and any distribution to skip persons (grandchildren, great-grandchildren, or unrelated persons more than 37.5 years younger than the surviving spouse) would be measured against her §2631 GST exemption, indexed to her death year. The reverse-QTIP election flips this: the first decedent remains the GST transferor throughout the surviving spouse's life and at her death, so the distribution to skip persons is measured against the first decedent's inclusion ratio (0 on the reverse-QTIP-elected corpus if fully exempted with the first decedent's §2631 exemption). The tax result at the surviving spouse's death is $0 GST tax on the reverse-QTIP-elected corpus regardless of how many years have elapsed and regardless of how much the corpus has appreciated in the intervening years.

What is the most common reverse-QTIP mistake in 2026 practice?

Failing to sever the QTIP trust under §2654(b) into GST-exempt and non-GST-exempt sub-trusts when the QTIP corpus exceeds the first decedent's remaining §2631 GST exemption. Without severance, the reverse-QTIP election on the full QTIP trust with an exemption allocation less than the trust value produces a fractional inclusion ratio (e.g., 0.25 for a $20M trust with $15M exemption allocated) — every future distribution to a skip person pays 40% × 0.25 = 10% GST tax, which grows with the corpus. The cure is to sever the trust at the first death into a $15M GST-exempt sub-trust (reverse-QTIP election, $15M exemption allocated, inclusion ratio 0, all future generation-skipping distributions tax-free) and a $5M non-GST-exempt sub-trust (no reverse-QTIP election, no exemption allocated, inclusion ratio 1.0, all distributions to skip persons pay full 40% GST tax). The severance permits the trustee to route generation-skipping distributions preferentially to the GST-exempt sub-trust and to route intra-generation distributions preferentially to the non-GST-exempt sub-trust, capturing the full benefit of the allocated exemption.
