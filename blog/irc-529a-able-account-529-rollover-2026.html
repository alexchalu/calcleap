IRC §529A ABLE Accounts in 2026: The SECURE 2.0 Age-46 Expansion, the §529-to-ABLE Rollover, the $19,000 Ceiling, and the SSI/Medicaid Playbook

For eleven years the disability community's most important tax-advantaged savings vehicle carried a hard age-26 gate. On January 1, 2026 that gate moved to age 46 — bringing an estimated six million additional Americans inside for the first time. Here is the complete practitioner's field guide to §529A after the change.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 2, 2026 28 min read How we research

The Achieving a Better Life Experience Act of 2014 — the statute that created what today we call ABLE accounts — passed with a design flaw hardwired into it: eligibility to open one was capped at individuals whose disability began before age 26.[1] The number was picked to align with the age at which Social Security's childhood-disability benefit vests, but it excluded almost everyone whose disabling injury or diagnosis arrived in their late twenties, thirties, or early forties — soldiers wounded on deployment, adults with early-onset multiple sclerosis, workers with sudden-onset spinal-cord injury, individuals whose bipolar or schizophrenia went undiagnosed through college. National advocacy for what became known as "ABLE Age Adjustment" ran for the full decade after enactment. In December 2022, President Biden signed the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2023, which carried SECURE 2.0 as Division T. Buried in that omnibus at Title I §124 was a single amendment that struck "26" and inserted "46" in IRC §529A(e)(1)(A) — effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.[2]

January 1, 2026 was that first tax year. Between the newly-eligible cohort of disabled Americans between ages 26 and 45 whose disability began after their own twenty-sixth birthday, the standard 2026 IRS Notice 2025-67 inflation adjustments that lifted the contribution ceiling to $19,000, and the arrival of a substantial body of Treasury Regulation guidance under TD 9923 (2020) plus decade-long IRS practitioner learning, ABLE accounts today are a materially different planning tool than they were even three years ago.[3] This is the field guide to what changed, what still applies, and how to fit an ABLE account into a broader Special Needs Trust, Medicaid-eligibility, or SSI-preservation plan.

What §529A ABLE accounts actually are — the statutory framework

Internal Revenue Code §529A, added by ABLE Act §102 in December 2014, creates a tax-preferred savings account modeled structurally on the §529 qualified tuition program but repurposed for individuals with disabilities. The parallels are deliberate — the drafting committee copied §529's basic architecture wholesale, then swapped every "qualified higher education expense" for "qualified disability expense" and layered in the means-tested-benefit protections that ABLE's actual purpose demanded.[4]

The core statutory grant sits in §529A(a): "A qualified ABLE program shall be exempt from taxation under this subtitle." That single sentence — parallel to §529(a) for college-savings plans — is what gives ABLE accounts the inside-the-account tax exemption on investment earnings that would otherwise generate annual §1(h) capital-gain or §61 ordinary-income tax. Withdrawals used for §529A(e)(5) Qualified Disability Expenses come out completely tax-free; non-qualified withdrawals are taxed under §529A(c)(3)(A) as ordinary income on the earnings portion plus a 10% additional tax that runs parallel to the §529(c)(6) college-plan penalty.

Only one ABLE account per designated beneficiary. §529A(b)(1)(B) makes this rule categorical: an individual may not simultaneously own two ABLE accounts. An in-flight rollover from one state's program to another is permitted under §529A(c)(1)(C) once every twelve months, but two accounts held open in parallel breach the one-account rule and trigger disqualification of the second account under Treas. Reg. §1.529A-2(c)(2).[5] This is the single most-common structural error practitioners see — a beneficiary opens an account in the state where they were born, then years later opens a second one in the state they live in without closing or rolling the first.

Ownership is nominal to the designated beneficiary; contributions may be made by anyone. §529A(c)(1)(A) permits contributions from the beneficiary themselves, a parent, a grandparent, an aunt or uncle, a friend, an employer, or even from a trust — but the annual contribution ceiling under §529A(b)(2)(B) is aggregate across all contributors, not per-contributor. Every dollar going in counts against the same $19,000 (2026) ceiling.

The SECURE 2.0 §124 age-46 expansion — the biggest ABLE change since enactment

Before January 1, 2026, an eligible individual under §529A(e)(1)(A)(i) was one whose blindness or disability occurred before age 26. SECURE 2.0 §124 amended this age threshold — but nothing else about the eligibility test — by striking "26" and inserting "46". The National Disability Institute estimates the change makes an additional roughly six million Americans potentially ABLE-eligible for the first time, with the largest single subgroup being veterans whose service-connected disabilities were diagnosed in their late twenties and thirties.[6]

Every other component of the §529A(e)(1) eligibility test is unchanged. The beneficiary must still either (a) be entitled to SSI or SSDI benefits by reason of the disability under §529A(e)(1)(A)(i), or (b) file a §529A(e)(1)(A)(ii) disability certification under penalty of perjury attesting to a "medically determinable physical or mental impairment which results in marked and severe functional limitations" expected to last at least twelve months, supported by a physician's signed diagnosis. Treas. Reg. §1.529A-2(d) sets out the mechanics — the individual (or an authorized legal representative) files the certification with the ABLE program at enrollment; the program retains the physician's diagnosis in its files and reports enrollments on Form 5498-QA.[7]

The onset-before-age-46 rule, not attained-age-46 rule

SECURE 2.0 §124 raised the age at which the disability must have begun from 26 to 46 — not the age at which the beneficiary must currently be. A 62-year-old whose disabling condition was diagnosed at age 41 is newly ABLE-eligible in 2026 because onset occurred before their 46th birthday. Conversely, a 44-year-old whose disability began at age 47 remains ineligible, notwithstanding their current age. The threshold is anchored at onset, not at the account-opening date.

The certification pathway matters more than the SSI/SSDI pathway for the newly-eligible age-26-to-45 cohort. Many working-age adults with disabling conditions never applied for SSI or SSDI — the wait times are long, the process is adversarial, and many households chose to preserve earning capacity rather than seek cash benefits. Those individuals can bypass the SSA disability determination entirely and enroll in an ABLE program on the strength of a physician-signed §529A(e)(1)(A)(ii) certification alone. IRS Notice 2015-81 §5.02 and the final regulations at Treas. Reg. §1.529A-2(e) make clear that the program is not required to verify the certification independently — the diagnosis in the file is sufficient, with the burden on the beneficiary to respond truthfully.

The §529A(b)(2)(B) contribution ceiling — 2026 numbers, ABLE-to-Work, and ordering rules

The annual contribution ceiling under IRC §529A(b)(2)(B) is set by direct cross-reference to the §2503(b) annual gift-tax exclusion. Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §3.42 set the 2026 §2503(b) figure at $19,000, up from $18,000 in 2025 and $17,000 in 2024.[8] That $19,000 is the aggregate ceiling across all contributors from all sources for calendar year 2026.

Layered on top of the base $19,000 is the ABLE to Work Act enhancement, codified as §529A(b)(2)(B)(ii) and originally sunset on December 31, 2025 under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act §11024 but made permanent by SECURE 2.0 §125.[9] A designated beneficiary who is employed and who does not participate in an employer-sponsored qualified retirement plan — a §401(a), §403(a), §403(b), §457(b), or SIMPLE plan under §408(p) — may contribute an additional amount up to the lesser of:

  • the beneficiary's compensation for the year, or
  • the federal poverty line for a one-person household in the state where the beneficiary resides, based on the HHS poverty guidelines issued for the calendar year before the contribution year.

The 2025 HHS poverty guidelines set the one-person household figure at $15,650 for the 48 contiguous states and D.C., $19,570 for Alaska, and $18,010 for Hawaii — those are the figures used to compute the 2026 ABLE-to-Work ceiling under Treas. Reg. §1.529A-2(g)(3).[10]

The employer-retirement-plan disqualification catches practitioners off-guard. A beneficiary who has any elective deferral, employer match, or nonelective contribution flowing into a §401(k), §403(b), §457(b), or SIMPLE-IRA during the year is entirely disqualified from the ABLE-to-Work bump — not merely required to offset. §529A(b)(2)(B)(iii) is a binary switch. A beneficiary who wants both the ABLE-to-Work capacity and workplace retirement plan participation must plan the calendar year around one or the other, not both.

Employer contributions to an ABLE account are also permitted under §529A(b)(1)(A) and are treated as wages excludable to the extent of the ABLE-to-Work ceiling — but neither the base $19,000 nor the ABLE-to-Work bump is a per-contributor limit. A working beneficiary whose employer contributes $10,000 has only $9,000 of §2503(b) headroom left for other contributors that year. This is another common structural mistake; families think of the $19,000 as "my parents' contribution" and add employer or self contributions on top without realizing the ceiling is unified.

The §529(c)(3)(D) §529-to-ABLE rollover mechanic

Section 11025 of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 added §529(c)(3)(D) to the Internal Revenue Code, permitting a tax-free rollover from a §529 qualified tuition program to an ABLE account for the same designated beneficiary or a §529(e)(2) member of the beneficiary's family.[11] The mechanic was originally scheduled to sunset on December 31, 2025 alongside the individual-provision TCJA sunset — a genuine risk that drove practitioner planning through 2024 and 2025. SECURE 2.0 §126 extended the rollover permanently.[12]

The mechanic itself is narrow. The rolled amount:

  • counts against the ABLE account's annual §529A(b)(2)(B) contribution ceiling for the year of rollover ($19,000 in 2026 for a non-working beneficiary; the enhanced ceiling for a working beneficiary);
  • must be delivered as a trustee-to-trustee transfer or a within-60-days direct rollover under §529(c)(3)(D)(iii);
  • must be to the same beneficiary or to a §529(e)(2) family member of that beneficiary, defined by §529(e)(2)(A) through (F) as the beneficiary's spouse, child or descendant, sibling, parent or grandparent, first cousin (added by EGTRRA 2001 §402(b)(2)), or any of those categories by marriage;
  • uses the receiving beneficiary's ABLE-account limits and eligibility, not the sending §529's — so the receiving beneficiary must independently satisfy §529A(e)(1).

The most common fact pattern: a family opened a §529 for a child expecting college attendance; the child was later diagnosed with a disability under the §529A(e)(1) test (or was already receiving SSI/SSDI); the §529 balance is now a poor fit because the child will not attend college and non-qualified §529 withdrawals trigger the §529(c)(6) 10% penalty plus §61 income tax on earnings. A §529(c)(3)(D) rollover to the child's ABLE account moves the money to a vehicle where it can fund a much broader set of qualified disability expenses — housing, transportation, assistive tech, ongoing therapy — without triggering the §529(c)(6) penalty and without the earnings being taxed.

Worked math — annualized rollover

A parent has $70,000 in a child's §529 that will not be used for college. The child is now an ABLE-eligible designated beneficiary. Because §529(c)(3)(D) rollovers count against the annual $19,000 ABLE ceiling, the family cannot roll the full $70,000 in one year. Instead: $19,000 rolled in year 1 (uses the full ABLE ceiling — no other contributions permitted that year), $19,000 in year 2, $19,000 in year 3, $13,000 in year 4. Alternately, if the child is working and eligible for the ABLE-to-Work bump, they can roll up to the enhanced $34,650 (continental U.S. 2026) ceiling per year — closing the four-year rollover in two years instead of four.

The 60-day-window direct-rollover option under §529(c)(3)(D)(iii) — where the account owner takes distribution then re-deposits at the ABLE program within 60 days — is a real option but a practitioner-disfavored one. The window is unforgiving; a missed deadline converts the distribution into a §529(c)(6)-penalized non-qualified withdrawal. Trustee-to-trustee transfers, coordinated directly between the sending §529 plan and the receiving ABLE program, remove the deadline risk entirely and are the standard practice.

Qualified Disability Expenses under §529A(e)(5)

Qualified Disability Expenses are the tax-free-out spending category for ABLE accounts. §529A(e)(5) defines QDEs by reference to a non-exclusive statutory list plus a general standard: any expense made "for the benefit of the designated beneficiary" that helps the beneficiary "maintain or improve his or her health, independence, or quality of life." The IRS elaborated this in Notice 2015-81, TD 9923, and the ABLE National Resource Center's ongoing IRS-coordinated interpretive guidance.[13]

Housing is the largest single QDE category in practice, and the one where the SSA/ABLE interaction requires the most care. Under Social Security's In-Kind Support and Maintenance (ISM) rules, third-party payment of a beneficiary's rent or mortgage counts as income and reduces the SSI benefit. When the beneficiary pays their own rent from ABLE funds, however, that payment is not income to them — and SSA's POMS SI 01130.740 §C.5 confirms ABLE distributions for housing QDEs do not count as ISM for SSI purposes.[14] The practical result: routing a parent's housing subsidy through an ABLE account (parent contributes to ABLE; beneficiary withdraws to pay rent) preserves the full SSI benefit that direct rent payment would reduce.

Non-qualified withdrawals hit twice. Under §529A(c)(3)(A) the earnings portion of a non-qualified distribution is taxed as ordinary income and hit with the 10% additional tax. Separately, under POMS SI 01130.740 §C.6 a non-qualified withdrawal that leaves the account and is not spent immediately can count as a resource to the beneficiary in the month received — potentially triggering the SSI $2,000 resource test and interrupting benefits. Documentation matters: keep receipts categorized against the ten §529A(e)(5) categories, keep withdrawal dates matched to expenditure dates, and preserve the audit trail.

The SSI $100K threshold and Medicaid full exclusion

ABLE Act §103 added two carve-outs to the means-tested-benefit framework that determine most of the account's real-world value. Neither is in the Internal Revenue Code — both live in the Social Security Act.

Medicaid: unconditional exclusion. Section 103(a) of the ABLE Act amended §1902(e)(15) of the Social Security Act (42 U.S.C. §1396a(e)(15)) to require every state Medicaid plan to disregard the entire ABLE account balance for purposes of determining Medicaid eligibility — regardless of size, regardless of whether the beneficiary is currently drawing SSI. This is the load-bearing rule for anyone whose Medicaid eligibility runs through the ABD (Aged, Blind, Disabled) pathway rather than through the ACA §1902(a)(10)(A)(i)(VIII) MAGI-expansion pathway. Without the §103(a) carve-out, an ABLE account holding $250,000 for education and housing would blow up Medicaid eligibility in every state that applies its default $2,000 asset test. The carve-out is unconditional; no state may opt out.[15]

SSI: $100,000 threshold with suspension, not termination. Section 103(b) of the ABLE Act amended §1613(b) of the Social Security Act (42 U.S.C. §1382b) to exclude the first $100,000 in an ABLE account from the SSI resource test — well above SSA's ordinary $2,000 resource ceiling. Balances above $100,000 do not disqualify the beneficiary from SSI; they suspend the monthly cash benefit. SSA's POMS SI 01110.115 makes the suspension mechanic explicit: the beneficiary retains SSI eligibility, does not lose their qualifying disability determination, and — critically — continues to be categorically Medicaid-eligible under §1902(a)(10)(A)(i)(II) of the Social Security Act during the suspension period. When the ABLE balance falls back below $100,000, SSI cash payments resume without a new application.[16]

The excluded-earnings design

The $100,000 ceiling is on the ABLE balance, not on cumulative contributions or on any specific slice of it. A beneficiary who receives $85,000 in third-party contributions plus $18,000 of tax-deferred earnings inside the account has crossed $100,000 and — absent a QDE withdrawal — will see their SSI cash benefit suspended in that month. Careful QDE spending in the months surrounding the threshold, or shifting contributions from third parties to withdrawals in the same year, can keep the balance below $100,000 indefinitely. Practitioners increasingly manage this proactively rather than allowing suspension to occur.

SNAP and HUD housing programs handle ABLE accounts similarly. Under the 2018 Farm Bill and USDA regulations at 7 CFR §273.8(e)(21), ABLE-account balances and QDE distributions are excluded from SNAP resource and income tests. HUD guidance in Notice PIH 2017-04 excludes ABLE assets from public-housing and Section 8 income calculations. The pattern across federal means-tested programs is consistent: ABLE distributions used for QDEs are not income; ABLE balances are not resources (subject to the $100,000 SSI carve-out).

§529A(f) Medicaid payback — the estate-planning wrinkle

IRC §529A(f) — the ABLE "clawback" or "Medicaid payback" provision — is the single most-litigated planning question for high-balance ABLE accounts. The statutory text reads:

IRC §529A(f)Subject to any outstanding payments due for qualified disability expenses incurred by the designated beneficiary, upon the death of the designated beneficiary all amounts remaining in the qualified ABLE account not in excess of the amount equal to the total medical assistance paid for the designated beneficiary after the establishment of the account, net of any premiums paid from the account or by or on behalf of the beneficiary to a Medicaid Buy-In program under any State Medicaid plan established under title XIX of the Social Security Act, shall be distributed to such State upon filing of a claim for payment by such State.

Three limits sit inside that sentence:

  1. Post-establishment only. The state's claim is limited to Medicaid paid after the ABLE account was opened. Pre-establishment Medicaid is not recoverable through §529A(f). This makes early account opening — as soon as the disability qualifies — meaningfully protective, because it starts the payback clock only from account-opening date forward.
  2. QDEs paid first. The state's claim is subject to "outstanding payments due for qualified disability expenses incurred by the designated beneficiary." Funeral and burial expenses, unpaid medical copays, unpaid rent, and any other QDEs the beneficiary incurred but had not yet paid out of the account before death are paid ahead of the state claim.
  3. Medicaid Buy-In premium offset. Any premiums the beneficiary paid to a state's Medicaid Buy-In program (under §1902(a)(10)(A)(ii)(XV) or (XVI) of the SSA) reduce the recoverable amount dollar-for-dollar.

The permissive word "shall be distributed to such State upon filing of a claim for payment by such State" has meaningful ambiguity: the state may file a claim, but §529A(f) does not require the state to do so. A growing number of state legislatures have used this opening to affirmatively decline to file §529A(f) claims. California's SB 218 (2023) directs CalABLE not to submit §529A(f) recovery claims; Oregon's HB 3227 (2023) does the same for the Oregon Achieving a Better Life Experience program; Pennsylvania and Washington state have adopted comparable no-recovery policies.[17]

The state-selection question therefore has two answers depending on planning priority. For a beneficiary primarily concerned with lifetime QDE spending, low fees and a strong investment lineup usually win — often pointing to Ohio's STABLE Account or Virginia's ABLEnow because they offer the lowest ongoing account fees and a wide range of investment options. For a beneficiary or family with substantial expected residual balance at death — say, an ABLE holding six-figure §529 rollover funds intended to pass to a sibling — a no-recovery state (California, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Washington, Ohio) may preserve $50,000-plus versus a default-posture state.

Choosing an ABLE program — the practical framework

PATH Act §303 (2015) struck the ABLE Act's original state-residency restriction, so a designated beneficiary in any state may open an account in any other state's program.[18] That opens the choice to a nationwide comparison across roughly forty-four active programs. The five factors that materially move the choice:

  1. State income-tax deduction for contributions. Thirty-three states plus D.C. offer income-tax deductions for contributions to their own state's ABLE program (parallel to §529 in-state-only deduction posture), with the deduction available only for in-state plans. Iowa, Kansas, Missouri, Montana, Pennsylvania, and Arizona are among the small group offering tax-parity deductions for contributions to any state's ABLE program. For a beneficiary in a deduction state, opening in-state is usually correct unless the fee gap is severe.
  2. Annual fees. Program fees range from Ohio's STABLE Account at $30/year plus a 0.19-0.34% asset-based fee to some smaller state programs at $60-$75/year plus 0.30-0.55% asset-based. Over a 20-year horizon on a $50,000 average balance, the fee difference between the cheapest and most-expensive program can exceed $3,000-$4,000.
  3. Investment lineup. Every ABLE program offers a checking-account option (FDIC-insured or NCUA-insured) plus a menu of Vanguard or BlackRock target-risk portfolios. Some programs also offer individual index funds (S&P 500, total-market, international) at institutional expense ratios below 0.10%. For a young beneficiary whose ABLE will hold funds for decades, access to low-cost equity index funds is meaningful.
  4. Debit card and QDE spending mechanics. Most programs issue a linked debit card (True Link is the largest vendor, used by CalABLE, Ohio STABLE, and several others). Card-based QDE spending simplifies audit trail and reduces the friction of monthly withdrawal-and-payment cycles. Programs without a debit card require the beneficiary to withdraw to a linked bank account and then pay QDEs from there — an extra layer of resource-timing risk.
  5. Medicaid payback posture. As Table 3 documents, the state whose program administers the account controls §529A(f) enforcement, not the state where the beneficiary resides.

Coordination with SNTs — ABLE, first-party trusts, and pooled trusts

ABLE accounts do not replace Special Needs Trusts. They complement them. The best-designed disability-financial plans typically use both — an SNT as the primary reservoir for larger balances that could never fit into an ABLE ceiling, and an ABLE account as the daily-living spend-down vehicle the beneficiary controls directly.

The load-bearing distinction: an ABLE account gives the beneficiary autonomy — they use a debit card, they choose what to spend on within the QDE categories, they build financial-management skills. A trustee-administered SNT gives no such autonomy but has no annual funding cap and can hold litigation settlements, inheritances, or life-insurance proceeds well above ABLE ceilings. Practitioners routinely fund an ABLE up to the $19,000 annual limit from an SNT — trustee cuts a check from the SNT to the ABLE program, ABLE spends it down on QDEs the beneficiary controls, cycle repeats annually.

Three worked case studies

Case 1 — Newly-eligible SECURE 2.0 §124 beneficiary: Elena (34), MS diagnosed at 29

Elena was diagnosed with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis at age 29 after a first demyelinating episode. She continued working part-time as a graphic designer earning $28,000/year with employer-provided health insurance but no employer retirement plan. Under the pre-2026 §529A(e)(1)(A)(i) rule, Elena was ineligible for an ABLE account because her disability onset was after age 26. Effective January 1, 2026 (SECURE 2.0 §124), the age threshold is 46 — Elena is newly ABLE-eligible.

Elena opens an ABLE account with her state's program in February 2026 using the §529A(e)(1)(A)(ii) certification pathway. Her neurologist signs the disability certification; she files with the program (no SSA disability adjudication required). Because Elena is working and has no employer retirement plan participation, she qualifies for the ABLE-to-Work enhancement. Continental U.S. resident, 2025 HHS poverty line for one-person household = $15,650. Her 2026 combined ceiling: $19,000 base + $15,650 ABLE-to-Work = $34,650.

Elena's mother contributes $12,000 to the ABLE for the year (staying under the $19,000 §2503(b) gift-tax exclusion so no Form 709 filing). Elena contributes $15,650 from her own wages as the ABLE-to-Work contribution, then claims the §25B Saver's Credit on Form 8880 for $2,000 of that contribution (Elena's AGI at $28,000 places her in the 10% Saver's-Credit tier for 2026 — $200 credit). Elena uses her ABLE debit card for MS-related copays, adaptive workstation equipment, and reliable transportation to appointments — all §529A(e)(5) QDEs. Nothing hits her SSI resource test (Elena is not on SSI); if she loses her job and later applies for SSI, her balance stays outside the $2,000 resource test up to $100,000.

Result: $27,650 in tax-advantaged QDE savings in year 1 that Elena controls directly, plus $200 of §25B Saver's Credit that would have been forfeit if Elena had contributed to a bank account. In no year before 2026 could Elena have accessed any of this.

Case 2 — §529-to-ABLE rollover for over-funded college plan: Marcus (18), autism spectrum disorder

Marcus was diagnosed at age 4 with autism spectrum disorder. His parents opened a §529 plan for him at age 5 and contributed for 13 years; the plan currently holds $85,000. Marcus's parents have concluded that traditional college attendance is unlikely; a supported-living residential program plus part-time work is the planned adult pathway. The parents open an ABLE account for Marcus in January 2026 (Marcus is a §529A(e)(1)(A)(i) SSI recipient — no certification pathway needed).

Under §529(c)(3)(D) the parents can roll from Marcus's §529 to Marcus's ABLE tax-free, subject to Marcus's annual ABLE ceiling of $19,000 (Marcus is not working — no ABLE-to-Work bump). At $19,000/year that is a four-year, five-month rollover schedule to move the full $85,000. Alternately, the parents can roll $19,000 to Marcus and $19,000 to Marcus's cousin Sophia (a §529(e)(2)(F) family member) who is also an ABLE beneficiary — accelerating the rollover if a family member with capacity exists.

The parents choose the four-year Marcus-only rollover. Year 1 ($19,000 rolled) uses the entire 2026 ABLE ceiling — no additional third-party contributions permitted for Marcus that year. Year 2 same. By end of year 5, the $85,000 has fully transitioned from §529 to ABLE. Marcus uses the ABLE for housing subsidies in his residential program (routed through ABLE to avoid ISM reduction of his SSI benefit), transportation, and assistive technology. Zero §529(c)(6) 10% penalty, zero §61 earnings tax, zero SSI benefit disruption.

Result: $85,000 of over-funded college savings converted to a lifetime QDE reservoir over four calendar years, all tax-free, all fully-excluded for Medicaid, all preserving Marcus's SSI cash benefit through housing-through-ABLE payment.

Case 3 — SNT/ABLE coordination for personal-injury settlement: Diana (42), spinal-cord injury at 39

Diana was injured in a motor-vehicle collision at age 39 resulting in T4-complete paraplegia. Her negligence-based settlement netted $1,850,000 after attorney fees and costs. Diana is on SSI ($967/month for 2026) and Medi-Cal. Diana's disability elder-law attorney funds a first-party (d)(4)(A) Special Needs Trust with the full $1,850,000 — this is standard practice; the settlement funds Diana's "own" for SSI purposes and would blow her past the $2,000 resource ceiling without SNT protection.

The (d)(4)(A) SNT carries mandatory Medicaid payback under 42 USC §1396p(d)(4)(A). California's Medi-Cal Estate Recovery Program will file a claim against SNT remainder at Diana's death for lifetime Medi-Cal paid on her behalf — potentially $600,000-$1,200,000 over Diana's remaining life expectancy.

Alongside the SNT, Diana opens a CalABLE account (California ABLE program, chosen because SB 218 waives §529A(f) recovery). Diana is newly ABLE-eligible under SECURE 2.0 §124 (onset at age 39, before age 46). Diana's SNT trustee funds the CalABLE at $19,000/year from SNT distributions (this is a permissible SNT distribution for Diana's benefit under standard SNT drafting; it does not count as a distribution to Diana for SSI purposes because it goes to the ABLE program directly).

Diana uses CalABLE for housing (rent), adaptive vehicle costs, and personal-support services she directs. She retains full autonomy over these spending decisions — no trustee approval required, no monthly disbursement schedule to coordinate. Over 20 years the CalABLE builds a working balance that, at Diana's death, passes to her named remainder beneficiaries under §529A(c)(1)(D) subject to §529A(c)(3)(A) taxation but WITHOUT §529A(f) Medi-Cal recovery (California's SB 218 waiver applies) and WITHOUT the (d)(4)(A) mandatory-payback that will consume the parallel SNT balance.

Result: Diana keeps the SNT structure for her large settlement (unavoidable given the size), but the parallel ABLE account preserves autonomy and — critically — provides a payback-free residual pathway for the $19,000-per-year segment of her assets that survives her death. If California's SB 218 posture holds for the next 30 years, the CalABLE remainder passes to Diana's chosen beneficiaries in full, while the SNT remainder will be substantially consumed by mandatory recovery.

Six recurring ABLE mistakes practitioners see

  1. Two accounts open at once. The §529A(b)(1)(B) one-account rule is categorical. A beneficiary who opens an account in State A and later opens a second account in State B (perhaps after moving) has to close the first account or roll it into the second within twelve months; otherwise the second account is disqualified back to opening and the earnings become currently taxable.
  2. ABLE-to-Work bump claimed while participating in an employer retirement plan. §529A(b)(2)(B)(iii) is binary. A single dollar of §401(k) elective deferral, employer match, §403(b) contribution, or SIMPLE-IRA contribution during the calendar year eliminates the entire ABLE-to-Work bump. Beneficiaries who want both must plan around one or the other — a common fix is to take a calendar-year suspension from workplace-plan contributions in a heavy-ABLE-contribution year.
  3. §529-to-ABLE rollover exceeding annual ceiling. §529(c)(3)(D) rollovers count against the ABLE annual §529A(b)(2)(B) ceiling for the calendar year. Attempting to roll $60,000 in one year — as a family might imagine when a §529 becomes surplus — will trigger an excess-contribution penalty on the amount over $19,000 (or the ABLE-to-Work enhanced ceiling). Multi-year sequenced rollovers or intra-family §529(e)(2) redirects to other ABLE-eligible relatives are the standard workarounds.
  4. Non-qualified withdrawal followed by delayed spending. A non-qualified withdrawal that lands in the beneficiary's checking account and sits there for more than the same-calendar-month period counts as a resource for SSI purposes in the next month under POMS SI 01130.740 §C.6. Practitioners guide clients to (a) confirm QDE status before withdrawing, and (b) time withdrawals to same-month spend.
  5. Missing the January-1 balance snapshot for the $100K SSI test. SSA measures ABLE balances against the $100,000 threshold on the first day of each month. A beneficiary at $99,500 on December 31 who receives a $2,000 January-1 contribution has crossed the threshold and will see January SSI suspension. Careful contributors sequence December contributions to keep the January-1 balance below $100,000.
  6. Choosing a program without checking §529A(f) posture. A beneficiary with substantial expected residual balance at death should not open in a default-payback state (New York, Massachusetts, Florida, Illinois, Texas) if a no-payback alternative (California, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Washington, Ohio) is available. The state-of-residence limitation on the state income-tax deduction is often the only reason to accept a default-payback program — and even then, the calculus flips at higher balances where residual protection outweighs the deduction.

8-item pre-opening ABLE account checklist

  1. Verify §529A(e)(1) eligibility. Confirm disability onset before age 46 (post-2025) or age 26 (pre-2026). Identify which pathway — (a)(i) existing SSI/SSDI recipient or (a)(ii) physician-certified disability certification. If certification pathway, obtain signed physician diagnosis before program enrollment.
  2. Confirm no existing ABLE account for the beneficiary. §529A(b)(1)(B) one-account rule. Check that no earlier ABLE account was opened in any state — commonly overlooked when families have moved.
  3. Choose program on the five-factor framework. State income-tax deduction / annual fees / investment lineup / debit card availability / §529A(f) recovery posture. Table 3 in this piece gives the payback-posture starting point; ABLE National Resource Center's state-by-state comparison tool has current fees and investment lineups.
  4. Identify all contributors and coordinate the aggregate §529A(b)(2)(B) ceiling. If parents, grandparents, or an employer will all contribute, agree on a per-contributor sub-cap so the combined total does not exceed $19,000 (or the ABLE-to-Work enhanced ceiling). Excess contributions must be returned by the tax-filing deadline or trigger a 6% excise tax under §4973(h).
  5. Confirm §529A(e)(1)(A)(ii) certification is filed if not on SSI/SSDI. The certification form is program-specific; retain a copy of both the certification and the underlying physician's diagnosis for at least six years (the §6501 statute-of-limitations period).
  6. Set up the debit-card / QDE-spending workflow. Most programs offer a linked debit card (True Link and comparable vendors). Configure spending categories, set alerts for suspicious activity, and create a monthly QDE-log spreadsheet to preserve the audit trail against §529A(e)(5) categories.
  7. If working, calculate ABLE-to-Work capacity. Verify no employer retirement plan participation in the current calendar year. Determine the applicable HHS poverty-line figure for the beneficiary's state (continental / Alaska / Hawaii). Coordinate wage contributions to hit but not exceed the enhanced ceiling.
  8. Coordinate with any existing SNT. If the beneficiary already has a first-party or third-party SNT, agree the funding pathway from SNT to ABLE — typically $19,000/year in trustee-cut checks from SNT to ABLE program. Confirm the SNT drafting permits ABLE-account funding (most modern SNT templates do; some 2015-era templates require decanting or reformation).

Frequently asked questions

Who is eligible for an ABLE account in 2026?

Any individual whose blindness or disability began before a threshold age — historically age 26 under the original 2014 ABLE Act, but raised to age 46 for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025 by SECURE 2.0 §124. Eligibility is established one of two ways: (a) the individual is already receiving SSI or SSDI on the basis of that disability, or (b) the individual (or their agent) files a disability certification under IRC §529A(e)(1)(A)(ii) attesting to a marked and severe functional impairment expected to last at least twelve months, supported by a signed physician's diagnosis.

How much can I contribute to an ABLE account in 2026?

The 2026 base annual contribution limit is $19,000, equal to the §2503(b) annual gift-tax exclusion per Rev. Proc. 2025-32. A working designated beneficiary who does not participate in an employer retirement plan can add up to the lesser of their compensation or the federal poverty line for a one-person household ($15,650 continental U.S., $19,570 Alaska, $18,010 Hawaii under 2025 HHS guidelines). Contributions from all sources combined cannot exceed those figures. A separate lifetime aggregate cap is set by each state ABLE program, typically $300,000-$600,000.

What is the §529-to-ABLE rollover?

IRC §529(c)(3)(D), added by TCJA §11025 in 2017, allows a tax-free rollover from a §529 college-savings account to an ABLE account for the same designated beneficiary or a §529(e)(2) member of the beneficiary's family. The rolled amount counts against the ABLE account's annual contribution limit for that year, so the mechanic is capped at $19,000 per year in 2026 (or the ABLE-to-Work-enhanced ceiling). SECURE 2.0 §126 made the mechanic permanent; it was originally scheduled to sunset on December 31, 2025.

Do ABLE assets affect SSI or Medicaid?

For Medicaid, ABLE assets are excluded from resource determinations regardless of amount — the exclusion is unconditional under ABLE Act §103. For SSI, ABLE balances up to $100,000 are excluded; above $100,000 the beneficiary's SSI benefit is suspended (not terminated), and the beneficiary remains categorically Medicaid-eligible during suspension under §1902(a)(10)(A)(i)(II) of the Social Security Act. When the balance drops back below $100,000, SSI cash benefits resume automatically without a new application.

What can I spend ABLE money on?

Qualified Disability Expenses (QDEs) under IRC §529A(e)(5) include education, housing, transportation, employment training and support, assistive technology, personal-support services, health, prevention and wellness, financial-management and administrative services, legal fees, oversight and monitoring, funeral and burial expenses, and any other expenses that help the designated beneficiary maintain or improve their health, independence, or quality of life. Non-qualified withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income on the earnings portion plus a 10% additional tax under §529A(c)(3)(A), and can also count as resources for means-tested programs.

What is Medicaid payback and does every state enforce it?

IRC §529A(f) permits — but does not require — the state that paid Medicaid benefits on behalf of the deceased designated beneficiary to file a claim against the remaining ABLE balance for medical assistance paid after the ABLE account was opened. Not every state actively pursues the claim; California (SB 218, 2023), Oregon (HB 3227, 2023), Pennsylvania, Washington, and Ohio have enacted state legislation waiving or restricting recovery. Even where enforced, the claim is capped at post-establishment Medicaid and stands behind funeral expenses and outstanding QDEs.

Do I have to open an ABLE account in my own state?

No. Congress removed the state-residency restriction in Section 302 of the Protecting Americans from Tax Hikes (PATH) Act of 2015, so a designated beneficiary may open an ABLE account in any state's program regardless of where they live. Beneficiaries frequently open out-of-state accounts to access lower fees, better investment lineups, or a friendlier Medicaid-payback posture — but state income-tax deductions for contributions are generally available only for in-state plans.

How does an ABLE account compare to a Special Needs Trust?

The two are complements, not substitutes. An ABLE account is beneficiary-controlled, cheap to open, funded from any source without changing the character of the contribution, and gives the beneficiary spending autonomy — but is capped at roughly $19,000 per year plus the ABLE-to-Work bump. A first-party or third-party Special Needs Trust has no annual funding cap and can hold litigation proceeds or inherited assets far above ABLE ceilings, but requires trustee administration, higher setup and ongoing legal fees, and — for first-party trusts — carries a mandatory Medicaid payback under 42 U.S.C. §1396p(d)(4)(A). Most sophisticated plans use both: an SNT as the primary reservoir and an ABLE account as the daily-living spend-down vehicle the beneficiary controls directly.

Can I claim the Saver's Credit for ABLE contributions?

Yes. Since tax years beginning after 2017 (PATH Act §303, codified at IRC §25B(d)(1)(D)), contributions made by the designated beneficiary to their own ABLE account qualify for the §25B Saver's Credit up to $2,000 of contributions per year. The credit rate is 50%, 20%, or 10% depending on adjusted gross income and filing status. For 2026, the top 50% rate applies to single filers with AGI at or below $24,750 per Rev. Proc. 2025-32. The Saver's Credit is nonrefundable.

What happens to the ABLE account when the beneficiary dies?

Under IRC §529A(f), the account terminates and the remaining balance is distributed. Outstanding qualified disability expenses (including funeral and burial costs, which are QDEs under Treas. Reg. §1.529A-2(h)) are paid first. Any state Medicaid claim under §529A(f), if the state enforces recovery, is paid next up to the amount of Medicaid provided after account opening. Anything left passes to the beneficiary's estate and is taxed under §529A(c)(3)(A) as ordinary income on the earnings portion (without the additional 10% penalty).
