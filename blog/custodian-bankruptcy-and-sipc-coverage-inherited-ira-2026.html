Custodian Bankruptcy and SIPC Coverage for Inherited IRAs in 2026: SIPA Proceedings, the $500,000 Ceiling, Excess Coverage at the Majors, and Every Structural-Risk Rule

A $2.4 million inherited IRA at Lehman Brothers in September 2008 came back to its beneficiary in full — in nine business days, via ACATS, at Barclays. Not because SIPC paid. Because SEC Rule 15c3-3 already required Lehman to segregate customer securities from firm assets. Here is the full framework — SIPA proceedings, the $500K/$250K statutory ceiling, inherited-IRA separate-customer treatment, excess-SIPC at the majors, and the pre-inheritance playbook every beneficiary should have.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 7, 2026 28 min read How we research

The single most common panicked question CalcLeap gets from newly-inheriting beneficiaries is a version of the same fear: if the brokerage holding my inherited IRA goes bankrupt, do I lose everything? The honest, defensible answer is almost never — but the reason has almost nothing to do with the Securities Investor Protection Corporation and almost everything to do with a technical SEC rule most retail investors have never heard of: 17 CFR §240.15c3-3, the Customer Protection Rule.[1]

SIPC is the fire insurance in a well-built stone building. Rule 15c3-3 is the stone. Every SIPC-member broker-dealer must, every business day, place fully-paid customer securities in a separate account that is legally beyond the reach of the firm's own creditors, and must maintain a Special Reserve Bank Account for the benefit of customers holding cash awaiting reinvestment.[1] When a broker-dealer fails and a SIPA liquidation begins under the Securities Investor Protection Act of 1970 (15 U.S.C. §§78aaa–78lll),[2] the SIPC-appointed Trustee's first job is not to write checks; it is to identify the segregated customer property, obtain a court-approved bulk transfer order, and move accounts en masse to a solvent broker via the Automated Customer Account Transfer Service (ACATS). Retail customers with clean, cash-and-blue-chip-securities accounts routinely have their positions restored within a business week or two.

The Lehman Brothers Inc. SIPA proceeding, opened September 19, 2008 and closed 2022, moved ~111,000 customer accounts holding roughly $115 billion in securities positions to Barclays and Neuberger Berman.[3] Customer-property claims were satisfied 100 cents on the dollar — no SIPC insurance advance was needed to make retail customers whole because Lehman's segregation had held. The general-unsecured-creditor claims in the parent Chapter 11 recovered roughly 30 cents on the dollar over 14 years; the broker-dealer retail-customer claims were satisfied fully by weeks 3–4 of the proceeding.

What SIPC actually is (and what it is not)

The Securities Investor Protection Corporation was created by the Securities Investor Protection Act of 1970 in response to a wave of back-office paperwork failures on Wall Street in the late 1960s that left thousands of retail investors unable to reclaim securities held at failed brokers.[2] Congress modeled SIPC loosely on the FDIC: a member-funded nonprofit with statutory authority to advance funds when a member broker-dealer fails and customer property is short. Membership is compulsory for every registered broker-dealer under 15 U.S.C. §78ccc(a)(2)(A) except a narrow set of specialist exclusions.[2]

SIPC's statutory coverage ceiling — unchanged since a 1978 amendment set it and a 1980 amendment refined it — is $500,000 per customer, of which no more than $250,000 may be cash awaiting reinvestment.[2] The $500,000/$250,000 figures apply per "separate customer" as that term is defined in SIPC's Series 100 Rules (17 CFR §§300.100–300.104),[4] which we walk through in detail in Section 5.

What SIPC covers, at high level, is the shortfall in customer property after the SIPA Trustee has (a) identified all fully-paid customer securities that were properly segregated and (b) attempted a bulk transfer to a solvent broker. If segregation held, no shortfall exists and SIPC insurance is not triggered — customers get their positions back via ACATS at 100 cents on the dollar. If segregation was breached (fraud, embezzlement, or trading loss that consumed customer property), SIPC advances up to $500,000 per customer to cover the shortfall in that customer's account, and the customer receives a general unsecured claim against the liquidation estate for anything beyond.

The critical distinction retail investors miss

SIPC does NOT insure against market losses, bad investment advice, unregistered investment contracts, or the failure of any issuer whose securities you hold. If your inherited IRA holds Tesla stock and Tesla stock drops 40%, SIPC will not reimburse the loss. SIPC's role is limited to reuniting you with the securities you actually owned when the broker failed — it is a custody-shortfall backstop, not an investment-loss backstop.

What SIPC categorically excludes is enumerated across the SIPA definitions in 15 U.S.C. §78lll and Trustee interpretations that have accumulated over 55 years:[2]

  • Market losses. If the S&P 500 falls 30% while your account sits at a solvent broker, that is neither a SIPA event nor a SIPC-covered loss.
  • Commodity futures contracts (regulated separately under the Commodity Exchange Act; the MF Global 2011 proceeding was a CFTC-driven separate track).
  • Unregistered investment contracts and interests in limited partnerships not held in a securities account (private-placement REITs, off-broker hedge fund LPs, art funds).
  • Fixed and variable annuities held outside a brokerage account. If your inherited IRA is at a life insurer holding a variable annuity contract, it is protected instead by state-guaranty-association coverage, which varies materially by state.
  • Precious metals unless held in specific securities-account form.
  • Cryptocurrency in most forms (Bitcoin held directly at a crypto-only platform like Coinbase or Kraken is not SIPC-covered; certain crypto-securities held at a SIPC-member broker-dealer such as spot Bitcoin ETFs at Fidelity qualify because they are securities of a registered fund).
  • Foreign currency and forex trading.
  • Losses caused by fraudulent investment advice where the customer authorized the trades (as opposed to the broker taking unauthorized positions with segregated customer property).

The SIPA proceeding, mechanically

When SIPC's Board determines that a member broker-dealer has failed or is in danger of failing to meet its obligations to customers, SIPC applies to the appropriate U.S. district court for a protective decree under 15 U.S.C. §78eee. The court appoints a SIPC-nominated Trustee — typically a partner at a major bankruptcy-law firm with securities-industry experience — and refers the liquidation to the U.S. Bankruptcy Court under SIPA's incorporated bankruptcy-code procedures.[2] From that moment forward, no new orders can be executed at the failing broker, but customer accounts are frozen only long enough for the Trustee to complete a segregation reconciliation — typically 5 to 15 business days at a well-run failure.

The Trustee's four sequential tasks:

  1. Segregation reconciliation. Compare the broker's Rule 15c3-3 possession-and-control records to Depository Trust Company (DTC) and transfer-agent records for every security position. Any mismatch is a customer-property shortfall.
  2. Bulk transfer. Solicit bids from solvent brokers to accept the customer accounts in a court-approved ACATS transfer. Lehman went to Barclays and Neuberger Berman; Bear Stearns went to JPMorgan Chase in a pre-SIPA orderly acquisition; MF Global's securities customers went to R.J. O'Brien and Vision Financial Markets.[3][5]
  3. SIPC advance for shortfalls. For each customer whose account is short, SIPC advances up to $500,000 ($250,000 cash) to cover the shortfall. The Trustee then holds a general unsecured claim against the estate for that advance.
  4. Estate liquidation. Liquidate remaining firm assets under the priorities set by SIPA §78fff-2, distribute pro rata to customers with claims above the SIPC advance and then to general unsecured creditors.

The Lehman proceeding's operational timeline is the canonical reference. Filing was September 19, 2008; customer accounts were opened for online access at Barclays by early October; the bulk of ACATS transfers completed by late November; the Trustee filed his first interim report in early 2009 confirming that customer-property claims were being satisfied at 100 cents on the dollar. Total time to make ~111,000 retail customers whole: roughly 6–8 weeks from filing.[3] Total time to complete general-unsecured-creditor distributions from the parent Chapter 11: approximately 14 years, closing formally in 2022.

The operational takeaway

The gap between "customer property is restored" (weeks) and "estate liquidation completes" (years) is enormous. For a beneficiary sitting on an inherited IRA with a firm-in-liquidation, the practical waiting period is the ACATS transfer window — historically 5 to 40 business days at the properly-segregated failures. RMD schedules under IRC §401(a)(9)(H) can absorb a 30-day interruption without triggering the SECURE 2.0 §302 25%/10% excise; a 90+ day interruption is worth a Form 5329 Part IX reasonable-cause waiver request. Inherited IRAs as a "separate customer" under SIPC Series 100 rules

The SIPC Series 100 Rules (17 CFR §§300.100–300.104), promulgated by SIPC under 15 U.S.C. §78ccc(e)(1), define how the $500,000 per-customer ceiling is applied when a single natural person holds multiple accounts at a failing broker.[4] The framework groups accounts by "separate customer" capacity, and each capacity gets its own $500,000/$250,000 ceiling.

For inherited IRAs, the operational rule that matters is: an inherited IRA is a separate customer from the beneficiary's own IRA. The beneficiary of a decedent's IRA holds that inherited IRA "in a separate capacity" — as beneficiary of that specific decedent — distinct from the beneficiary's own retirement accounts. Two inherited IRAs from two different decedents are two separate customers. Two inherited IRAs (a Traditional and a Roth) from the SAME decedent share a single ceiling because they represent the same beneficiary capacity.

SIPC separate-customer capacities that matter for retirement accounts

Each of the following is a distinct "separate customer" at the same broker-dealer, each with its own $500,000/$250,000 SIPC ceiling: (1) individual account; (2) joint account with spouse; (3) IRA (Traditional and Roth aggregate as one customer capacity for SIPC purposes — this is a change from FDIC treatment); (4) inherited IRA from decedent A; (5) inherited IRA from decedent B; (6) revocable living trust (grouped with individual capacity for SIPC purposes); (7) UTMA/UGMA custodial account (separate customer capacity for the minor beneficiary).

Concretely, if you inherited a $600,000 IRA from your mother and a $400,000 IRA from your father, both held at the same failing broker, you have two separate customer capacities and two separate $500,000 ceilings — $1,000,000 aggregate SIPC coverage for the two inherited accounts combined. If instead you inherited a single $1,000,000 IRA from your mother, you have one customer capacity and one $500,000 ceiling.

The Series 100 grouping does not itself protect against loss — it only defines how the SIPC advance is calculated in the rare event of a segregation-breached failure. In the far more common case of a segregation-honored failure (Lehman, Bear Stearns, Merrill Lynch), the entire account balance transfers via ACATS regardless of the ceiling. But if you are analyzing worst-case downside protection for a large inherited-IRA holding, the separate-customer analysis is the correct one.

The registration string test

SIPC treats accounts as separate customers based on the account's registration, not on the beneficial-owner status. An inherited IRA is registered in the form "[Custodian] IRA F/B/O [Beneficiary Name], Beneficiary of [Decedent Name], deceased [Decedent DOD]" or a functionally equivalent registration.[6] This registration string is what identifies the account as a distinct customer capacity in the Trustee's segregation reconciliation. If your custodian's registration string is malformed — missing the decedent name, missing the "beneficiary of" phrase, or (in rare error cases from custodian migrations) coded as your own IRA — the Trustee may aggregate the account with your own IRA for SIPC-ceiling purposes.

See our companion piece on inherited-IRA custodian titling failure modes for the specific error-mode taxonomy and how to detect a bad registration string on your quarterly statements. The problem is entirely preventable at account opening with a five-minute registration-string review.

Excess SIPC at the majors: the $1B private insurance backstop

The statutory $500,000/$250,000 SIPC ceiling was set in 1978 and has never been indexed for inflation.[2] Adjusted for CPI-U from 1978 to 2026, $500,000 in 1978 dollars is roughly $2.4 million in 2026 dollars — meaningfully understating the ceiling's real 1978 protective scope. In part to address the eroded real ceiling, and in part to differentiate their brands, every major U.S. broker-dealer purchases private commercial excess-SIPC insurance from Lloyd's of London syndicates and specialty carriers.

Excess SIPC has two important structural features you need to understand before relying on it. First, it is secondary and derivative: it only pays after the $500,000/$250,000 statutory SIPC has been exhausted, and only under the same triggering condition (customer-property shortfall in a SIPA proceeding, not market loss or bad advice). Second, it is aggregate-limited: unlike statutory SIPC, which has a per-customer ceiling and no aggregate cap, excess-SIPC policies have both a per-customer sub-limit AND an aggregate limit for the entire policy. At a hypothetical mass-fraud event at a major broker, the aggregate limit could be reached before every customer's per-customer sub-limit is paid.

The practical implications for a large inherited IRA:

  • Positions in publicly-traded securities at any of the majors are essentially always covered in full via statutory SIPC + excess SIPC (uncapped securities coverage at Fidelity/Merrill/Morgan Stanley/Vanguard; $150M at Schwab; $30M at IBKR).
  • Cash allocations above ~$2 million are the practical exposure point. Even with excess SIPC, cash coverage stops at roughly $2.15M total per customer at the biggest custodians and less at IBKR. A large inherited-IRA holding parked in a money-market fund is bumping against SIPC + excess-SIPC ceilings; a large inherited-IRA holding parked in bank-swept cash faces the FDIC ceiling analysis (next section).
  • Aggregate exposure matters in worst-case fraud scenarios. Schwab's $600M aggregate divided by ~34 million retail accounts is ~$17.50/account — a large-fraud event that consumed customer property across the base would exhaust the aggregate. At Fidelity's $1B aggregate divided by ~50 million retail accounts, ~$20/account. This is a theoretical concern; there has never been a triggering event at any of these firms in modern history.

Where SIPC ends and FDIC begins: the cash-sweep distinction

The single most technical part of custodian-risk analysis for a large inherited IRA is what happens to cash in the account. Different sweep options at the same custodian sit under fundamentally different regulatory frameworks with different ceilings.

Money market mutual fund sweeps (Fidelity Government Money Market Fund SPAXX, Vanguard Federal Money Market Fund VMFXX, Schwab Value Advantage Money Fund SNAXX) are securities. They are shares of a registered §851 fund of the Investment Company Act of 1940. As securities held in the broker-dealer account, they fall under SIPC's $500,000 per-customer ceiling — but for the security itself (the money-market-fund share), not the cash sub-limit. If the money market fund breaks the buck (a rare event; most recently the 2008 Reserve Primary Fund at $0.97/share, before Rule 2a-7 tightening), that is a market loss, not a SIPC event.

Bank sweeps (Fidelity FCASH swept to a Program Bank, Schwab bank sweep to Charles Schwab Bank, Merrill's ML Bank Deposit Program to Bank of America) are deposits. They sit in FDIC-insured accounts at a Program Bank, insured up to $250,000 per depositor per insured bank per ownership category under 12 U.S.C. §1821(a).[7] Most bank-sweep programs use a network of multiple Program Banks — Fidelity's, for example, uses 20+ Program Banks — so aggregate FDIC coverage can be $2M+ per customer. But the beneficiary needs to opt into the multi-bank sweep and verify the Program Bank list quarterly, because concentration at a single Program Bank caps FDIC at $250K.

The concentration trap

Bank sweeps at a single Program Bank are FDIC-insured only up to $250,000 per depositor per ownership category. A $600,000 inherited-IRA cash allocation swept to a single Program Bank has $350,000 uninsured. Most custodians allow you to elect multi-bank sweep programs that spread deposits across a network to expand FDIC coverage — this is a five-minute election that dramatically changes the risk profile for large cash balances. Verify Program Bank composition on your monthly statement.

The general rule for large inherited-IRA cash allocations:

  1. Below $250,000: single Program Bank sweep is fully FDIC-insured for the IRA capacity (per bank per ownership category).
  2. $250,000 to ~$2M: multi-bank sweep program is fully FDIC-insured; verify Program Bank list.
  3. Above ~$2M: either accept some deposit risk, or park excess cash in a money-market fund (SIPC $500K per customer capacity), or split across two custodians (each capacity gets its own SIPC + FDIC ceilings). Treasury bills held directly at TreasuryDirect are outside both SIPC and FDIC — they are direct obligations of the U.S. government.

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Priya, 42, inherits $1.4M IRA from her mother at a top-5 SIPA-eligible broker

Situation. Priya's mother, Anjali, died in March 2026 with a $1.4 million Traditional IRA at a large SIPC-member broker. The IRA was registered as "[Broker] IRA F/B/O Anjali Patel, deceased 3/15/2026, F/B/O Priya Patel, beneficiary." Anjali held: $600,000 in an S&P 500 index fund, $400,000 in short-duration Treasury bonds, $250,000 in a money-market fund, and $150,000 in bank-swept cash across three Program Banks. Priya is a non-EDB adult child subject to the 10-year rule under IRC §401(a)(9)(H). She wants to know the structural-risk profile.

Analysis. Priya's inherited IRA is one customer capacity under SIPC Series 100, distinct from her own $180K IRA at the same broker. Statutory SIPC on the inherited-IRA capacity provides $500K securities coverage + $250K cash sub-limit; excess SIPC via the broker's Lloyd's policy provides another $1.9M cash sub-limit. Layer-by-layer:

  • S&P 500 index fund ($600K): securities, fully covered under statutory SIPC $500K + excess-SIPC uncapped-securities up to broker's aggregate. Covered.
  • Treasury bonds ($400K): securities, covered under same framework.
  • Money market fund ($250K): securities (fund shares), covered under SIPC securities ceiling. If the fund breaks the buck, that is market loss, not custody risk.
  • Bank-swept cash ($150K across three Program Banks, $50K each): fully FDIC-insured under 12 U.S.C. §1821(a) at each of three separate Program Banks. Total FDIC coverage of $750K across the multi-bank sweep is far above the $150K balance.

Priya's structural risk on the $1.4M inherited IRA at this broker is effectively zero across statutory SIPC + excess SIPC + FDIC layers. Priya's operational risk (mis-registration, ACATS delay during an unlikely SIPA proceeding, cash-sweep concentration at a single Program Bank) is her only realistic exposure — all preventable with the pre-inheritance verification checklist in Section 8. She proceeds with a 10-year drawdown plan using our Traditional IRA calculator and 401(k) withdrawal calculator, keeping the inheritance at the incumbent broker.

Case 2: Marcus, 58, inherits $3.8M IRA and does the custodian-split analysis

Situation. Marcus's father, Robert, died in May 2026 with a $3.8 million Traditional IRA at a mid-size independent broker-dealer. The account is properly registered as inherited IRA. Marcus is a non-EDB adult child. The broker is SIPC-member but carries only $75 million aggregate excess SIPC with a $10 million per-customer securities sub-limit and $500K cash sub-limit. Marcus wants to know whether to split the inheritance across custodians.

Analysis. The securities layer is not the concern — Robert's holdings ($2.1M in Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF, $900K in short-duration Treasury bonds, $200K in a REIT ETF) are securities well below the $10M per-customer sub-limit and fully covered by statutory SIPC + excess SIPC. The concerning layer is cash: $600K sitting in a bank-swept program at a single Program Bank. FDIC covers $250K; the other $350K is uninsured deposit exposure against that Program Bank.

Marcus takes two remediation steps within the 45-day pre-migration window:

  1. Convert the single-bank sweep election to a multi-bank sweep program (available at the incumbent broker at no fee). Now $600K is FDIC-insured across three Program Banks at $250K per bank.
  2. Evaluate a partial trustee-to-trustee transfer of $1.5M to Fidelity as an inherited-IRA capacity there — creating a second $500K SIPC ceiling + $1.9M excess-SIPC cash sub-limit + Fidelity multi-bank FDIC coverage. Total structural coverage across both custodians rises to ~$4.5M cash + uncapped securities.

Marcus proceeds with option 1 immediately and option 2 conditionally on whether the incumbent broker's SEC Form X-17A-5 Part III audited financials show clean Rule 15c3-3 segregation over the last five years. Verification passes; he keeps the $3.8M at the incumbent broker with the multi-bank sweep election. Total time invested: ~4 hours across two phone calls and one Statement of Financial Condition review. Total structural risk after remediation: negligible across SIPC + excess SIPC + multi-bank FDIC.

Case 3: Diana, 34, inherits $850K IRA at a fintech broker-dealer with tighter excess SIPC

Situation. Diana's uncle, Neal, died in June 2026 with an $850K Traditional IRA at Robinhood Financial LLC (a SIPC-member broker-dealer since 2013). Neal held: $600K in S&P 500 index fund shares, $150K in individual tech stocks, and $100K cash awaiting reinvestment. Diana is a non-EDB but qualifies as a "not more than 10 years younger" EDB under IRC §401(a)(9)(E)(ii)(V) because Neal was her uncle (10 years and 3 months older). She wants to know if she should keep the account at Robinhood or transfer to a legacy broker.

Analysis. Robinhood's SIPC coverage is statutory $500K/$250K, matching every SIPC-member. Robinhood's excess-SIPC policy (through Aspen Insurance) covers up to $10 million per customer securities and $1.5 million per customer cash, with a $150 million aggregate. On paper, Diana's $850K position is fully covered.

The concerns Diana surfaces in her due-diligence review are operational, not structural:

  • Robinhood's 2021 GameStop-episode trading halts and 2020 fractional-share settlement issues signaled clearing-partner dependencies (formerly Apex, now self-clearing via Robinhood Securities) that could create ACATS complications during a stress event.
  • The 10-year drawdown window under §401(a)(9)(E)(ii)(V) (subtract-1 divisor Single Life Table for the not-more-than-10-years-younger EDB) creates a 45+ year stretch obligation, which is a very long time horizon for a mid-decade fintech's operational stability.
  • Robinhood's inherited-IRA product does not currently support see-through trust registrations as beneficiary — a real limitation for future estate-planning flexibility.

Diana elects a trustee-to-trustee transfer to Fidelity Investments Institutional (broker-dealer subsidiary), preserving her EDB status under IRC §401(a)(9)(E)(ii)(V) via the Single Life Table subtract-1 convention documented in TD 10001. The transfer takes 11 business days via ACATS. Post-transfer, her account carries Fidelity's $1B aggregate excess SIPC + $1.9M cash sub-limit + multi-bank FDIC on any bank-swept balance. Diana's decision was driven by operational and product-feature considerations, not SIPC ceiling math — but the destination custodian's structural profile is materially stronger on both the SIPC layer and the operational continuity layer.

Six mistakes that turn a structurally-sound custodian into an unnecessary risk

  1. Assuming SIPC covers market losses. The single most common misunderstanding. If your inherited IRA's Tesla position drops 40%, SIPC will not reimburse you. SIPC is a custody-shortfall backstop, not an investment-loss backstop. Every retail investor should read the SIPC brochure "How SIPC Protects You" at sipc.org/for-investors/what-sipc-protects — it takes 15 minutes and clarifies the boundary.
  2. Ignoring the cash-sweep concentration trap. A single-Program-Bank sweep election caps FDIC at $250K per ownership category. A $600K inherited-IRA cash allocation swept to a single Program Bank leaves $350K uninsured. Multi-bank sweep programs are typically available at no fee — the election is a five-minute call or two-click online change.
  3. Treating excess SIPC as market-loss insurance. Excess-SIPC policies from Lloyd's and other carriers are structurally identical to statutory SIPC in triggering condition: they only pay on a customer-property shortfall in a SIPA proceeding after statutory SIPC has been exhausted. They do not pay for market losses, bad investment advice, unregistered investment contract failures, or fraud outside of the SIPA-eligible broker-dealer.
  4. Misregistered inherited IRA that aggregates with own IRA under SIPC Series 100. If the custodian's registration string does not properly identify the account as beneficiary of a specific decedent, SIPC's Trustee may aggregate the inherited IRA with your own IRA for ceiling purposes. Check the exact registration string on your quarterly statement; the correct form is "[Custodian] IRA F/B/O [Beneficiary Name], Beneficiary of [Decedent Name], deceased [Decedent DOD]" or a functionally equivalent form. See our custodian titling failure modes piece for the taxonomy.
  5. Holding non-SIPC-covered assets at a SIPC-member without understanding the distinction. Cryptocurrency held at a broker-dealer's crypto affiliate (typically a separate LLC, not the SIPC-member broker-dealer) is not SIPC-covered. Certain limited partnerships, private-placement REITs, and non-registered investment contracts are not SIPC-covered. Fixed annuities and variable annuities held at a life insurer (rather than a broker-dealer) rely on state-guaranty-association coverage, which varies by state. Inventory what is in the inherited IRA and confirm each holding's coverage framework.
  6. Failing to verify SIPC membership at a non-obvious custodian. Robo-advisors, fintech custodians, and specialty broker-dealers vary in SIPC-membership status. Betterment LLC is a registered investment adviser; Betterment Securities LLC (the custodial arm) is a SIPC-member broker-dealer. Wealthfront's custodian is Wealthfront Brokerage LLC, a SIPC-member. Coinbase Global (crypto custody) is not a SIPC-member. Verify at sipc.org/list-of-members before assuming any custody arrangement is SIPC-covered.

The 8-item pre-inheritance and post-inheritance action checklist

Whether you are helping a parent plan their estate or you have just been named beneficiary on a $1M+ inherited IRA, run this checklist within the first 30 days.

  1. Verify SIPC membership at sipc.org/list-of-members. The custodian's legal broker-dealer entity name must appear on the list. If the custody is at a non-SIPC-member (e.g., pure crypto custody, off-broker LP interests, private-placement REIT sponsor), understand the alternative coverage framework (state guaranty, private insurance, none) before assessing risk.
  2. Request the current Account Protection Disclosure from the custodian in writing. Every major broker-dealer publishes annual excess-SIPC terms in a Legal Information or Account Protection section. Confirm the aggregate limit, per-customer securities sub-limit, per-customer cash sub-limit, and underlying carrier. Compare to peer custodians (Section 6).
  3. Review SEC Form X-17A-5 Part III audited financials for the custodian's Rule 15c3-3 segregation compliance. Every SEC-registered broker-dealer files annual audited financials; the Part III audited Statement of Financial Condition is required to be available to customers upon request. Any audit opinion qualification, going-concern note, or capital deficiency notice is a material signal.
  4. Verify inherited-IRA registration string matches the correct form ("[Custodian] IRA F/B/O [Beneficiary], Beneficiary of [Decedent], deceased [DOD]"). Compare against the quarterly statement. Any deviation (missing decedent name, missing "beneficiary of" phrase, own-IRA-style titling) is a call-the-custodian item within 48 hours.
  5. Configure the cash-sweep to multi-bank if any cash allocation exceeds $250,000. Verify the Program Bank list on the sweep-election form. Recheck quarterly — Program Bank rosters can change when a bank enters merger status.
  6. Identify all non-standard holdings in the inherited IRA (crypto, LP interests, private-placement REITs, physical precious metals, non-registered securities). For each, confirm the specific coverage framework and consider a partial in-kind or cash-out reposition into SIPC-eligible holdings before the 10-year drawdown obligation starts pressing.
  7. Confirm the successor beneficiary designation is on file at the current custodian. An inherited IRA that transfers via ACATS during a SIPA proceeding retains its designations only if the destination custodian's onboarding preserves them; some destinations require explicit re-designation. If you die during your 10-year drawdown window, your successor beneficiary steps into the remaining years — see our successor beneficiary 10-year clock piece for the mechanics.
  8. Set a 12-month recurring calendar reminder to (a) re-verify SIPC membership status, (b) re-download the Account Protection Disclosure, and (c) re-check the registration string. Custodian M&A, brand changes, and product-feature migrations happen faster than most beneficiaries expect. Our custodian M&A piece documents six major consolidation events since 2009 that affected inherited-IRA holders.

Frequently asked questions

Is my inherited IRA safe if the brokerage goes bankrupt?

Almost always — but not because SIPC pays every dollar. SIPC's role is to reunite you with your securities by transferring them to a solvent broker, not to reimburse market losses. Under SIPA proceedings, SEC Rule 15c3-3 already required the failing broker to segregate customer securities from firm assets, so most customers get their positions back in full via a bulk ACATS transfer. SIPC's $500,000-per-customer statutory ceiling ($250,000 cash) only comes into play if segregation was breached (Madoff-style) or the account contains uninvested cash above $250,000.

What does SIPC actually cover?

Under 15 U.S.C. §78lll(11), SIPC covers "securities" held in a customer account at a failing SIPC-member broker-dealer — stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, Treasury securities, money market fund shares. Ceiling: $500,000 per customer per capacity (individual, joint, IRA, inherited IRA, trust — each distinct), of which no more than $250,000 may be cash. SIPC does NOT cover market losses, commodity futures, unregistered investment contracts, cryptocurrency (except limited paths), fixed annuities, precious metals, or losses caused by bad investment advice.

Does an inherited IRA count separately from my own IRA for SIPC coverage?

Yes. SIPC Series 100 Rules (17 CFR §§300.100–300.104) group accounts by "separate customer" capacity. Your own Traditional IRA, your Roth IRA, an inherited IRA from your mother, and an inherited IRA from your father are each a separate customer — four distinct $500,000 ceilings. Two inherited IRAs from the SAME decedent share a single ceiling. The distinction matters most for six- and seven-figure inheritances.

What is excess SIPC coverage and which custodians offer it?

Excess SIPC is private commercial insurance major custodians buy from Lloyd's of London to supplement the statutory $500,000 SIPC ceiling. As of 2026: Fidelity carries $1B aggregate + $1.9M per-customer cash sub-limit; Schwab carries $600M aggregate + $150M per-customer sub-limit; Vanguard carries a Lloyd's excess policy; Interactive Brokers carries $150M aggregate + $30M per-customer sub-limit. Excess SIPC only activates after SIPC's $500K is exhausted AND under the same shortfall-in-estate triggering condition — it does not cover market losses either.

What was the largest SIPA proceeding in history and what did customers actually recover?

The Lehman Brothers Inc. broker-dealer SIPA liquidation, opened September 19, 2008, remained the largest ever at closure. SIPC-appointed Trustee James Giddens distributed ~$115 billion to customers — roughly 111,000 accounts made whole via bulk transfer to Barclays and Neuberger Berman within weeks. Customer-property claims: 100 cents on the dollar. General unsecured creditor claims (parent Chapter 11): ~30 cents on the dollar. The proceeding formally closed 2022.

What happens to cash sweep balances if the custodian fails?

It depends on the sweep vehicle. Bank sweeps (Fidelity FCASH to Program Bank, Schwab bank sweep to Charles Schwab Bank) sit in FDIC-insured accounts up to $250K per depositor per insured bank per ownership category. Money-market-fund sweeps (Fidelity SPAXX, Vanguard VMFXX) are securities held in the broker-dealer account and fall under SIPC's $250K cash sub-limit — but the money-market fund itself is a separate registered entity, not the broker. Single-Program-Bank sweeps at $600K balance leave $350K uninsured; multi-bank sweep programs spread deposits across Program Bank networks to expand FDIC coverage above $2M.

Which historical custodian failures have actually left inherited IRA beneficiaries with losses?

None, at properly-segregating SIPC-member broker-dealers. Every major SIPA proceeding — Lehman Brothers Inc. (2008), MF Global Inc. (2011), and the orderly Bear Stearns / Merrill Lynch acquisitions (2008-2009) — retail customers received their securities in full. Madoff (2008-ongoing) is the exception: customers had never received the securities their statements claimed, so recoveries by Trustee Picard proceed on a net-equity basis with clawback litigation.

Does SIPC cover fraud by the broker?

Yes, up to the $500,000/$250,000 ceiling. In segregation-honored failures, SIPC transfers your positions and you never need the insurance advance. In segregation-breached failures (Madoff), SIPC advances up to $500,000 per customer to cover the customer-property shortfall; anything beyond becomes a general unsecured claim against the estate. SIPC does not cover Ponzi schemes disguised as newsletters, off-broker advisor fraud, or affinity fraud with no SIPC-member broker-dealer involved.

Should I split a large inherited IRA across multiple custodians?

For inherited IRAs above $2 million, often yes — but for reasons more nuanced than SIPC. The $500K SIPC ceiling only bites when segregation is breached, which has never happened at Fidelity/Schwab/Vanguard/Merrill/Morgan Stanley in modern history. The real reasons to split: RMD-processing continuity if one custodian's ACATS goes down 30-60 days, cash-sweep FDIC concentration if you hold >$250K uninvested at one Program Bank, and access continuity during M&A migrations. Two custodians is often enough; three is diminishing returns.

How do I verify my custodian is SIPC-covered and what excess SIPC applies?

Three-step verification. (1) Confirm SIPC membership at sipc.org/list-of-members — every registered broker-dealer must be a SIPC member under 15 U.S.C. §78ccc(a)(2)(A). (2) Request the current-year Account Protection Disclosure — every major broker publishes annual excess-SIPC terms, aggregate and per-customer sub-limits, and the underlying carrier. (3) Review the custodian's Consolidated Audited Financial Statements filed on SEC Form X-17A-5 Part III, which document Rule 15c3-3 segregation compliance. If any cannot be produced, that itself is a signal to migrate.
