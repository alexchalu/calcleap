IRC §2652(a)(3) Reverse-QTIP Election for GST-Exemption Utilization on QTIP-Held Inherited IRAs in 2026: The Default-Transferor Trap, the Schedule R-1 Mechanic, and the Doubled-Exemption Playbook

The August 20 IRC §2056(b)(7) piece flagged the failure to make a reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3) as the highest-value planning error identified in surviving-spouse Form 706 preparation. That single missed election wastes the deceased spouse's entire $15 million GST exemption on the QTIP property and pushes every grandchild-remainderman distribution from the QTIP-held inherited IRA into 40% GST tax under IRC §2601. Today's piece walks §2652(a)(3) end to end: the §2652(a)(1) default-transferor trap, the Form 706 Schedule R-1 mechanic, the all-or-nothing rule of Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(a), the multi-trust drafting workaround, coordination with the DSUE portability election, interaction with the SECURE Act 10-year rule for inherited IRAs, six recurring failure modes, and a worked $12M inherited-IRA case study.

CalcLeap Editorial Published August 23, 2026 26 min read How we research

The federal generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax was Congress's 1986 answer to a specific planning pattern used by wealthy families for most of the twentieth century: transfer property to a trust for the benefit of your children, then have the trust remainder pass to your grandchildren, and pay estate tax only once in three generations rather than twice. The GST tax, codified at IRC §§2601-2664, imposes a flat 40 percent tax on every transfer that skips a generation, so a grandparent-to-grandchild transfer no longer bypasses the middle-generation estate tax without a matching GST tax at the same effective rate.[1] To keep the GST tax from crushing modest multi-generation planning, Congress paired the tax with a lifetime exemption at IRC §2631 — currently $15,000,000 per person for 2026 per IRS Notice 2025-67, tracked to the estate-tax exemption post-OBBBA — that shelters up to $15M of skip-transfers per grandparent from GST tax entirely.[2]

For a couple planning to leave property to grandchildren, the natural question is: can both spouses use their $15M GST exemptions, or does the marital-deduction machinery of IRC §2056 waste one of them? The answer is Congress-authored: the reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3) preserves the deceased spouse's GST exemption for QTIP-elected property that would otherwise fall under the default transferor rule of §2652(a)(1). Get the election right on the deceased spouse's Form 706, and both spouses' GST exemptions apply to the couple's multi-generation planning — doubling effective GST capacity from $15M to $30M for the 2026 baseline. Miss the election, and the deceased spouse's $15M is wasted forever.[3]

This piece walks IRC §2652(a)(3) end to end as applied to a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA. It sits as the seventh piece in the retirement-inheritance sub-cluster's practitioner-toolkit arc: 2026-08-17 non-UFIPA §409 field guide, 2026-08-18 §104 power to adjust, 2026-08-19 §106 unitrust conversion, 2026-08-20 IRC §2056(b)(7) QTIP election, 2026-08-21 IRC §2519 gift-treatment mechanics, 2026-08-22 IRC §2518 qualified disclaimer, and today's IRC §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election.[4] A practitioner who understands all seven pieces can walk the full inherited-IRA-plus-QTIP-plus-grandchild-remainderman mechanic from the deceased spouse's Form 706 through the SECURE Act 10-year rule, the surviving spouse's own death, and the ultimate grandchild-remainderman distribution — without a wasted GST exemption at the front, a §2518 disclaimer defect during the nine-month window, a §2519 recharacterization in the middle, a §2056(b)(7) qualification defect at any point, or a §2601 GST liability at the back.

The IRC §2652 statutory framework

The GST tax under IRC §2601 imposes a flat tax at the highest estate-tax rate (currently 40 percent per IRC §2001(c)) on every generation-skipping transfer. Three types of transfers fall within the GST: a direct skip (a transfer of property to a "skip person," typically a grandchild) under IRC §2612(c), a taxable distribution from a trust to a skip person under §2612(b), and a taxable termination of a trust in favor of skip persons under §2612(a). The tax is imposed on the "transferor" of the property — the individual who most recently was taxed on the property for federal estate or gift tax purposes.[5]

IRC §2652(a) defines the transferor for GST purposes:

  1. §2652(a)(1) — General rule. The transferor of property for GST purposes is the individual most recently subject to estate or gift tax on the property. For property included in the decedent's gross estate under IRC §§2033, 2038, 2039, 2041, 2042, 2043, or 2044, that individual is the decedent. For property transferred by lifetime gift under IRC §2501, that individual is the donor.
  2. §2652(a)(2) — Gift-splitting rule. When spouses elect gift-splitting under IRC §2513, each spouse is treated as the transferor of one-half of the split gift for GST purposes.
  3. §2652(a)(3) — Reverse-QTIP election. In the case of any qualified terminable interest property (QTIP) to which an election under §2523(f) or §2056(b)(7) applies, the executor of the deceased spouse's estate may elect to treat the deceased spouse as the transferor for GST purposes, notwithstanding the general rule of §2652(a)(1).

Treas. Reg. §26.2652-1 elaborates the general rule and Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2 walks the reverse-QTIP election mechanic. The election is made on the deceased spouse's Form 706 and is irrevocable once made.[6]

The default-transferor trap

The default rule of §2652(a)(1) creates a specific trap for QTIP-elected property. When the deceased spouse's Form 706 makes a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election, the marital deduction is claimed and no estate tax is imposed on the deceased spouse for that property. Under IRC §2044, the QTIP property is included in the surviving spouse's gross estate at her later death — that inclusion is the price the surviving spouse pays for the deceased spouse's marital deduction. The surviving spouse is the individual most recently subject to estate tax on the QTIP property at her death. Under §2652(a)(1), she is therefore the GST transferor of the QTIP property.[3]

The consequences of the default rule for a couple's GST planning are severe. Only the surviving spouse's $15M GST exemption applies to shelter distributions or terminations of the QTIP trust in favor of grandchildren. The deceased spouse's $15M GST exemption on the QTIP property is permanently wasted — it cannot be transferred to the surviving spouse under any portability mechanism (the GST exemption is not portable, unlike the estate-tax exemption under IRC §2010(c)(5)). For a couple with a $10M QTIP trust and grandchild remaindermen, the default rule means the surviving spouse's remaining GST exemption (after any lifetime skip gifts she has made) is the only shield against §2601 tax on the eventual grandchild-remainderman distribution.

Why §2652(a)(3) exists

Congress enacted the modern GST tax and the accompanying §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election in the Tax Reform Act of 1986 as part of a package that transformed the earlier 1976 GST tax (which had a $250,000 exemption per grandchild and complex rate mechanics) into the current uniform regime.[7] The drafters recognized that the §2652(a)(1) default rule created a specific asymmetry between QTIP property (where the marital deduction pushed the GST transferor to the surviving spouse) and non-QTIP property (where the GST transferor was the decedent whose exemption could shelter the property). Without a corrective election, wealthy couples would face a choice between (a) using the marital deduction to defer estate tax but losing the deceased spouse's GST exemption on the deferred property, or (b) not electing marital-deduction treatment and losing the estate-tax deferral to preserve the GST exemption. The reverse-QTIP election lets the executor choose both benefits — marital-deduction estate-tax deferral AND deceased-spouse GST-exemption preservation — for the QTIP-elected property.

The election mechanic on Form 706

The reverse-QTIP election is made on the deceased spouse's Form 706, filed nine months after death (or fifteen months with an automatic six-month extension under IRC §6081 requested on Form 4768 before the original due date).[8] The mechanic involves three coordinated Form 706 entries:

  1. Schedule M — the QTIP election under §2056(b)(7). The executor identifies the QTIP-eligible property (the QTIP trust into which the inherited IRA or other property passes), computes the marital-deduction value, and elects QTIP treatment. This is the gateway election — without a valid §2056(b)(7) QTIP election, there is no property for the reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) to apply to.
  2. Schedule R — allocation of the deceased spouse's GST exemption. Schedule R (Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax) is used to allocate the deceased spouse's GST exemption to specific transfers. For property to which the reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) applies, the deceased spouse's GST exemption is allocated to that property on Schedule R Part 2 (allocation of GST exemption to trusts of which the decedent is not the transferor absent the election).
  3. Schedule R-1 — the reverse-QTIP election itself. Schedule R-1 of Form 706 is a specific-purpose schedule used to document the reverse-QTIP election. The executor identifies the QTIP trust or property to which the election applies, and the election is complete when the schedule is signed and filed with the Form 706. The instructions to Form 706 (currently at Instructions for Form 706 (Rev. September 2024) as updated for 2026 filings) walk the Schedule R-1 mechanic in detail.[9]

The election is irrevocable once made. Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(b) is explicit: the election cannot be revoked, modified, or partially undone on any subsequent return. If the executor makes the election on the full QTIP trust and later concludes that a partial election would have been optimal, no remedy is available — the choice at the Form 706 filing is final.[6]

The reciprocal effect on the surviving spouse's Form 706

When the deceased spouse's executor makes a valid reverse-QTIP election, the effect on the surviving spouse's later Form 706 is corresponding. The QTIP property is still included in the surviving spouse's gross estate under IRC §2044 (the reverse-QTIP election does not change the estate-tax inclusion). But because the deceased spouse is the GST transferor under §2652(a)(3), the surviving spouse's death does not create a new GST transferor with respect to the QTIP property. The inclusion ratio of the QTIP property for GST purposes is determined by reference to the deceased spouse's GST exemption allocated on the Form 706 that made the reverse-QTIP election.[6]

This is the mechanism by which the reverse-QTIP election works: at the surviving spouse's later death, when the QTIP property is distributed (or the trust terminates) in favor of the couple's grandchildren, the GST calculation looks back to the deceased spouse's Form 706 for the transferor identity and the applicable inclusion ratio. If the deceased spouse's executor allocated GST exemption sufficient to produce a 0% inclusion ratio, no GST tax is imposed on the distribution. If the allocation was insufficient, a partial inclusion ratio applies and GST tax is imposed on the excess.

The inclusion ratio formula

The inclusion ratio under IRC §2642(a) determines what fraction of a transfer from a trust is subject to GST tax. The formula is: Inclusion ratio = 1 − (GST exemption allocated ÷ Value of property transferred). If the deceased spouse's executor allocates $10M of GST exemption to a $10M QTIP trust to which the reverse-QTIP election applies, the inclusion ratio is 1 − ($10M ÷ $10M) = 0. Every subsequent distribution or termination from that trust to a skip person is sheltered from GST tax. If the executor allocates only $6M of GST exemption to a $10M QTIP trust, the inclusion ratio is 1 − ($6M ÷ $10M) = 0.4. Each subsequent skip-transfer from the trust is taxed at 0.4 × 40% = 16% effective GST rate.

The all-or-nothing rule under Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(a)

Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(a) contains a specific procedural rule that shapes essentially every high-net-worth couple's QTIP drafting: the reverse-QTIP election must apply to all of the property in a single QTIP trust, or to none of it. Partial elections within a single trust are not permitted.[6]

The regulation's text is unambiguous. If the executor makes a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on a $12 million QTIP trust and wishes to make a reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) on that trust, the reverse-QTIP election applies to the entire $12 million. The executor cannot elect reverse-QTIP treatment for the first $8 million (matching the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption after prior gifts) and leave the remaining $4 million under the default §2652(a)(1) rule. The choice is binary: all $12 million under reverse-QTIP (with a partial inclusion ratio if GST exemption is insufficient) or all $12 million under the default rule (with the surviving spouse as later GST transferor).

Why the all-or-nothing rule exists

The Treasury's stated rationale for the all-or-nothing rule is administrative simplicity: allowing partial elections within a single trust would require the trustee to track two separate inclusion ratios for the same trust and to allocate every distribution proportionally between the reverse-QTIP portion and the default portion. That accounting burden would fall on the trustee for the trust's entire life, potentially decades after the executor's Form 706 election. The regulation forces the accounting complexity to be resolved at the drafting stage — before the deceased spouse's death — by requiring separate QTIP trusts if the executor wants partial reverse-QTIP treatment on the couple's aggregate QTIP property.[6]

The single-trust suboptimal outcome

For an estate plan that funds a single QTIP trust with property exceeding the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption, the all-or-nothing rule produces a suboptimal outcome. Consider a $20 million QTIP trust and a deceased spouse with $15 million of remaining GST exemption (no prior lifetime skip gifts). The executor has two choices:

  • Option A — Reverse-QTIP on the full $20M trust. The deceased spouse is the GST transferor for the full $20M. The $15M GST exemption produces an inclusion ratio of 1 − ($15M ÷ $20M) = 0.25. Every subsequent skip-transfer is taxed at 0.25 × 40% = 10% effective GST rate. Grandchild distributions later face a 10% GST tax on the taxable portion.
  • Option B — Default §2652(a)(1) on the full $20M trust. The surviving spouse is the GST transferor at her later death. Her own $15M GST exemption applies at her death. If she has made no lifetime skip gifts, the entire $20M trust may fall within her exemption at her death (though the exemption at her death may be less than $15M if the OBBBA-2025 exemption sunsets or is reduced by 2035+ legislative action). The deceased spouse's $15M GST exemption is wasted.

Neither option is optimal. Option A wastes $5M of GST-exempt trust value that would have been sheltered by the surviving spouse's own exemption. Option B wastes the entire $15M of the deceased spouse's exemption. The optimal outcome — sheltering the full $20M with a mix of both spouses' exemptions — requires multi-trust drafting.

The multi-trust drafting workaround

The multi-trust workaround splits the deceased spouse's QTIP property into two (or more) separate QTIP trusts at the drafting stage — typically funded at the moment of the deceased spouse's death by pecuniary or fractional formula clauses in the will or revocable trust. The executor makes the reverse-QTIP election on one trust (matched to the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption) and the default treatment on the other (matched to the surviving spouse's later GST exemption).[10]

The two-QTIP-trust drafting pattern

A well-drafted estate plan for a high-net-worth couple with grandchild remaindermen typically includes:

  1. Marital QTIP Trust A (the "GST-exempt QTIP trust") — funded at the deceased spouse's death with a pecuniary amount equal to the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption ($15M for 2026 decedents with no lifetime skip gifts). The executor makes both the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election and the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on this trust. GST exemption is allocated to produce a 0% inclusion ratio.
  2. Marital QTIP Trust B (the "GST-non-exempt QTIP trust") — funded at the deceased spouse's death with the balance of the QTIP-eligible property. The executor makes the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election but not the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election. The surviving spouse becomes the GST transferor at her later death.

Both trusts have identical dispositive provisions during the surviving spouse's lifetime (both distribute annually to her the greater of trust-accounting income or actual receipts, per Rev. Rul. 2000-2). At her death, both trusts distribute the remainder to the couple's grandchildren. The only difference is the identity of the GST transferor and the applicable GST exemption. The two-trust structure preserves both spouses' $15M exemptions and doubles the GST-exempt capacity from $15M to $30M for the 2026 baseline.

Formula clause drafting

The typical drafting mechanic uses a pecuniary or fractional formula clause that funds Trust A with an amount matched to the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption at death. Two common formulations:

  1. Pecuniary formula. "The Marital QTIP Trust A shall be funded with a pecuniary amount equal to the maximum amount of the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption at his death, computed after taking into account any lifetime skip gifts and any GST exemption allocated to lifetime transfers to non-QTIP trusts. The Marital QTIP Trust B shall be funded with the balance of the QTIP-eligible property." This formulation guarantees that Trust A is exactly matched to the remaining GST exemption at death and is the most common approach.
  2. Fractional formula. "The Marital QTIP Trust A shall be funded with a fractional share of the QTIP-eligible property equal to the ratio of the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption at his death to the total QTIP-eligible property." This formulation shares gain and loss between the trusts pro rata during the funding period, which can be preferable in a volatile-market environment.

Either formulation must be paired with tax-apportionment provisions that direct how any estate tax attributable to the QTIP property is paid (typically from Trust B rather than Trust A, to preserve Trust A's 0% inclusion ratio). Practitioners should also consider funding-mechanic provisions that address which assets fund Trust A versus Trust B — for a QTIP-held inherited IRA fact pattern, the IRA should typically fund the GST-exempt Trust A if the grandchild-remainderman distribution is expected during the 10-year post-death window, or fund the non-GST-exempt Trust B if other liquid assets are available to fund Trust A more cost-effectively.

Coordination with the DSUE portability election

The DSUE portability election under IRC §2010(c)(5) is a separate election that transfers the deceased spouse's unused estate and gift tax exemption to the surviving spouse for use during her lifetime or at her death.[11] The DSUE election is made on the deceased spouse's Form 706 by checking a box on the return and filing timely. Once made, the surviving spouse's "applicable exclusion amount" for estate and gift tax purposes is increased by the DSUE amount (the deceased spouse's remaining exemption at death).

The DSUE election does NOT extend to the GST exemption. IRC §2631 governs the GST exemption and does not contain a portability provision. The deceased spouse's unused GST exemption at death cannot be transferred to the surviving spouse under any statutory or regulatory mechanism. This asymmetry — estate exemption is portable, GST exemption is not — is the specific reason the reverse-QTIP election under §2652(a)(3) is not redundant with the DSUE election. Rev. Proc. 2022-32 five-year DSUE relief

The IRS has provided a specific extended-relief window for late DSUE elections in Rev. Proc. 2022-32, which grants automatic extensions for up to five years after the deceased spouse's death to file a Form 706 solely to elect DSUE portability (provided no estate tax was due on the deceased spouse's estate). Rev. Proc. 2022-32 does NOT extend to the reverse-QTIP election — a late reverse-QTIP election still requires a private letter ruling under §9100 relief.[13] This asymmetry is another reason to distinguish the two elections at the front-end drafting stage and to plan the Form 706 filing to capture both.

Application to a QTIP-held inherited IRA

The reverse-QTIP election is particularly consequential for a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA. Three specific interactions matter.

The SECURE Act 10-year rule accelerates the timeline

Under IRC §401(a)(9)(H) as enacted in the SECURE Act (Pub. L. 116-94 §401, December 20, 2019) and interpreted in the final regulations at TD 10001 (89 Fed. Reg. 58886, July 19, 2024), a QTIP trust holding an inherited IRA is a see-through trust subject to the 10-year rule.[14] The entire IRA must be distributed from the QTIP trust within ten years of the account owner's death. If the account owner died before the required beginning date (RBD) for RMDs (generally age 73 under SECURE 2.0 §107), the 10-year rule can operate without any interim annual RMD obligation. If the account owner died after the RBD, annual RMDs are required during the 10-year window under the "at least as rapidly" rule of §1.401(a)(9)-5.

The practical implication is that the IRA is fully distributed from the QTIP trust within ten years of the deceased spouse's death — often during the surviving spouse's lifetime. Distributions to the surviving spouse pass through the QTIP trust under Rev. Rul. 2000-2 mechanics (greater of trust-accounting income or actual IRA receipt).[15] If the surviving spouse outlives the 10-year window, all of the IRA proceeds have been distributed to her before her death, and the remaining trust corpus at her death consists of reinvested non-IRA assets. If the surviving spouse dies within the 10-year window, some or all of the IRA proceeds remain in the QTIP trust at her death, and the residual balance passes to the grandchild remaindermen under the trust's dispositive provisions.

The grandchild-remainderman GST exposure

Whether the remaining trust corpus at the surviving spouse's death consists of undistributed IRA proceeds or reinvested non-IRA assets, the transfer to grandchild remaindermen is a taxable termination under IRC §2612(a) subject to GST tax at 40% on the amount exceeding the applicable GST exemption. Without a reverse-QTIP election, the surviving spouse is the GST transferor and only her remaining exemption applies. With a reverse-QTIP election, the deceased spouse's exemption applies (or the pre-computed inclusion ratio from his Form 706 election, if the exemption was insufficient).

For a couple with a $10 million inherited IRA passing to a QTIP trust with grandchild remaindermen, the reverse-QTIP election can save $4 million in GST tax (the difference between 40% GST on the full $10M without exemption sheltering versus 0% GST with the deceased spouse's $15M exemption fully sheltering the transfer). This is not a hypothetical: it is the specific quantitative reason the August 20 §2056(b)(7) piece flagged the reverse-QTIP omission as the highest-value Form 706 preparation error in surviving-spouse review.

Coordination with the QTIP see-through trust requirements

For the QTIP trust to be a see-through trust under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4 (and to be subject to the 10-year rule rather than the shorter 5-year non-designated-beneficiary rule), the trust must satisfy four requirements: (a) valid under state law, (b) irrevocable at the account owner's death, (c) identifiable beneficiaries from the trust instrument, and (d) proper documentation delivery to the plan administrator by October 31 of the year following death.[16]

The multi-trust drafting workaround (two QTIP trusts — GST-exempt Trust A and GST-non-exempt Trust B) does not affect the see-through analysis: each trust is separately analyzed for see-through compliance, and each trust must independently satisfy the four requirements. Practitioners should ensure that both trusts include identical-form beneficiary designations, that both trusts are irrevocable at death, and that the trustee delivers the required documentation for both trusts to the IRA custodian by the October 31 deadline. The IRA custodian's administration typically accommodates a split beneficiary designation naming both trusts by percentage, though some custodians impose paperwork friction and the funding formula clauses in the will or revocable trust must dovetail with the custodian's beneficiary-designation form.

State-law GST tax considerations

As of 2026, only a small number of jurisdictions impose a separate state-level GST tax. Connecticut is the primary state with a distinct state-level GST tax, with an exemption tracked to the federal exemption ($13.99M as of 2025, increased to $15M for 2026 under coupled inflation adjustments per Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-644).[17] Most decoupled-estate-tax states (Illinois, Massachusetts, New York, Oregon, Washington, Maryland, Vermont, Rhode Island, Minnesota, Maine, Hawaii, District of Columbia) impose state estate tax but do not impose a separate state GST tax on generation-skipping transfers.

For a decedent domiciled in a state without a separate GST tax, the reverse-QTIP analysis is purely federal — the deceased spouse's federal Form 706 election governs, and no parallel state-level election is required. For a decedent domiciled in Connecticut (the only state with a substantive independent state GST tax as of 2026), the Connecticut estate tax return should mirror the federal reverse-QTIP election, and the deceased spouse's state GST exemption should be allocated in parallel with the federal allocation. Practitioners representing Connecticut decedents should confirm the state-return mechanics against the Connecticut Department of Revenue Services' current CT-706 instructions and the Connecticut Uniform Trust Code overlay.

Six recurring reverse-QTIP election mistakes

The following mistakes account for the vast majority of reverse-QTIP election failures that reach practitioner attention.

Mistake 1 — Failing to make the reverse-QTIP election on a QTIP trust with grandchild remaindermen. This is an unforced error that wastes the deceased spouse's $15 million GST exemption forever. It typically occurs when the Form 706 preparer treats the return as a routine estate-tax filing without a GST-planning review. Prevention: every Form 706 for a decedent whose QTIP-eligible property is destined for grandchild remaindermen should include a specific GST-planning memorandum documenting the reverse-QTIP analysis and either the election or the specific reasons the election was declined. The memorandum becomes the record if the surviving spouse's later Form 706 shows a wasted exemption.

Mistake 2 — Making the election on a single mixed QTIP trust when multi-trust drafting would have allowed partial reverse-QTIP treatment. The all-or-nothing rule of Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(a) forces the executor into a binary choice for a single trust. Prevention: at the front-end drafting stage, split the QTIP-eligible property into two (or more) separate QTIP trusts matched to the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption. This is standard practice for high-net-worth couples above the $15M-per-person threshold.

Mistake 3 — Failing to coordinate the reverse-QTIP election with the surviving spouse's own GST exemption planning. When the deceased spouse's executor makes the reverse-QTIP election, the surviving spouse retains her own $15M GST exemption for use on her own lifetime skip gifts and her own residual trust remainders. Poor coordination can result in duplicative allocation (the surviving spouse allocates exemption to a trust already sheltered by the reverse-QTIP election) or under-allocation (the surviving spouse fails to shelter her own trust remainders because she believes the deceased spouse's election already covered them). Prevention: maintain a couple-level GST exemption tracking spreadsheet updated at each Form 709 and Form 706 filing, and cross-reference every allocation against both spouses' remaining exemption balances.

Mistake 4 — Missing the Form 706 deadline for the reverse-QTIP election. The election must be made on the deceased spouse's Form 706, filed nine months after death or fifteen months with a §6081 extension. Missing the deadline forces reliance on §9100 relief through a private letter ruling, which is expensive ($38,000 user fee plus attorney time) and time-consuming (six to nine months typical processing). Prevention: calendar the Form 706 deadline immediately upon the deceased spouse's death; file the extension request (Form 4768) as a matter of course to preserve the 15-month window; identify all QTIP-eligible property and its remainder beneficiaries within the first sixty days after death.

Mistake 5 — Treating the reverse-QTIP election as a technical checkbox rather than a strategic drafting decision at the front end of the estate plan. The executor's ability to make an optimal reverse-QTIP election depends on the front-end drafting: whether the QTIP trust exists at all, whether multi-trust drafting was used, whether the funding formulas match the exemption thresholds, and whether the grandchild-remainderman provisions are properly drafted. Prevention: every estate plan for a couple with net worth above $10 million should include a specific GST-planning module addressing the reverse-QTIP mechanic and the multi-trust structure. Waiting until the deceased spouse's Form 706 filing to think about GST planning is generally too late.

Mistake 6 — For a QTIP-held inherited IRA, failing to model the SECURE Act 10-year rule acceleration against the deceased spouse's GST exemption capacity. The 10-year rule forces distribution of the entire IRA within ten years, meaning the grandchild-remainderman exposure crystallizes on an accelerated timeline. The reverse-QTIP allocation should be sized against the expected trust corpus at the surviving spouse's death (which may be considerably less than the IRA face value if the surviving spouse consumes most of the distributions during her lifetime) rather than against the face value of the IRA at the deceased spouse's death. Prevention: engage a retirement-plan-experienced Form 706 preparer for any QTIP-plus-IRA fact pattern, and model the expected trust corpus at multiple age scenarios for the surviving spouse before making the reverse-QTIP allocation.

Worked case study: $12 million inherited IRA

Consider David and Elena, a first-marriage couple in their late 60s with three adult children (Jordan, Priya, and Kai) and eight grandchildren aged 4 to 18. David (age 71) dies in March 2026, survived by Elena (age 68). David's largest asset is a $12,000,000 traditional IRA held at Vanguard. David's estate plan includes a well-drafted revocable trust that funds two separate QTIP trusts at his death using pecuniary formula clauses:

  • Marital QTIP Trust A ("the GST-exempt QTIP") — funded with a pecuniary amount equal to David's remaining GST exemption at death ($15 million per Notice 2025-67). Given David's other assets ($3M house, $2M taxable brokerage, $500K cash) totaling $5.5M outside the IRA, the QTIP-eligible IRA property is $12M, all of which falls within David's $15M exemption. Trust A is funded with the full $12M IRA.
  • Marital QTIP Trust B ("the GST-non-exempt QTIP") — funded with the balance of the QTIP-eligible property. In this fact pattern, Trust B is funded with the $2M taxable brokerage (not the residence, which is disposed of separately outside the QTIP structure).

Elena is the life-income beneficiary of both trusts and receives annually the greater of trust-accounting income or actual IRA receipts (Rev. Rul. 2000-2). The eight grandchildren are the ultimate remainder beneficiaries of both trusts, per stirpes through the three children.

David's Form 706 election choices

David's executor faces three concurrent elections on David's Form 706:

  1. §2056(b)(7) QTIP election. Elect QTIP treatment on both Trust A and Trust B. Marital deduction of $14M ($12M + $2M) claimed. Zero estate tax due (well below the $15M exemption after the $5.5M non-QTIP-eligible net estate at market value).
  2. §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Trust A only. Elect reverse-QTIP on Trust A. Allocate David's $15M GST exemption to Trust A: $12M produces a 0% inclusion ratio on Trust A. The remaining $3M of David's GST exemption is allocated to lifetime skip gifts or forfeited at his death (as David made no lifetime skip gifts, the $3M is effectively wasted). Do not make the reverse-QTIP election on Trust B — Elena will be the GST transferor for Trust B at her later death.
  3. IRC §2010(c)(5) DSUE portability election. Elect portability to preserve David's unused estate exemption for Elena's later use. David used $0 of estate exemption at his death (marital deduction fully offset the QTIP-elected property). DSUE amount transferred to Elena is $15M − $5.5M (non-QTIP property offsetting his own exemption if not covered by marital deduction... actually the $5.5M non-QTIP passed under the will to Elena directly qualifying for marital deduction too). Effective DSUE = $15M.

Recommended alternative for this fact pattern

For David and Elena's fact pattern, the executor should:

  1. Make the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on both Trust A and Trust B.
  2. Make the §2652(a)(3) reverse-QTIP election on Trust A only, allocating $12M of David's $15M GST exemption to Trust A (producing a 0% inclusion ratio).
  3. Make the DSUE portability election to preserve the $15M of David's unused estate exemption for Elena's later use.
  4. Coordinate with Vanguard as IRA custodian to ensure the IRA beneficiary designation names Trust A specifically (not Elena directly), that Trust A satisfies see-through requirements under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4, and that the trustee delivers the required documentation to Vanguard by October 31, 2027 (October 31 of the year following David's death).
  5. Set up a couple-level GST exemption tracking spreadsheet showing David's $15M initial exemption, $12M allocated to Trust A (leaving $3M unused at death, which is forfeited under §2632(e)), Elena's $15M exemption still fully available, and the DSUE $15M available to Elena for estate/gift purposes only.

Pre-election action checklist

The 8-item pre-reverse-QTIP-election checklist for a decedent-QTIP-plus-inherited-IRA fact pattern

Complete each item before filing the deceased spouse's Form 706 for any fact pattern involving QTIP-eligible property with skip-person remaindermen. Retain the completed checklist and the estate-planning attorney's contemporaneous work notes for the statute of limitations plus a reasonable margin.

  1. Identify all QTIP-eligible property and its ultimate remainder beneficiaries. Review the deceased spouse's will, revocable trust, IRA beneficiary designations, life insurance beneficiary designations, and any other governing instruments to determine what property may be the subject of a §2056(b)(7) QTIP election and who takes the property when the surviving spouse dies. Flag any property with grandchild, great-grandchild, or unrelated-younger-person (below the surviving spouse's own generation) remaindermen — that is the property for which the reverse-QTIP election matters.
  2. Compute the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption at death. Start with the applicable $15M exemption for 2026 decedents (Notice 2025-67). Subtract all GST exemption allocated to lifetime skip gifts on prior Form 709 filings. Subtract any GST exemption allocated to non-QTIP trusts on the current Form 706. The residual is the amount available for allocation to the reverse-QTIP trust.
  3. Confirm the multi-trust structure at the drafting level. If the estate plan uses a single QTIP trust for all QTIP-eligible property, note that the all-or-nothing rule of Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(a) will force a binary choice on the reverse-QTIP election. If the plan uses two or more separate QTIP trusts (typically Trust A funded to the exemption amount and Trust B funded with the balance), the executor has flexibility to make the election on some trusts and not others. If the plan uses a single trust and the trust's value exceeds the deceased spouse's remaining exemption, consider whether pre-filing division of the trust is available under state law (some UTC states permit trustee-directed division without court approval; others require a court order).
  4. Coordinate with the DSUE portability election. DSUE portability preserves the deceased spouse's estate exemption for the surviving spouse's use; reverse-QTIP preserves the deceased spouse's GST exemption for QTIP property. Both elections should be made on the same Form 706. Confirm that the Form 706 preparer has budgeted for the additional Schedule R-1 and Schedule R Part 2 work required for the reverse-QTIP election.
  5. Confirm see-through trust compliance for any QTIP trust holding an IRA. The QTIP trust must satisfy the four requirements of Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4 to be a see-through trust. The trustee must deliver the trust instrument or a certified summary to the IRA custodian by October 31 of the year following death. Failure to comply forces the IRA into the 5-year non-designated-beneficiary rule, accelerating the GST-planning timeline dramatically.
  6. Model the expected trust corpus at multiple surviving-spouse-death scenarios. For a QTIP-held inherited IRA subject to the 10-year rule, the trust corpus at the surviving spouse's later death depends heavily on when she dies and how much she consumes. Model at least three scenarios: (a) surviving spouse dies within the 10-year window (much of the IRA still in trust), (b) surviving spouse dies shortly after the 10-year window (IRA fully distributed but reinvested proceeds still substantial), and (c) surviving spouse dies decades after the 10-year window (much of the reinvested proceeds consumed by lifetime expenses).
  7. Execute the reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R-1 of the Form 706. The election is signaled by filing Schedule R-1 identifying the specific QTIP trust or property to which the election applies. Allocate the deceased spouse's GST exemption on Schedule R Part 2 in the amount computed to produce the desired inclusion ratio (typically 0%). File Form 706 within the nine-month deadline (or fifteen months with a §6081 extension).
  8. Document the election decision in a GST-planning memorandum. Prepare a written memorandum documenting: (a) the identification of QTIP-eligible property and its remainder beneficiaries, (b) the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption computation, (c) the choice to make (or decline) the reverse-QTIP election on each trust, (d) the allocation of GST exemption across trusts, (e) the resulting inclusion ratio for each trust, and (f) the coordination with the DSUE portability election. The memorandum becomes the practitioner's file record if the election is later challenged or if a surviving-spouse Form 706 review discovers a wasted exemption.

Frequently asked questions about IRC §2652(a)(3) and inherited IRAs

What is IRC §2652(a)(3) and why does it matter for a QTIP-held inherited IRA?

IRC §2652(a)(3) is a one-time election on the deceased spouse's Form 706 that treats the deceased spouse — not the surviving spouse — as the transferor of QTIP-elected property for federal GST tax purposes. Without the election, IRC §2652(a)(1) makes the surviving spouse the GST transferor because the QTIP property is included in her gross estate under IRC §2044 at her later death. The default rule wastes the deceased spouse's $15 million GST exemption entirely. For a QTIP-held inherited IRA with grandchild remaindermen, the reverse-QTIP election is often the difference between $0 and multiple millions of dollars in GST tax at the ultimate distribution.

How does IRC §2652(a)(1) create the default-transferor trap?

IRC §2652(a)(1) defines the transferor for GST purposes as the individual most recently subject to estate or gift tax on the property. For QTIP property, the deceased spouse claimed a marital deduction under §2056(b)(7), so no estate tax was imposed on him. When the property is later included in the surviving spouse's gross estate under IRC §2044, she becomes the most-recently-taxed individual and therefore the default GST transferor. Only her $15M GST exemption applies — the deceased spouse's is wasted.

When and how is the reverse-QTIP election made?

The election is made on the deceased spouse's Form 706, filed nine months after death (or fifteen months with a §6081 extension). The mechanic uses Schedule R-1 to signal the election, Schedule R Part 2 to allocate the deceased spouse's GST exemption, and coordination with the §2056(b)(7) QTIP election on Schedule M. The election is irrevocable once made and cannot be revoked, modified, or partially undone on any subsequent return.

What is the all-or-nothing rule under Treas. Reg. §26.2652-2(a)?

The reverse-QTIP election must apply to ALL of the property in a single QTIP trust — the executor cannot elect reverse-QTIP treatment for part of a trust's property and default treatment for the balance. For a $12M trust and $10M of remaining GST exemption, the choice is binary: full reverse-QTIP with a partial inclusion ratio (0.17), or full default treatment with the surviving spouse as later transferor. The rule is administrative-simplicity motivated and forces multi-trust drafting for high-net-worth planning.

What is the multi-trust drafting workaround?

Split the QTIP-eligible property into two (or more) separate QTIP trusts at drafting — typically Trust A funded to the deceased spouse's remaining GST exemption and Trust B funded with the balance. The executor makes the reverse-QTIP election on Trust A (producing a 0% inclusion ratio) and default treatment on Trust B (surviving spouse becomes later GST transferor). Both spouses' $15M exemptions apply — doubling effective GST capacity from $15M to $30M for the 2026 baseline. Multi-trust drafting is standard for high-net-worth couples above $15M-per-person.

How does the reverse-QTIP election interact with the DSUE portability election?

DSUE portability under §2010(c)(5) preserves the deceased spouse's ESTATE tax exemption for the surviving spouse's later use on non-QTIP property. Reverse-QTIP under §2652(a)(3) preserves the deceased spouse's GST exemption for QTIP property. The two elections fill different gaps — the GST exemption is NOT portable under §2631. Make BOTH elections on the deceased spouse's Form 706 for a couple with grandchild remaindermen.

How does the reverse-QTIP election apply to a QTIP-held inherited IRA?

Under the SECURE Act 10-year rule (IRC §401(a)(9)(H)), the entire inherited IRA must be distributed from the QTIP trust within ten years of the account owner's death. If the surviving spouse dies with residual IRA proceeds in the trust — either undistributed IRA balance or reinvested proceeds — the transfer to grandchild remaindermen is a taxable termination under §2612(a) at 40% GST rate. With the reverse-QTIP election, the deceased spouse's $15M GST exemption applies. Without it, only the surviving spouse's remaining exemption is available.

Can the reverse-QTIP election be made after the Form 706 due date?

Only through §9100 relief under a private letter ruling. Treas. Reg. §§301.9100-1 through 301.9100-3 provide a framework for granting extended time to make an inadvertently omitted election, but the process is expensive ($38,000 user fee per Rev. Proc. 2025-1) and slow (six to nine months). Rev. Proc. 2022-32's five-year automatic-relief window for DSUE portability does NOT extend to the reverse-QTIP election. File on time.

How does the reverse-QTIP election interact with state GST tax?

Connecticut is the primary state with a separate state-level GST tax (Conn. Gen. Stat. §12-644), and the Connecticut estate tax return should mirror the federal reverse-QTIP election. Most decoupled-estate-tax states (NY, IL, MA, OR, WA, MD, DC, VT, RI, MN, ME, HI) impose state estate tax but do not impose a separate state GST tax, so no state parallel election is required. Practitioners representing Connecticut decedents should confirm the CT-706 mechanics against the DRS's current instructions.

What are the most common reverse-QTIP election mistakes?

The most common: (1) failing to make the election at all when grandchild remaindermen are named — an unforced error wasting $15M in GST exemption; (2) making the election on a single mixed QTIP trust rather than using multi-trust drafting; (3) failing to coordinate with the surviving spouse's own GST planning; (4) missing the Form 706 deadline; (5) treating the election as a technical checkbox rather than a strategic drafting decision; and (6) for a QTIP-held IRA, failing to model the SECURE Act 10-year rule acceleration against the exemption capacity.
