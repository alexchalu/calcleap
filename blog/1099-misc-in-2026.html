1099-MISC in 2026: Rents, Royalties, Attorney Gross Proceeds, and What Still Goes on the Form After 1099-NEC Took Over Nonemployee Comp

1099-MISC has quietly become the most misunderstood information return on the IRS's shelf. Nonemployee compensation moved to Form 1099-NEC in 2020. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 lifted the general §6041 reporting threshold from $600 to $2,000 for tax year 2026, but did nothing to the $10 royalty rule under §6050N and nothing to the $600 attorney gross-proceeds rule under §6045(f). What is left is a form with fifteen boxes, four different statutory backbones, three different threshold regimes, and a growing gap between what payers assume and what the payee actually owes. This is the complete 2026 walkthrough — every box, every threshold, every penalty, and three worked case studies for the payer roles that see 1099-MISC most: landlords, authors, and personal-injury attorneys.

By CalcLeap Editorial Published July 2, 2026 Reviewed against IRS Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC (2026 revision), IRC §6041 / §6041A / §6045(f) / §6050N, and Pub. L. 119-21 §70431–§70433 ~28 min read

If you spent the last five years quietly ignoring the letter "MISC" on Form 1099, you were not alone. From 2020 through 2025 most tax practitioners treated 1099-MISC as an oddball residual — the form that catches whatever didn't fit on 1099-NEC, 1099-K, 1099-INT, 1099-DIV, or 1099-B. Then two things happened at once. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 rewrote the §6041 threshold from $600 to $2,000, effective for tax year 20261. And a wave of gig-economy, side-hustle, rental-property, and settlement-driven payments pushed millions of people into the 1099-MISC universe for the first time. The form suddenly matters again — and the rules that govern it are not what most payers, or most payees, remember from the pre-2020 era.

This guide answers the questions that show up in every tax preparer's inbox from November through April: which box does what, which threshold applies in 2026, which OBBBA changes matter and which ones do not, how to tell a legal fee from a settlement pass-through, when a landlord must issue a 1099 versus when the tenant issues one to the landlord, and what to do when the amount on the form is wrong. It cross-links to our companion guides on 1099-K vs 1099-NEC, W-9 vs W-4, the 1099 self-employment survival guide, and estimated quarterly taxes in 2026, and to our 1099 tax calculator which reconciles all three of the 1099 forms most freelancers, landlords, and side-hustlers receive.

What Form 1099-MISC is today

Form 1099-MISC is the Internal Revenue Service's general-purpose information return for miscellaneous payments made in the course of a trade or business that are not wages, not nonemployee service compensation, not credit-card or third-party settlement transactions, and not interest, dividends, or securities proceeds2. It is one of the oldest information returns on the IRS's roster — the 1099 series traces to the Revenue Act of 1918, which first required businesses to report payments to non-employees, and 1099-MISC in its recognizable modern form dates to the 1980s. For most of that history, the form's Box 7 held "Nonemployee compensation" — the single biggest number on the form and the source of the classic "1099 versus W-2" distinction that defined the American gig economy.

Then, for tax year 2020, the IRS split Box 7 off onto its own dedicated form — Form 1099-NEC — resurrecting a form last used in 19823. The stated rationale in the preamble to T.D. 9903 was that Congress's 2015 PATH Act had accelerated the 1099-NEC filing deadline to January 31 while leaving most other 1099-MISC boxes on the older February 28 / March 31 schedule, and the IRS's Combined Federal/State Filing program could not reliably handle a single form with two different deadlines. The split was permanent. Since 2020, "nonemployee compensation" — a plumber's invoice, a graphic designer's project fee, a delivery-app driver's platform earnings above threshold — belongs exclusively on 1099-NEC. Everything else that a business pays out and needs to information-report goes on 1099-MISC.

"Everything else" is a large bucket. It includes rents paid to landlords, royalties paid to authors and mineral-rights owners, prizes and awards, damages from lawsuits that are not for physical injury, medical and health-care payments to non-corporate providers (with an important exception for corporate providers, discussed below), attorney gross proceeds passing through a client-trust account, crop insurance proceeds, fishing-boat proceeds, direct-sales checkboxes for consumer-product distributors, and a small set of specialty items — §409A deferrals, excess golden parachute payments, nonqualified deferred compensation, and a FATCA marker for foreign-account holders.

The core distinction, in one sentence

1099-NEC = "I paid this person for services." 1099-MISC = "I paid this person for something else that the IRS wants to know about." Everything downstream — thresholds, deadlines, penalty exposure, backup-withholding rules — flows from that distinction.

The fifteen boxes and what each one reports

The 2026 Form 1099-MISC has fifteen numbered boxes for financial data, plus a payer-information block and a recipient-information block at the top of the form. Each box has its own statutory basis, its own reporting threshold, its own recipient-copy deadline in some cases, and its own audit trail through the IRS's automated Computer Paragraph 2000 (CP2000) matching program4. The IRS's 2026 Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC is the controlling operational document; the summary below tracks it box by box, incorporating the OBBBA §70433 threshold changes effective for payments made in calendar year 2026.

Four statutory backbones show up in the "basis" column: §6041 (the general information-return rule for miscellaneous income of $2,000-or-more post-OBBBA), §6050N (a 1988 addition specifically for royalties at $10), §6045(f) (a 1997 addition for attorney payments at $600 that lawyers and their payers cannot afford to miss), and §3406 (the backup-withholding rule that flows through Box 4). Getting the right box means knowing which statute owns the payment.

Why the OBBBA threshold change didn't lift every box

Congress did not rewrite every information-return statute in the OBBBA. §70433 amended IRC §6041(a) (general miscellaneous income), which pulled Boxes 1, 3, 5 (cash-only), 6, and 9 to the new $2,000 floor. Congress separately amended IRC §6041A(a) — nonemployee compensation — in the same section to raise the 1099-NEC threshold from $600 to $2,000. But Congress left IRC §6050N (royalties, at $10) alone, and Congress left IRC §6045(f) (attorney gross proceeds, at $600) alone. A payer who assumes "everything is $2,000 now" will silently under-report royalties and attorney settlements, both of which carry §6721 + §6722 penalty exposure exceeding $1,300 per missed form for the current filing year5.

Box 10: the $600 attorney gross-proceeds rule that OBBBA left alone

The single biggest 1099-MISC trap in 2026 is Box 10 — gross proceeds paid to an attorney under IRC §6045(f). It carries the highest penalty exposure per unit of dollar underpaid on the form, and it interacts with §6041A (Box 1 of 1099-NEC, legal fees for services) in a way that trips even experienced insurance-carrier claims departments.

The mechanics are these. Whenever a payer in the course of a trade or business — most commonly a defendant, an insurance carrier, or a defendant's insurer — pays $600 or more to an attorney in connection with legal services, and the payment is not solely for the attorney's own services (which would go on 1099-NEC), the payer must issue a 1099-MISC to the attorney with the entire amount in Box 106. This is true even when the payer knows that the attorney will remit most of the money to the client — the reporting is on gross, not on net, and the $600 threshold is a floor, not a de minimis.

The clearest example is a personal-injury settlement. A homeowner's insurance carrier settles a slip-and-fall claim for $100,000 payable to "Roberts, Patel & Chen LLP, attorneys for the plaintiff." The law firm deposits the $100,000 into its client trust account (an IOLTA account in most jurisdictions), retains a 33.33% contingent fee ($33,333) and reimbursement of $4,200 in advanced costs, and disburses the remaining $62,467 to the plaintiff. From a §6045(f) perspective, the carrier's obligation is straightforward: issue the law firm a 1099-MISC with $100,000 in Box 10 (gross proceeds) and issue the same law firm a separate 1099-NEC with the $33,333 fee in Box 1 (legal services). The plaintiff never gets a 1099 from the carrier — the payment ran through the attorney, and §6045(f) makes the attorney the reportable recipient. If the settlement is entirely for personal physical injury or physical sickness, the plaintiff's $62,467 share is excluded from gross income under IRC §104(a)(2) and never lands on the plaintiff's Form 1040; the attorney's $33,333 fee is separate service income.

The most common Box 10 payer error

Insurance-carrier claims systems often "batch" year-end 1099 reporting and use a single default box for all payments to the same TIN. If the batch job routes a $100,000 personal-injury settlement into Box 3 ("Other income") instead of Box 10 ("Gross proceeds paid to an attorney"), the automated IRS CP2000 program treats the entire $100,000 as taxable miscellaneous income to the attorney's law firm. The firm then has to prove the money passed through the trust account — a process that typically requires the firm to produce IOLTA reconciliations, settlement statements, and the client's Form 8275 disclosure of the §104(a)(2) exclusion.

Why §6045(f) was kept at $600 in OBBBA

The OBBBA's Congressional Joint Committee on Taxation description of §70431–§70433 (published as JCX-49-25 in the days after enactment) explains that the $600 floor for §6045(f) attorney payments was preserved because attorney-mediated payments are one of the primary mechanisms through which underreported income enters the tax system — and because Congress did not want to weaken the IRS's ability to trace settlement payments that carry the highest risk of characterization disputes7. In dollars-and-cents terms, a $1,500 attorney-mediated payment can carry more downstream audit risk than a $50,000 W-2 wage payment because the attorney's client may be tempted to exclude the entire payment under an aggressive reading of §104(a)(2). Keeping the reporting floor at $600 preserves the trigger for automated matching.

Rents (Box 1): who reports what, and when the roles flip

Box 1 is the second-most-common 1099-MISC box after Box 3, driven by the sheer volume of commercial-lease and equipment-lease payments that businesses make each year. The mechanics are surprisingly nuanced, and the OBBBA §70433 threshold increase has quietly changed which payments require a form.

The default rule

A trade or business that pays $2,000 or more in rents during the calendar year to a non-corporation landlord must issue a 1099-MISC with the aggregate rent in Box 18. "Rents" includes rent for real property (office, warehouse, retail space), rent for personal property (equipment, machinery, temporary storage containers), and pasture rentals. It does not include real-estate agents' commissions on the transaction (those are the tenant's, if any) or the landlord's payment of property tax if the landlord's practice is to invoice property tax separately.

The property-manager pass-through

A property manager who collects rent on behalf of a landlord is treated as the payer for §6041 reporting purposes under Treas. Reg. §1.6041-1(e). The property manager — not the tenant — issues the 1099-MISC to the landlord. This means the tenant of a professionally-managed commercial building generally never issues a 1099-MISC because the tenant is not the direct payer to the landlord; the tenant pays the property manager, and the property manager issues the form to the landlord downstream. The property manager's own management fee, if it is paid separately by the landlord, is a service payment and goes on 1099-NEC to the manager.

The corporation exception

Payments to a corporation are exempt from 1099-MISC reporting under Treas. Reg. §1.6041-3(p) — meaning a tenant does NOT have to issue a 1099-MISC to a landlord that is a C corporation or an S corporation. Because most professional real-estate holding entities are limited-liability partnerships (LLPs), limited-liability companies (LLCs) treated as partnerships, single-member LLCs treated as disregarded entities, or individual landlords holding title in their own name, the exception is narrower than tenants often assume. The safe check is the W-9 the landlord returned to the tenant: if Box 3 of the W-9 shows "C corporation" or "S corporation," no 1099 is required; if it shows "LLC" with tax classification "P" (partnership) or "D" (disregarded entity), the 1099 is required at $2,000 or more9.

The 2011 landlord-reporting reversal

The Small Business Jobs Act of 2010 briefly required rental-property owners who earned rental income to issue 1099s to their own vendors starting in 2011. Congress repealed the requirement in the Comprehensive 1099 Taxpayer Protection Act of 2011 (Pub. L. 112-9), reverting to the pre-2010 position that a rental-property owner is not automatically in a trade or business for §6041 purposes10. The current position — reaffirmed in the 2026 IRS instructions — is that a rental-property owner is required to issue 1099s to vendors only if the rental activity rises to a §162 trade or business under the Groetzinger framework. A single-property landlord with a full-time property manager, a passive investor in a syndicated apartment building, and a snowbird renting one Florida condo to seasonal tenants all typically fall below the §162 line and are not required to issue vendor 1099s. A hands-on landlord managing six units full-time will typically be above the §162 line and does have vendor-reporting obligations.

Royalties (Box 2): the $10 threshold that catches everyone

Box 2 sits inside 1099-MISC but does not follow the same rules as the other boxes. Its statutory basis is IRC §6050N, added by the Technical and Miscellaneous Revenue Act of 1988, which sets a $10 calendar-year reporting threshold — the lowest threshold on any 1099 series form. OBBBA §70433 did not touch §6050N, so the $10 threshold survived the 2026 overhaul unchanged11.

Publishers, streaming platforms, music-licensing collectives, patent-licensing entities, and oil-and-gas mineral-interest operators are the highest-volume Box 2 payers. Practical results:

  • An author with a self-published Kindle Direct Publishing title that earned $12 in 2026 royalties receives a 1099-MISC with $12 in Box 2. The recipient's tax obligation is unambiguous: the $12 is ordinary income reportable on Schedule E line 4 (or Schedule C line 1 if the author's writing activity rises to a §162 trade or business).
  • A songwriter with a single track that generated $46 of ASCAP performance royalties receives a 1099-MISC from ASCAP with $46 in Box 2. If the songwriter has separately received mechanical royalties from The Harry Fox Agency of $23, and streaming royalties from Spotify of $87, three separate 1099-MISCs arrive — each independently over the $10 floor and each required.
  • A small-percentage mineral-rights owner with $23 of quarterly oil-and-gas royalties (roughly $92 for the year) from an operator in the Permian Basin receives a 1099-MISC with $92 in Box 2. If the same owner has interests in three different wells operated by three different companies, three separate 1099-MISCs are required, each triggering at $10.
  • A pharmaceutical company that licenses a patent from an inventor for $8,400 during the calendar year issues a 1099-MISC with $8,400 in Box 2 — not Box 1 or Box 3. Patent royalties belong in Box 2 even when the licensee is a for-profit corporation.

Working-interest royalties vs. overriding royalties

Oil-and-gas taxation adds a wrinkle. A "royalty interest" is a right to a share of production revenue without responsibility for operating costs — Box 2 income. A "working interest" is a partnership-style share of both revenue and costs — that income flows through on Schedule K-1 as partnership income and is not 1099-MISC reportable. Owners of small overriding royalty interests almost always receive a 1099-MISC. Owners of working interests should not receive a 1099-MISC; if they do, the operator has misclassified the interest, and the owner should request a corrected form.

Box 3: prizes, awards, damages, and the catch-all bucket

Box 3 is the residual bucket — the box for taxable payments that don't fit any other box on 1099-MISC or 1099-NEC. Its statutory basis is IRC §6041 (like Boxes 1, 6, and 9), so the OBBBA §70433 threshold change lifted its floor from $600 to $2,000 for 2026 payments.

Common Box 3 payments in 2026:

  • Prizes and awards from sweepstakes, contests, and game shows. The classic Publishers Clearing House winner receives a 1099-MISC with the prize amount in Box 3. Nobel and Pulitzer prizes have their own §74(b) exclusion mechanics but are Box 3 if the exclusion does not apply.
  • Taxable damages from lawsuits that are not for physical injury or physical sickness under IRC §104(a)(2). A hostile-work-environment settlement, an emotional-distress award without an accompanying physical injury, and a punitive-damage component all land in Box 3.
  • Research-study participant payments at hospitals and universities — the $250 per-session stipend a clinical research volunteer receives, aggregated across the year, is Box 3 if the total is $2,000 or more.
  • Native American per-capita distributions from tribal gaming revenue under 25 U.S.C. §2710(b)(3), which the tribe reports on 1099-MISC to each enrolled member.
  • Certain retail incentive payments that are not for the recipient's services — a wholesale distributor's cash rebate to a retailer, a manufacturer's "spiff" payment to a retail employee (though those often go on 1099-NEC now that Box 7 has moved).
  • Termination payments to former self-employed insurance agents under IRC §1402(k) — where the payment is not for services rendered post-termination but is a residual on prior sales.
  • Sales incentive payments to non-employees where the recipient is not performing services (e.g., a customer-loyalty referral bonus that does not require the referrer to sell anything on the company's behalf).

Box 3 vs. Box 1 of 1099-NEC

The clearest test: did the recipient perform services in exchange for the payment? If yes, 1099-NEC Box 1. If no — if the payment is a prize, an award, a taxable damage, a rebate, a per-capita distribution, or a termination residual — it is Box 3 of 1099-MISC. Payments to attorneys are a separate universe with their own Box 10 gross-proceeds rule under §6045(f).

Box 6: medical payments and the corporate-payee exception

Box 6 reports medical and health-care payments — a business's payments to physicians, dentists, chiropractors, nurses, psychologists, physical therapists, veterinarians, medical laboratories, and hospitals for services rendered to employees or to the business's clients (in an insurance-carrier context). The 2026 threshold is $2,000, up from $600, matching the OBBBA §6041 lift.

The distinguishing feature of Box 6 is that payments to corporations ARE reportable. This is the exception written into Treas. Reg. §1.6041-3(p)(1) — the general "no reporting for corporate payees" rule expressly does not apply to Box 6 medical payments or to Box 10 attorney gross proceeds. A self-insured employer paying $6,500 to a professional-corporation medical group for an employee's covered claim issues a 1099-MISC to the medical group with $6,500 in Box 6, even though the medical group is a C corporation12.

Insurance carriers are the highest-volume Box 6 payers. When a covered claim is paid, the carrier issues 1099-MISCs to every provider that received $2,000 or more from the carrier during the calendar year. Providers who exclusively see insurance-covered patients often receive several dozen 1099-MISCs each January, one from each contracted carrier. The corporate-payee exception is what makes those forms mandatory; without it, most providers (who operate as professional corporations for state-law liability reasons) would slip out of the reporting net entirely.

What Box 6 does NOT report

Box 6 is medical-and-health-care payments to providers. It does not report:

  • Payments to a pharmacy for prescription drugs (a separate exception under Treas. Reg. §1.6041-3(k) for drug wholesalers).
  • Payments to a health-insurance carrier for premium coverage (those are insurance premiums, not medical payments).
  • Reimbursements to an employee for out-of-pocket medical costs (those are employee reimbursements, not third-party medical payments — accountable-plan reimbursements are not income).
  • Payments a health-savings-account or flexible-spending-account custodian makes to a provider on behalf of the employee (HSA/FSA distributions are separately reported on 1099-SA).

Deadlines and the §6721/§6722 penalty stack

Every information-return form comes with a two-headed penalty exposure. IRC §6721 penalizes the payer for failure to file the IRS copy on time. IRC §6722 penalizes the payer for failure to provide the recipient copy on time. The penalties stack — a single late 1099-MISC can trigger both §6721 and §6722 for the same underlying failure, doubling the exposure.

2026 penalty rates

The IRS publishes the annual inflation-adjusted §6721/§6722 amounts in the fall of each preceding year; the 2026 numbers were published in Rev. Proc. 2025-3213. The penalty schedule for 2026 payments (reporting due in January-March 2027) is:

§6722 mirrors these amounts for the recipient-copy failure. Because the two penalties stack, a business that fails to file a 1099-MISC entirely and has receipts above $5 million faces $340 (§6721) + $340 (§6722) = $680 per unfiled form. For a business that missed 200 forms, the exposure is $136,000 — with an intentional-disregard finding pushing that to $1,360+ per form and no cap.

The 10-form electronic-filing mandate

T.D. 9972, the IRS's 2023 final regulation, requires any payer filing 10 or more information returns (aggregated across ALL 1099, 1042-S, 1094, 1095, W-2, and 8027 forms) to file electronically14. The aggregation is across all forms, not per form type — so a business with 6 W-2s, 2 1099-NECs, and 3 1099-MISCs has 11 returns and is over the e-file threshold, even though no single form type exceeds 10. Paper filing when e-filing is required triggers §6721(a)(2)(A), treated as a failure-to-file penalty at the full $340-per-form rate.

Backup withholding under IRC §3406

Backup withholding is the safety net that catches payments the IRS's information-return system cannot track. When a payer requests a W-9 from a recipient and the recipient either fails to provide a valid taxpayer identification number (TIN), provides a TIN that fails the IRS's TIN Matching Program, is notified by the IRS that they are subject to backup withholding for underreported interest or dividends, or fails to certify the exempt-from-backup-withholding box on Part II of the W-9, the payer must withhold 24% of every reportable payment and remit that withholding to the IRS15. The withheld amount appears in Box 4 of the 1099-MISC.

The threshold interaction with OBBBA §70433 is important. Section 70433 raised the backup-withholding trigger threshold under §3406(b)(6)(A) from $600 to $2,000 for §6041 payments in parallel with the reporting threshold change. This means a payer who receives a valid W-9 from a landlord and pays that landlord $1,750 of rent during 2026 has no backup-withholding obligation — the payment is below both the reporting threshold (Box 1 at $2,000) and the backup-withholding threshold under §3406(b)(6). But a payer who receives an invalid or missing W-9 from a landlord and pays that landlord $1,750 of rent may still owe backup withholding if the aggregate for the year is projected to exceed $2,000 — the payer's obligation is anticipatory, based on the reasonably expected annual total. §6045(f) attorney gross proceeds and §6050N royalty payments retain their pre-OBBBA thresholds ($600 and $10, respectively) for backup-withholding purposes.

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Marta — commercial landlord, three tenants, all above threshold

Marta owns a small commercial retail strip in Austin, Texas. Title is held in an LLC that has elected disregarded-entity status. In 2026 she has three tenants:

  • A hair-salon chain (S corporation) paying $3,600 per month = $43,200 annual rent.
  • A local coffee roaster (LLC treated as a partnership) paying $2,800 per month = $33,600 annual rent.
  • A yoga studio (single-member LLC treated as a disregarded entity, owned by an individual) paying $1,900 per month = $22,800 annual rent.

All three tenants request Marta's W-9 at the start of the year. The W-9 correctly reports her as an individual (not a corporation) — her LLC's disregarded-entity status means the IRS looks through to her as the reportable party. Because Marta is not a corporation, all three tenants have a 1099-MISC obligation at the OBBBA-adjusted $2,000 threshold. Each tenant issues Marta a 1099-MISC in January 2027 with the annual rent total in Box 1. Marta receives three forms totaling $99,600 in Box 1 income.

Marta then reports the $99,600 on Schedule E of her Form 1040, as rental real-estate income. She subtracts depreciation, mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, and repairs, arriving at (say) $28,400 of net rental income. Because Marta's rental activity is passive (she has a property manager handling day-to-day operations, and she personally spends fewer than 250 hours per year on the property), her net rental income is taxed as ordinary income but is not subject to self-employment tax under IRC §1402(a)(1)'s rental-real-estate exception. If Marta had crossed the §162 trade-or-business line — spending 750+ hours personally managing the property, no manager, day-to-day operational involvement — she would have both self-employment tax exposure and an obligation to issue vendor 1099s to her own contractors. She does not cross that line.

Two additional Marta-side details matter. First, all three tenants have to issue their 1099-MISCs by January 31, 2027 — the recipient-copy deadline. If any tenant misses the deadline, that tenant faces a $60 §6721 + $60 §6722 = $120 per-form penalty. Second, Marta's LLC needs a W-9 correctly checked. If Marta had ticked "C Corporation" on Part I of the W-9 (which some LLC owners incorrectly do because their LLC uses "LLC" in its legal name), the tenants would have properly excluded her from 1099-MISC reporting — and Marta would have been at risk of failing to report the rental income entirely at the individual level.

Case 2: Devon — self-published author with royalties from four platforms

Devon is a self-published nonfiction author living in Denver, Colorado. In 2026 he has book titles selling on four platforms:

  • Amazon Kindle Direct Publishing: $8,470 in royalties across three ebooks and two paperbacks.
  • Apple Books: $214 in royalties.
  • Kobo Writing Life: $67 in royalties.
  • Draft2Digital (which aggregates Barnes & Noble Press, Google Play Books, and Smashwords): $1,142 in aggregated royalties, disbursed monthly.

Each of the four platforms is required to issue a 1099-MISC to Devon under IRC §6050N because each payment exceeds the $10 royalty threshold — a threshold that OBBBA §70433 did NOT change. Devon receives four separate 1099-MISCs, each with the platform's payment in Box 2 (Royalties). Total Box 2 income: $9,893.

Devon's tax obligation depends on whether his writing activity rises to a §162 trade or business under the Groetzinger framework. Devon has been publishing continuously for four years, spends approximately 20 hours per week on writing and self-promotion, and treats his author income as his primary occupation. Under Groetzinger factors — continuity, regularity, and profit motive — Devon's activity is clearly a §162 trade or business. He reports the $9,893 on Schedule C (Profit or Loss from Business), not Schedule E. That subjects the $9,893 to both federal income tax and to self-employment tax at 15.3% on the first $9,140 (92.35% of net) — approximately $1,398 of SE tax. It also lets Devon deduct home-office expenses under §280A, subscription costs to design software, book-cover-illustrator payments (which Devon issued 1099-NECs for at year end, above the OBBBA $2,000 threshold), and travel to industry conferences.

If Devon were a hobbyist author — writing sporadically as a side activity with no continuous profit motive — his royalty income would instead go on Schedule 1, Line 8l (income from renting personal property not for-profit), with no SE tax but also no business-expense deductions (hobby losses are non-deductible after TCJA). The 1099-MISCs arrive whether or not Devon is a §162 trade or business; what changes is where he reports the income on the return.

Case 3: Priya — personal-injury attorney with settlement proceeds passing through

Priya is the managing partner at a three-lawyer plaintiffs' firm in Miami, Florida. In 2026 her firm settled a slip-and-fall case for $180,000, payable by the defendant's homeowner's insurance carrier. Under the retainer, Priya's firm keeps 33.33% of the settlement ($60,000) as its contingent fee, is reimbursed $8,500 for costs advanced (expert-witness fees, court reporters, medical-records requests), and disburses the remaining $111,500 to the plaintiff client.

The insurance carrier's information-return obligations under §6045(f) are:

  • 1099-MISC Box 10 (Gross proceeds paid to an attorney): $180,000 to Priya's firm. The threshold is $600 under §6045(f), which OBBBA did NOT change. This is the mandatory piece and carries the highest reporting risk.
  • 1099-NEC Box 1 (Nonemployee compensation): $60,000 to Priya's firm. This reports the firm's attorney's fee for services rendered. The threshold under §6041A is $2,000 post-OBBBA — obviously met.

Priya's firm reports the $60,000 as ordinary partnership income on Form 1065, flowing through to the partners' K-1s and ultimately to Schedule E of each partner's Form 1040 (with the partner-level SE tax computed on Schedule SE via the K-1's Box 14A). The $111,500 that flowed through the trust account to the client is NOT the firm's income — it is a fiduciary receipt-and-disbursement, tracked in the firm's IOLTA ledger, and reconciled to the 1099-MISC Box 10 on the firm's return via Form 8275 disclosure. The plaintiff-client's $111,500 receipt is excludable from gross income under IRC §104(a)(2) because the underlying settlement was for personal physical injury. The plaintiff receives no 1099 from the insurance carrier for the $111,500 because §6045(f) makes the attorney (not the client) the reportable recipient.

If the carrier's automated year-end 1099 system mis-boxes the settlement — routing the $180,000 into Box 3 ("Other income") instead of Box 10 ("Gross proceeds paid to an attorney") — Priya's firm faces an automated CP2000 mismatch asserting $180,000 of taxable miscellaneous income. Priya's firm responds with an IOLTA trust-account reconciliation showing the pass-through, a request for a corrected 1099-MISC from the carrier, and (if the carrier refuses to correct) an attached explanation to the firm's Form 1065. This is a routine but time-consuming CP2000 defense that a fee-only tax practitioner in the plaintiff-attorney niche runs several times each year.

Six 1099-MISC mistakes that cost real money

1. Assuming all §6041 thresholds moved together

Payers who read the OBBBA one-line summary ("the $600 rule became $2,000") often assume every 1099-MISC threshold moved. Royalty payers who apply the $2,000 floor to Box 2 will silently under-report to the IRS on every recipient below $2,000 who is above $10 — a §6050N failure that carries the same $340-per-form penalty as any other §6721 failure. The largest exposure per payer is at royalty aggregators and streaming platforms, where the pre-OBBBA $600 threshold was already generating hundreds of thousands of 1099-MISCs annually to sub-$600 recipients.

2. Confusing 1099-NEC Box 1 with 1099-MISC Box 10 on attorney payments

The single biggest attorney-payment error. A payer who lumps the entire settlement into 1099-NEC Box 1 (legal services) understates gross proceeds to the IRS — and if the settlement crosses the §104(a)(2) exclusion line, the mis-reporting can trigger years of CP2000 correspondence. A payer who lumps everything into 1099-MISC Box 10 overstates the attorney's fee income relative to the actual retainer split. The correct approach is two forms: 1099-MISC Box 10 with gross proceeds ($180,000 in the Priya case) and 1099-NEC Box 1 with the attorney's fee ($60,000).

3. Missing the Box 6 corporate-payee exception

Insurance carriers and self-insured employers who apply the general "no reporting for corporations" rule to Box 6 medical payments are wrong. Treas. Reg. §1.6041-3(p)(1) expressly excludes Box 6 (and Box 10) from the corporate-payee exemption. Missing this typically shows up as a §6721 penalty on hundreds or thousands of professional-corporation medical groups when the IRS's information-return unit runs a matching audit.

4. Reporting rent to a property manager as if the manager were the landlord

A tenant who pays $50,000 of annual rent to a property-management company and issues the 1099-MISC to the management company (rather than to the underlying landlord) has misreported. Under Treas. Reg. §1.6041-1(e), the property manager is the payer to the landlord downstream; the tenant's obligation is discharged by paying the manager, and the tenant does not issue a 1099-MISC unless the manager is a non-corporation payee for services (in which case the fee, not the rent, is the reportable payment, and it belongs on 1099-NEC).

5. Overlooking the January 31 versus February 28 dual deadline

Most 1099-MISC boxes have a January 31 recipient-copy deadline and a February 28 (paper) / March 31 (electronic) IRS-copy deadline. But Box 8 (substitute payments) and Box 10 (attorney gross proceeds) have a delayed February 16 recipient-copy deadline. A payer who batches all 1099-MISCs to a January 31 delivery calendar has done nothing wrong for most boxes but has voluntarily accelerated Box 8 and Box 10 delivery. A payer who tries to delay Box 1 (rents) to February 16 has missed the recipient-copy deadline and triggered §6722.

6. Ignoring the T.D. 9972 10-form electronic-filing aggregation

A business with 6 W-2s, 2 1099-NECs, and 3 1099-MISCs has 11 information returns in aggregate and is required to e-file all of them, per the 2023 final regulation. Paper filing when e-filing is required is treated as a failure-to-file under §6721(a)(2)(A) — the full $340-per-form penalty applies to every paper form filed, not a reduced rate for "late" filing. The aggregation is across all information return types, not per form type; a small business that just barely stayed below the threshold under the old 250-form rule can easily be over the new 10-form rule.

Action checklist for anyone touching 1099-MISC in 2026

  1. Refresh every W-9 you rely on. Re-request W-9s from every landlord, every royalty recipient, every medical provider, and every attorney by the second business day of the calendar year. Verify TINs against the IRS TIN Matching Program before the first payment of the year.
  2. Set up an aging bucket for each Box. Track year-to-date payments per payee separately for §6041 payees ($2,000 threshold), §6050N royalty recipients ($10), §6045(f) attorney gross proceeds ($600), and §3406 backup-withholding candidates. Any payee approaching a threshold in November should trigger a compliance review.
  3. Distinguish attorney fees from gross proceeds up front. If your business regularly settles claims through counsel, encode the split on the payment authorization form (both an attorney-fee line item and a gross-proceeds line item) so year-end reporting produces the correct two-form pair automatically.
  4. Do not apply the corporate exception to Box 6 or Box 10. Every medical provider and every attorney gets a 1099-MISC at the applicable threshold regardless of entity type.
  5. Enable e-file for all information returns. Any payer expecting to file 10 or more forms in aggregate needs e-file capability. Pre-file a Form 4419 or use an authorized transmitter to establish the FIRE account well before the January 31 recipient-copy deadline.
  6. Calendar the delayed Box 8 and Box 10 deadlines. February 16 for recipient copies of substitute-payment (Box 8) and attorney-gross-proceeds (Box 10) forms. The IRS-copy deadline (February 28 paper / March 31 electronic) is unchanged.
  7. Reconcile 1099s received against your books. If you are the payee, the CP2000 program will match every 1099 the IRS receives against your Form 1040. Reconciliations happen at the IRS end regardless of whether you do them at your end. Do yours first.
  8. Request corrections when you find them. The corrective process is straightforward — CORRECTED-box 1099s filed by the original payer resolve almost every discrepancy. Do not paper over payer errors on your own return; document them and request a CORRECTED form.

FAQ — the questions people actually ask

What income is reported on Form 1099-MISC in 2026?

1099-MISC now reports miscellaneous payments that are not wages, not nonemployee service compensation, and not credit-card or third-party network settlements. The most common categories in 2026 are rents paid to a landlord or property manager (Box 1); royalties paid to authors, songwriters, and oil-and-gas mineral owners (Box 2); prizes, awards, taxable damages, and other miscellaneous income (Box 3); federal income tax withheld under backup-withholding rules (Box 4); fishing boat proceeds (Box 5); medical and health-care payments (Box 6); a direct-sales checkbox at $5,000 or more (Box 7); substitute payments in lieu of dividends or interest (Box 8); crop insurance proceeds (Box 9); gross proceeds paid to an attorney (Box 10); and a handful of specialized boxes for §409A deferrals, FATCA marking, excess golden parachutes, and nonqualified deferred comp. Nonemployee service compensation moved off this form to Form 1099-NEC starting with tax year 2020.

Did OBBBA change the 1099-MISC $600 threshold in 2026?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 (Pub. L. 119-21) §70433 raised the base information-return reporting threshold under IRC §6041 from $600 to $2,000, indexed in $100 increments starting 2027. That change lifted the Box 1 (rents), Box 3 (other income), Box 6 (medical and health-care payments), and Box 9 (crop insurance proceeds) reporting floors from $600 to $2,000 for payments made in 2026 and later. Box 2 (royalties) remained at $10 because its statutory basis is IRC §6050N, which OBBBA did not touch. Box 10 (gross proceeds paid to an attorney) remained at $600 because its statutory basis is IRC §6045(f), which OBBBA also did not touch — a critical foot-fault for payers who assume every 1099-MISC threshold moved together.

What is the difference between 1099-NEC Box 1 and 1099-MISC Box 10?

1099-NEC Box 1 reports payments to an attorney for services the attorney actually performed — a legal fee. 1099-MISC Box 10 reports gross proceeds paid to an attorney that pass through the attorney's trust account on the way to the client — the classic example is a $100,000 personal-injury settlement paid to the plaintiff's attorney, of which the attorney keeps a $33,333 contingent fee and remits $66,667 to the plaintiff. The insurance carrier or defendant issues a 1099-MISC to the attorney with $100,000 in Box 10 (gross proceeds under §6045(f) at the $600 threshold that OBBBA did not change) and a separate 1099-NEC to the attorney with the $33,333 contingent fee in Box 1 (services under §6041A at the OBBBA-raised $2,000 threshold). Confusing the two is one of the most common attorney-payment errors.

Do I have to send a 1099-MISC to a corporation?

Generally no — payments to a C corporation or S corporation are exempt from 1099-MISC reporting under Treas. Reg. §1.6041-3(p). But there are two important carve-outs written directly into the 2026 Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC: Box 6 medical and health-care payments ARE reportable even when the payee is a corporation (this is why an insurance carrier issues 1099-MISC to a professional-corporation medical group), and Box 10 gross proceeds paid to an attorney ARE reportable even when the attorney's practice is a professional corporation. Ignoring these two exceptions is a top-three payer-side error each filing season.

What is the royalty reporting threshold on 1099-MISC in 2026?

The reporting threshold for Box 2 royalties is $10 in a calendar year — dramatically lower than the general 1099-MISC threshold — because Box 2 has its own statutory basis in IRC §6050N (enacted in 1988), which OBBBA §70433 did not modify. Publishers, streaming platforms, record labels, oil-and-gas mineral-interest operators, and patent-licensing entities are required to issue a 1099-MISC to any royalty recipient who received $10 or more in the calendar year. This is why a self-published author with $12 of Kindle Direct Publishing royalties still receives a 1099-MISC, and why a small-percentage mineral-rights owner with $23 of quarterly oil-and-gas royalties still gets one every year.

Who reports rent payments on 1099-MISC in 2026?

A trade or business that pays $2,000 or more in rent to a non-corporation landlord in the calendar year must issue a 1099-MISC with the total rent in Box 1. This covers a business paying office, warehouse, or retail space rent to a landlord who is an individual, partnership, or LLC. It also covers a property manager who collects rent on behalf of a landlord — the property manager files the 1099-MISC to the landlord under Treas. Reg. §1.6041-1(e). Rental real estate held by an individual owner who is not engaged in a §162 trade or business generally does NOT have to issue 1099-MISCs to their vendors under the current IRS position (which reversed a 2010-era attempt at broader landlord reporting), but the rules are still active if the landlord's rental activity rises to a §162 trade or business.

What is Box 3 "Other Income" on 1099-MISC used for in 2026?

Box 3 is the catch-all bucket for taxable payments that do not fit any other 1099-MISC or 1099-NEC box. Common Box 3 examples in 2026 include prizes and awards from sweepstakes, contests, and game shows (the classic Publishers Clearing House winners' 1099-MISC); taxable damages from lawsuits that are NOT for physical injury or physical sickness under IRC §104(a)(2) (a hostile-work-environment settlement, an emotional-distress award without accompanying physical injury, a punitive-damage component); Native American per-capita distributions from tribal gaming revenue; certain research-study participant payments; and taxable retail incentive payments that are not for the recipient's services (e.g., a rebate program). The threshold is $2,000 post-OBBBA (up from $600) because Box 3 rides on IRC §6041 like Boxes 1, 6, and 9.

What are the 1099-MISC filing deadlines and penalties in 2026?

The recipient copy is due by January 31, 2027 for tax year 2026 payments (except Boxes 8 and 10, which have a February 16, 2027 recipient-copy deadline). The IRS copy is due by February 28, 2027 if filed on paper or March 31, 2027 if filed electronically. Any payer who files 10 or more information returns in total across all forms must file electronically under the T.D. 9972 final regulations, which took effect for calendar year 2023 forward. Failure-to-file penalties under IRC §6721 in 2026 range from $60 per form (filed within 30 days of the deadline) up to $340 per form (filed after August 1 or not filed at all), with total annual caps ranging from roughly $712,500 to $4.1 million depending on business size. Intentional disregard carries a penalty of at least $680 per return with no annual cap. Payee-statement failures under IRC §6722 mirror the §6721 amounts and stack, so a single unfiled 1099-MISC can carry combined §6721 + §6722 exposure of $1,360 or more.

Is 1099-MISC the same as 1099-NEC in 2026?

No. 1099-NEC is exclusively for nonemployee compensation — a single amount in Box 1 under IRC §6041A — and was split out of 1099-MISC starting with tax year 2020 to eliminate the mid-1980s confusion between service payments and other miscellaneous payments. 1099-MISC covers everything else: rents, royalties, prizes, awards, medical payments, attorney gross proceeds (not fees for services), crop insurance, fishing-boat proceeds, and specialized items like §409A deferrals and excess golden parachute payments. In 2026, most independent contractors will receive a 1099-NEC for services performed, and most landlords, authors, medical providers, and personal-injury attorneys will receive a 1099-MISC for their respective payment types.

What should I do if I receive a 1099-MISC I do not agree with?

First, verify whether the payer's reported amount matches the recipient's actual receipts. Payer errors are common — a $3,500 Box 3 award reported as $35,000, a landlord's Box 1 rent that includes a security deposit refund, an attorney's Box 10 gross proceeds that double-count a partial settlement release. If the payer made an error, request a CORRECTED 1099-MISC — the payer files a new form with the CORRECTED box marked and issues a copy to the recipient and to the IRS. If the payer refuses, the taxpayer files the return based on the correct amount, attaches an explanation (or files Form 8275 disclosure if the position is potentially aggressive), and keeps documentation. The IRS's automated Computer Paragraph 2000 (CP2000) matching program will flag the mismatch; responding to a CP2000 with the corrected numbers plus supporting bank statements typically resolves the issue within 60-90 days.
