IRC §2036 Retained Life Estate in 2026: The §2036(a)(1) Income-Interest Rule, §2036(a)(2) Designation Power, §2036(b) Byrum Reversal, and the Powell/Bongard/Strangi FLP Doctrine

§2036 is the section that makes "you gave it away, but it's still yours" real. A lifetime transfer that reserves the income, keeps the possession, or holds the power to say who enjoys the property comes back into the gross estate at date-of-death fair market value — not the value on the day it left. It is the load-bearing rule behind every GRAT that has to outlive its grantor, every QPRT that has to outlive its term, every FLP that survives a Tax Court audit, and every deeded-with-a-life-estate family home. This is the 2026 walkthrough of every operative subsection, every §7520-driven inclusion formula, and the FLP attack line that starts at Strangi and Bongard and ends at Estate of Powell.

CalcLeap Editorial Published September 13, 2026 22 min read How we research

Every estate planner working an estate near the $15 million §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount that the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, §70106, July 2025) permanently reset eventually meets the same question: did the client actually give the property away, or did they keep something that will drag it back into the gross estate?[1] The answer routes through IRC §2036 — the retained-life-estate rule — and the answer is that Congress, the Treasury, and the Tax Court have spent a full century steadily broadening what counts as "keeping something."[2]

§2036 has two operative rules under subsection (a), a controlled-corporation voting-rights rule under subsection (b) reversing the Supreme Court's 1972 Byrum decision, and a bona fide sale exception under subsection (c). §2036(a)(1) pulls property back if the decedent retained possession, enjoyment, or the right to income.[3] §2036(a)(2) pulls property back if the decedent retained — alone or with any other person — the right to designate who possesses or enjoys the property. §2036(b) extends §2036(a)(1) to retained voting rights over stock of a controlled corporation. And §2036(c) excepts transfers that were bona fide sales for adequate and full consideration.[3]

This guide walks §2036 from the statute forward: the historical arc from the Revenue Act of 1916 through the 1976 Byrum reversal, the operative mechanics of each subsection, the §2036(b) controlled-corporation attribution rules under §318, the Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(c)(2) formula that determines exactly how much of a GRAT comes back if the grantor dies during the annuity term, the QPRT rent-payment trap under §2036(a)(1), the FLP §2036 attack line from Estate of Strangi through Estate of Bongard to Estate of Powell, the §2035(a) three-year lookback interaction, and three worked case studies. Every dollar figure is the 2026 figure under Rev. Proc. 2025-32.[4] When you're ready to size the numbers, our estate-tax calculator, retirement calculator, and compound-interest calculator handle the arithmetic side.

The 1916 → 1932 → 1976 arc — a century of broadening

The current §2036 is the fifth generation of the statute. The Revenue Act of 1916 first imposed a federal estate tax and included in the gross estate any property transferred by the decedent "in contemplation of or intended to take effect in possession or enjoyment at or after his death."[5] The Supreme Court read that early phrase narrowly in May v. Heiner, 281 U.S. 238 (1930), holding that a transfer with a retained life income interest was not a transfer "intended to take effect in possession or enjoyment at or after death" because the transferee held a vested remainder interest during the decedent's lifetime.[6] Congress responded in the Joint Resolution of March 3, 1931 — six days after May v. Heiner became final — by amending the statute to reach transfers with retained life interests, later codified in the Revenue Act of 1932 §803(a) as the ancestor of today's §2036.[7]

The Internal Revenue Code of 1954 restated the rule as §2036 with the two-part structure of §2036(a)(1) (retained enjoyment) and §2036(a)(2) (retained designation power) that persists today.[3] In 1972, the Supreme Court decided United States v. Byrum, 408 U.S. 125, holding 5-4 that a decedent who transferred controlled-corporation stock to a trust while retaining the voting rights had not retained §2036(a)(1) enjoyment because the fiduciary duty of a controlling shareholder to minority shareholders — plus the corporate board's independent fiduciary duty — prevented the retained vote from constituting enjoyment.[8] Congress reversed Byrum four years later in the Tax Reform Act of 1976 §2009(a) by adding §2036(b), which explicitly treats the retention of the right to vote (directly or indirectly) shares of stock of a controlled corporation as retention of the enjoyment of the transferred property.[9]

The 1976 amendment defined a "controlled corporation" as one in which the decedent owned, or had the right (either alone or with any other person) to vote, stock possessing at least 20% of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote, applying the constructive-ownership rules of §318 (as modified for §2036(b)(2)).[10] The 20% threshold plus the §318 attribution rules make §2036(b) reach further than most closely-held-business owners realize on first read.

§2036(a)(1) — retained possession, enjoyment, or right to income

The current text of §2036(a)(1) provides that the value of the gross estate shall include the value of all property to the extent of any interest therein of which the decedent has at any time made a transfer (except in the case of a bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth), by trust or otherwise, under which he has retained for his life or for any period not ascertainable without reference to his death or for any period which does not in fact end before his death — the possession or enjoyment of, or the right to the income from, the property.[3]

The rule has three temporal triggers, each independently sufficient:

Temporal triggerWhat it capturesTypical fact pattern
For lifeRetention explicitly for the decedent's lifetimeDeed reserving life estate; trust reserving income for life
Period not ascertainable without reference to deathRetention tied to an event that cannot be measured without knowing the death dateTrust reserving income until "such time as the grantor's needs are met"
Period which does not in fact end before deathFixed-term retention that happens to outlast the decedent15-year GRAT annuity where grantor dies in year 12

Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(a) makes clear that the retention need not be legally enforceable — an implied agreement or understanding is enough.[11] This is the doctrinal foundation for the IRS's regular §2036 attacks on parents who deed the family home to children but continue to live in it. Estate of Maxwell v. Commissioner, 3 F.3d 591 (2d Cir. 1993), affirmed the Tax Court's finding of an implied agreement where an 82-year-old widow deeded her home to her son and daughter-in-law for a $270,000 note but continued to live in the house until death, paying $10 per month in nominal rent while the son paid all the property taxes.[12] The Second Circuit held that the transaction failed the bona fide sale test, and even absent that failure, the implied-agreement retention triggered §2036(a)(1).

The family-home implied-agreement trap

The single most common §2036(a)(1) mistake is a parent deeding the family home to children — often as part of a Medicaid-planning strategy or a well-meaning estate reduction — while continuing to occupy the residence rent-free. Absent a genuine arm's-length lease at fair-market rent, actually paid, the IRS will assert an implied agreement under Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(a). A $2 million home returned to the gross estate at date-of-death FMV consumes $2 million of §2010(c) exemption and, at the 40% top rate under §2001, can generate up to $800,000 of federal estate tax on top of state estate tax exposure in states like Massachusetts and Oregon.

What counts as "enjoyment" beyond direct occupancy

The Tax Court has treated a wide range of retained economic benefits as §2036(a)(1) enjoyment, including:

  • The right to the income stream itself. The classic case — a trust reserving all trust income to the grantor for life — is squarely within §2036(a)(1).
  • The obligation to satisfy a legal support duty. Where transferred property is used to discharge the grantor's legal obligation to support a spouse or minor child, the grantor is treated as having retained the enjoyment. Douglass v. Commissioner, 143 F.2d 961 (3d Cir. 1944).
  • Rent-free use of transferred real estate. Even when the transferee holds legal title, ongoing rent-free use of the transferred property by the decedent produces §2036(a)(1) inclusion under the implied-agreement doctrine.
  • Use of transferred funds to pay the grantor's personal expenses. Estate of McNichol v. Commissioner, 265 F.2d 667 (3d Cir. 1959), pulled back property that the grantor had transferred to family members but continued to enjoy the income from through informal arrangements.[13]

§2036(a)(2) — retained power to designate who enjoys the property

§2036(a)(2) is the parallel rule targeting retained control rather than retained economic benefit. The section pulls back property if the decedent retained — either alone or in conjunction with any other person — the right to designate who shall possess or enjoy the property or the income from it, during any of the three temporal windows above.[3]

The section reaches even a grantor who kept no personal economic benefit. A grantor who transfers securities to an irrevocable trust for the benefit of the grantor's four children, retaining as trustee the sole discretion to sprinkle income among the children, has retained §2036(a)(2) designation power — the full trust corpus comes back into the gross estate at death regardless of the fact that the grantor personally received not a dollar. The rule is why properly designed grantor trusts vest sprinkle discretion in an independent trustee rather than the grantor.

The Supreme Court in United States v. O'Malley, 383 U.S. 627 (1966), held that the retained power need not be exercisable in the grantor's sole discretion; a power exercisable "in conjunction with any other person" — even a beneficiary of the trust — is sufficient for §2036(a)(2).[14] This is the doctrinal engine behind the FLP §2036 attacks discussed below: even a limited-partner decedent who could not act alone under state law can still fall within §2036(a)(2) if the partnership agreement gives the decedent, together with other partners, the power to liquidate or amend distributions.

The Byrum ghost — why §2036(a)(2) matters more than it looks

Before Congress added §2036(b) in 1976, the government tried repeatedly to fit retained voting rights into §2036(a)(2), arguing that a majority shareholder's vote for dividends is a designation of enjoyment. The Supreme Court in Byrum rejected that reading under both §2036(a)(1) and §2036(a)(2), citing the majority shareholder's fiduciary duty as a limiting principle.[8] §2036(b) fixed the specific controlled-corporation problem. But the underlying §2036(a)(2) doctrine — that any retained ability to influence who receives distributions is a retained designation power — survived Byrum and has been vigorously applied by the Tax Court in the FLP context ever since. Powell's §2036(a)(2) holding is a direct descendant.

§2036(b) — the Byrum reversal on controlled-corporation voting rights

§2036(b) provides that, for purposes of §2036(a)(1), the retention of the right to vote (directly or indirectly) shares of stock of a controlled corporation is considered to be a retention of the enjoyment of transferred property.[3] A "controlled corporation" means any corporation in which the decedent owned (with application of the §318 attribution rules) or had the right (alone or in conjunction with any person) to vote stock possessing at least 20% of the total combined voting power of all classes of stock entitled to vote, at any time after the transfer and during the three-year period ending on the date of death.[10]

Three features are load-bearing:

  • 20% threshold. Materially lower than the classic §368 "control" definition of 80%, and much lower than a majority interest. A decedent who transferred stock to a trust while retaining the right to vote 22% of the company's voting shares is within §2036(b) even though the transferred shares were only 5% of the vote and the retained vote is a minority position.
  • §318 attribution. Stock owned by the decedent's spouse, children, grandchildren, and parents is attributed to the decedent for the 20% test. Stock owned by a corporation in which the decedent owns 50% or more of the value is also attributed. The attribution rules can push a nominally 15% shareholder past the 20% threshold when family ownership is considered.[15]
  • Three-year lookback. The 20% test looks back over the three-year period ending on the date of death. A decedent who owned 25% at the time of transfer, then dropped to 15% two years before death, is still within §2036(b) — the 20% threshold was met at some point within the three-year window.

The §2036(b) inclusion amount is the full date-of-death value of the transferred stock, not the value of the retained vote alone. This is the sharp edge: a decedent who transferred $30 million of company stock to a trust while retaining voting rights sees the full $30 million pulled back into the gross estate at date-of-death value, not the (typically small) actuarial value of the retained voting incident. On a $30 million estate above the §2010(c) exemption, that translates to $12 million of federal estate tax at the 40% top rate.

The §2036(b) drafting fix

The standard practitioner response is a recapitalization before transfer that separates voting from economic ownership. Non-voting common stock (or non-voting Class B stock) transferred to the trust — with the decedent retaining voting Class A stock personally — escapes §2036(b) because the transferred stock carries no vote for the decedent to have "retained." The recapitalization must have independent business or estate-planning purpose to survive a §2701 anti-freeze attack. Where the transfer must include voting stock, the alternative is to place voting control in an independent trustee (not the grantor) — the grantor holds no vote at all, directly or indirectly, over the transferred shares.

§2036(c) — the bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration exception

§2036(c) provides that §2036 does not apply to a transfer that was a bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth.[3] The exception is the doctrinal gate through which every sale-to-IDGT installment note and every properly-structured FLP contribution must pass.

The Tax Court's two-prong Bongard test for the bona fide sale exception in the FLP context requires (1) a legitimate and significant nontax reason for the transaction, and (2) that the transferor receive an interest in the partnership proportional to the fair market value of the property transferred.[16] The first prong has been read expansively by the Tax Court to encompass genuine consolidation of family investments, active asset management by a family manager, protection from creditors of family members with real litigation exposure, and preservation of a family operating business. It has been read narrowly against pure tax-driven transactions with no operational purpose — Estate of Strangi v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2003-145 (aff'd 417 F.3d 468 (5th Cir. 2005)), being the canonical example of a §2036 loss where the FLP had no purpose beyond estate-tax reduction.[17]

The second prong — proportional interest — is a check on the "grantor gets nothing back" fact pattern that arose in early FLP challenges where wealthy taxpayers would contribute assets to a partnership and receive a limited-partnership interest with little or no economic value. If the transferor receives a partnership interest whose fair market value is a fraction of what was contributed (because minority and marketability discounts wipe out most of the notional value), the courts have treated the transaction as a partial gift rather than a full-consideration sale, and §2036 has applied to the gift portion.

§2036 and GRATs — the Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(c)(2) inclusion formula

A Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT) under IRC §2702 transfers property to an irrevocable trust in exchange for a fixed annuity payable to the grantor for a term of years, with the remainder passing to designated beneficiaries at the end of the term. The gift-tax value of the remainder interest is computed under §7520 using the applicable federal rate, and a "zeroed-out" GRAT (structured under the Walton v. Commissioner, 115 T.C. 589 (2000), technique) can produce a near-zero taxable gift.[18] If the trust assets outperform the §7520 rate, the excess passes to the remainder beneficiaries transfer-tax-free.

The load-bearing risk is §2036: if the grantor dies during the annuity term, the grantor has retained the right to income (via the annuity) for "any period which does not in fact end before his death" — squarely within §2036(a)(1). Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(c)(2) provides the formula for the inclusion amount:

Inclusion = Annuity amount ÷ §7520 rate at death

The formula computes the principal necessary to produce the annuity amount indefinitely at the §7520 rate in effect on the decedent's date of death. When §7520 rates are low, the formula produces a large inclusion — often the entire GRAT corpus. When §7520 rates are high, the inclusion is smaller. The 2026 §7520 rates have hovered in the 5.0%-5.4% range through the summer per the IRS Section 7520 Interest Rates monthly publication; see our §7520 valuation deep-dive for the current rate table and monthly history.[19]

The formula's practical output: a $5 million zeroed-out 10-year GRAT paying a $650,000 annual annuity to the grantor, where the grantor dies in year 6 with a 5.2% §7520 rate at death, produces an inclusion of $650,000 ÷ 0.052 = $12.5 million — but only up to the actual date-of-death fair market value of the GRAT corpus, which caps the inclusion. In practice the entire GRAT corpus is pulled back into the gross estate, and the transfer-tax benefit of the GRAT is destroyed. The counter-strategy is the "rolling short-term GRAT" — 2-year or 3-year GRATs that reduce mortality exposure to a small window at the cost of less time for the trust assets to outperform.

§2036 and QPRTs — the residence, the term, and the fair-market rent

A Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT) under Treas. Reg. §25.2702-5(c) transfers a personal residence (or a vacation home) to an irrevocable trust, with the grantor retaining the right to occupy the residence rent-free for a fixed term of years. At the end of the term, the residence passes to the remainder beneficiaries. The taxable gift is computed under §7520 as the value of the remainder interest — often a small fraction of the residence's fair market value because the grantor retains the use for years.[20]

The two §2036 risks are:

  • Death during the retained term. If the grantor dies before the term expires, §2036(a)(1) pulls back the full date-of-death FMV of the residence — undoing the entire plan. Term selection is therefore an actuarial question: too long a term reduces the taxable gift but increases mortality risk; too short a term reduces mortality risk but leaves too much value in the taxable gift.
  • Post-term rent-free occupancy. If the grantor wants to continue living in the residence after the term ends, the grantor must pay fair-market rent to the remainder trust. Failure to pay adequate rent supports an implied-agreement §2036(a)(1) attack under Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(a): the grantor "retained" enjoyment beyond the stated term. The rent payment must be documented, actually paid, and reflect true market rent — a lease with a below-market rate is not enough.

§2036 and family limited partnerships — Strangi, Bongard, Powell

The family limited partnership (FLP) is a state-law entity into which parents contribute investment assets in exchange for limited-partnership interests, retaining general-partner control (often through a single-purpose LLC). The limited-partnership interests are then gifted or sold to children at valuations discounted for minority interest and lack of marketability — a 30%-40% aggregate discount is typical. If the discounts are respected, the FLP structure moves substantial wealth out of the parents' estates at reduced transfer-tax cost.

The IRS has attacked FLPs under §2036 continuously since the late 1990s. The three landmark cases:

Estate of Strangi v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo 2003-145 (aff'd 417 F.3d 468 (5th Cir. 2005))

Strangi transferred 98% of his assets — $9.9 million of marketable securities and cash — to a family limited partnership two months before death, retaining a general partnership interest through his son-in-law's management company. The Tax Court held that Strangi retained a §2036(a)(1) implied right to enjoyment (because the FLP paid the decedent's expenses, funded his household, and distributed to him as a partner) and a §2036(a)(2) power to designate enjoyment (because as a partner he could join in a vote to liquidate).[17] The bona fide sale exception failed because the transaction had no significant nontax purpose. The full $9.9 million was pulled back into the gross estate.

Estate of Bongard v. Commissioner, 124 T.C. 95 (2005)

Bongard involved a two-tiered transaction: contribution to a holding company (Empak) with a legitimate business consolidation purpose, followed by a contribution to an FLP with no similar business purpose. The Tax Court applied the two-prong bona fide sale test and held that the Empak transaction survived the §2036 attack (legitimate business consolidation purpose + proportional interest received) but the FLP transaction did not (no significant nontax purpose beyond estate-tax reduction).[16] Bongard is the source of the two-prong test that has since dominated FLP §2036 litigation.

Estate of Powell v. Commissioner, 148 T.C. 392 (2017)

Powell is the modern high-water mark. Nancy Powell, acting through an agent under a durable power of attorney seven days before death, transferred $10 million of cash and securities to an FLP in exchange for a 99% limited-partnership interest. The agent then assigned the limited-partnership interest to a family charitable lead annuity trust in exchange for a 100% charitable interest in the CLAT and a remainder to Powell's children. The Tax Court held under §2036(a)(2) that Powell's ability, together with the general partner (her children), to dissolve the partnership under Delaware state law was a retained right to designate who enjoyed the property.[21] The court also raised a §2043(a) double-inclusion theory that would have produced additional §2036 exposure even under §2036(a)(1) alone.

Powell dramatically increased §2036 exposure for FLPs because the underlying holding — that a limited partner's participatory right to join in a dissolution or amendment vote is a §2036(a)(2) retained power — arguably reaches every FLP where the decedent held any partnership interest at death. Practitioners have responded with structural changes (independent general partner, dissolution requiring unanimous consent excluding the decedent, forfeiture of the decedent's dissolution vote on transfer) and with a renewed emphasis on demonstrating genuine nontax purpose under the Bongard test.

Interaction with the §2035(a) three-year lookback

§2035(a) pulls back into the gross estate the value of property that would have been included under §2036 if the decedent had retained the interest until death — provided the decedent released the retained interest within three years of death.[22] The interaction between §2036 and §2035(a) matters most in three fact patterns:

  • QPRT term ends, grantor releases residency rights, grantor dies within 3 years. The grantor's release of §2036(a)(1) possession triggers §2035(a) three-year lookback. The residence is pulled back at date-of-death FMV.
  • GRAT terminates, grantor releases annuity, grantor dies within 3 years. The annuity termination is a §2036(a)(1) release. §2035(a) pulls back if death within 3 years, though in practice the grantor's death after full annuity payment leaves nothing in the estate to include beyond what was already received.
  • FLP dissolves and distributes to partners, grantor dies within 3 years. The dissolution is a release of §2036(a)(2) designation power. §2035(a) pulls back the FLP's date-of-death corpus into the gross estate at date-of-death FMV.

DSUE portability and the surviving-spouse effect

§2010(c)(4) portability, made permanent by OBBBA §70106, lets a surviving spouse inherit the deceased spouse's unused §2010(c) exemption (the DSUE amount) if the first spouse's estate files Form 706 and elects portability.[1] A §2036 inclusion at the first death consumes exemption dollar-for-dollar and reduces the DSUE that ports to the surviving spouse.

Consider a $20 million first-death estate that includes a $5 million QPRT pulled back under §2036(a)(1) because the grantor died during the retained term. Absent the §2036 inclusion, the gross estate would have been $15 million and DSUE porting to the surviving spouse would have been $15M − $15M = $0. With the §2036 inclusion, the gross estate is $20 million, taxable estate exceeds the $15M exemption by $5 million, federal estate tax at 40% is $2 million, and DSUE porting to the surviving spouse is now $0 (exhausted). If the first-death estate had been under $15 million pre-inclusion (say, $12 million with a $5 million QPRT contribution that was expected to fully escape the estate), the §2036 inclusion pushes gross estate to $17 million; DSUE porting drops from $3 million to $0. Second-death planning based on the expected DSUE port has to reflect the §2036 tail risk.

State-level treatment

The 12 state-estate-tax jurisdictions plus the District of Columbia — Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Washington — generally conform their gross-estate definitions to federal §2031, incorporating §2036 by reference or by parallel state statute.[23] Our state estate-tax field guide covers the state-by-state mechanics; the §2036 exposure is particularly acute in the states with low state-exclusion amounts (Oregon at $1 million; Massachusetts at $2 million; Rhode Island at $1.9 million in 2026) where a §2036 inclusion that has no federal cash-tax effect (because the federal $15M exemption absorbs the taxable estate) can still generate substantial state estate tax.

A representative fact pattern: an Oregon decedent with a $6 million estate transfers her $3 million home to a QPRT with a 10-year retained term. She dies in year 8. §2036(a)(1) pulls the residence back into the gross estate. Federal §2010(c) exemption fully covers the $6 million taxable estate — zero federal estate tax. Oregon estate tax on $6 million above the $1 million state exemption is roughly $600,000 at the state's graduated schedule (max 16% at the top). The QPRT plan that produced no federal tax reduction still costs $600,000 in state estate tax because §2036 pulled the residence back.

Worked case studies

Case 1: The QPRT death during the retained term

Margaret (68) is a Massachusetts resident with a $12 million estate composed of $8 million in liquid marketable securities and a $4 million Boston brownstone (her primary residence). Her advisor recommends a Qualified Personal Residence Trust with a 10-year retained-occupancy term. Using the §7520 rate of 5.2% in effect on the transfer date, the taxable gift of the remainder interest is computed as approximately $1.8 million (roughly 45% of the residence FMV, reflecting the actuarial value of a 10-year retained-use interest at Margaret's age).

Margaret transfers the residence to the QPRT on January 15, 2024. Margaret dies on March 12, 2028 — approximately 50 months later, still 68 months short of the QPRT term end.

The §2036(a)(1) analysis:

  • Retained interest. Margaret retained rent-free occupancy for the 10-year term. That is a §2036(a)(1) retained possession of the property for a period that does not in fact end before her death (10 years, actual retention 50 months, death well before term end).
  • Inclusion amount. The full date-of-death FMV of the residence. Assume the Boston brownstone has appreciated to $5.2 million by March 2028.
  • Bona fide sale exception. Not applicable — Margaret received no consideration for the transfer.

Federal consequence: Margaret's gross estate is $8M (securities) + $5.2M (residence pulled back) = $13.2M. The §2010(c)(3) exemption (assume $15.6M in 2028 with typical CPI-U indexing) fully covers the taxable estate — zero federal estate tax. Margaret used up $1.8M of §2010(c) exemption on the 2024 QPRT gift; that exemption is restored implicitly because §2036 inclusion removes the transaction from the "taxable gifts" side of the ledger to the "gross estate" side. DSUE porting to Margaret's surviving spouse Frank is $15.6M − $13.2M = $2.4M — much smaller than the $15.6M full port that would have been available if Margaret had held the residence directly and not attempted the QPRT.

Massachusetts consequence: Massachusetts estate tax on a $13.2M estate above the $2M state exemption at the state's graduated schedule (max 16%) produces roughly $1.4 million of state estate tax. Without the §2036 pull-back — if Margaret had survived to January 2034 — the residence would have passed to remainder beneficiaries outside the estate, Massachusetts gross estate would have been $8M with an estimated Massachusetts estate tax of approximately $770,000 on the $6M-above-exemption base. The QPRT failure cost approximately $630,000 in Massachusetts state estate tax alone.

Case 2: The GRAT and the §7520 low-rate trap

David (72) is a New York City resident with a $30 million estate. His advisor structures a $6 million 10-year zeroed-out GRAT paying David a $780,000 annual annuity, funded with a concentrated position in a growth technology stock that he believes will materially outperform the applicable §7520 rate over the term. The §7520 rate at the transfer date is 5.0%. The taxable gift under §2702 is approximately $0 (zeroed-out per Walton).

David dies in year 7. The stock has appreciated substantially, and the GRAT corpus at David's death is $9.4 million. The §7520 rate on the date of David's death is 4.6% (a slight decline from the transfer date).

The §2036(a)(1) analysis:

  • Retained annuity. David retained the right to receive $780,000 per year for 10 years, a period that did not in fact end before his death (10 years, actual survival 7 years).
  • Inclusion formula. Under Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(c)(2), the inclusion is the principal necessary to produce the $780,000 annuity indefinitely at the 4.6% §7520 rate: $780,000 ÷ 0.046 = $16.96 million. But the inclusion is capped at the actual date-of-death value of the GRAT corpus, which is $9.4 million. The full $9.4 million is pulled back into David's gross estate.
  • Bona fide sale exception. Not applicable — the GRAT was structured as a gift with a retained interest, not a sale.

Federal consequence: David's gross estate rises by $9.4 million (the pulled-back GRAT corpus). Assuming David's other assets total $24 million by the date of his death, the total gross estate is $33.4 million. The §2010(c) $15M exemption covers the first $15M; the taxable estate is $18.4M; federal estate tax at 40% under §2001 is $7.36 million. If David had survived the 10-year term, the entire $9.4M of appreciation would have passed to the remainder beneficiaries transfer-tax-free — a savings of roughly $3.76M in federal estate tax at the 40% rate. Mortality during the term destroyed the plan.

New York consequence: New York estate tax on $33.4M with the "cliff" rule (New York taxes the full estate, not just the amount above the state exemption of $6.94M for 2026 per NY Tax Law §952, if the estate exceeds 105% of the exemption) produces roughly $3.9M of state estate tax. The total federal-plus-state transfer tax on David's death is approximately $11.26M against a $33.4M gross estate — a 33.7% effective transfer-tax rate.

Case 3: The FLP §2036(a)(2) Powell attack

Robert (79) is a Florida resident with a $50 million estate. His attorney forms a Delaware family limited partnership to consolidate the family's investment holdings, with Robert's three adult children as general partners (each holding a 1/3 interest in a single-purpose LLC that serves as the general partner) and Robert as a 98% limited partner. Robert contributes $40 million of marketable securities in exchange for the 98% limited-partnership interest. Six months later, Robert gifts a 40% limited-partnership interest to a family GST-exempt trust for his grandchildren, claiming a 35% aggregate minority-interest-and-marketability discount that values the gift at $10.4 million ($40M × 40% × 0.65 = $10.4M).

Robert dies 22 months after the initial FLP contribution and 16 months after the gift. The IRS challenges the transaction under §2036(a)(2) on Powell principles.

The Tax Court analysis (hypothetical, applying Powell):

  • §2036(a)(2) retained power. Under Delaware law, dissolution of the FLP requires the unanimous consent of all partners. As a 98%-turned-58% limited partner, Robert had the ability, together with the general partner and the trust as gift recipient, to consent to dissolution. Under Powell, that participatory consent right is a retained §2036(a)(2) power to designate who enjoys the property.
  • Bona fide sale exception failure. Under Bongard's two-prong test, the FLP formation must serve a legitimate and significant nontax purpose. Robert's attorney documented "family investment consolidation" as the purpose, but the FLP's actual operation showed distributions primarily to Robert to pay his personal living expenses, no active investment management by the general partner beyond continuing the prior brokerage relationships, and no discernible business or non-tax operational purpose. The Tax Court finds the transaction fails prong 1.
  • Inclusion amount. Under §2036(a)(2), the full date-of-death FMV of the property contributed to the FLP — $42 million as of Robert's date of death (reflecting market appreciation) — is pulled back into Robert's gross estate. The 35% valuation discount claimed on the gift is disregarded; the full undiscounted value is included. The prior gift of the 40% limited-partnership interest is also affected: under Powell's §2043(a) analysis, the estate must include the full pre-discount value of the gifted interest and offset only the discounted value received in the gift transaction, producing an additional gross-estate increase.

Federal consequence: Robert's gross estate is $10M (non-FLP assets) + $42M (FLP corpus pulled back) = $52M. Robert used $10.4M of §2010(c) exemption on the earlier gift; the remaining exemption is $15M − $10.4M = $4.6M. Taxable estate: $52M − $4.6M = $47.4M. Federal estate tax at 40%: approximately $18.96M. Against a $50M lifetime economic estate, that is a 37.9% effective transfer-tax rate.

Compare to the counterfactual: if the FLP had genuine business purpose sufficient to survive Bongard's prong 1, the 35% discount would have been respected, the gift would have consumed $10.4M of exemption, the balance of the FLP interest would have been included at Robert's death at a discounted $30.4M ($47M × 58% × 0.65), remaining §2010(c) exemption would still have been $4.6M, taxable estate would have been $10M + $30.4M − $4.6M = $35.8M, federal estate tax at 40% would have been $14.32M — a savings of approximately $4.64M relative to the Powell-attack outcome. The FLP's lack of documented nontax purpose cost the family $4.64M in avoidable federal transfer tax.

Six recurring mistakes in §2036 planning

  1. Deeding the family home to children while continuing to occupy rent-free. The classic implied-agreement §2036(a)(1) trap. If the parent will continue to live in the residence after transfer, execute a genuine arm's-length lease at fair-market rent, document it, and actually collect the rent. Anything less invites §2036 inclusion under Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(a) and Estate of Maxwell principles.
  2. Selecting a GRAT term longer than the grantor's actuarial life expectancy. A 15-year or 20-year GRAT looks efficient on paper because the taxable gift is small, but any GRAT term long enough to produce a low taxable gift also carries meaningful mortality risk. The rolling short-term GRAT strategy (2-year or 3-year rolling GRATs) sacrifices some compounding for meaningfully lower §2036 exposure. For elderly clients or clients with health concerns, term selection is an actuarial exercise, not an estate-planning exercise.
  3. Failing to pay fair-market rent post-QPRT term. The QPRT term expires, the residence has passed to the remainder trust, and the grantor wants to keep living in the house. Any post-term occupancy without a genuine arm's-length lease at fair-market rent supports an implied-agreement §2036(a)(1) attack. Set the lease before the term expires, ensure it is properly documented, and pay the rent through a standing transfer arrangement so there is a durable record.
  4. Retaining voting rights over transferred stock of a controlled corporation. The §2036(b) Byrum reversal reaches lower than most closely-held-business owners realize — the 20% threshold plus §318 attribution rules capture many minority-interest situations. Where voting must be retained, use a recapitalization to separate voting from economic ownership: transfer non-voting stock and retain voting stock personally. Where the transfer must include voting stock, place voting control in an independent trustee.
  5. Structuring an FLP with no genuine nontax purpose. The Bongard two-prong test is unforgiving. An FLP that exists only for discount-driven estate-tax reduction — with no active investment management, no consolidation of previously scattered assets, no protection from real creditor exposure — will lose a §2036 challenge under Strangi principles, and the entire contributed value will be pulled back into the gross estate at full undiscounted value. Document business purpose contemporaneously with formation, not as a post-audit reconstruction.
  6. Retaining any power to sprinkle income or designate distributions in an irrevocable trust. The classic §2036(a)(2) trap. A grantor who names themselves as trustee with discretion to sprinkle income among the beneficiaries — even if the grantor personally receives nothing — has retained a designation power sufficient for full §2036 inclusion. Vest sprinkle and other discretionary distribution powers in an independent trustee. The Uniform Trust Code's provisions on trustee-substitution can help preserve family control without triggering §2036(a)(2).

Action checklist — before executing any transfer with §2036 exposure

  1. Identify the retained interest. Walk through each of §2036(a)(1) (possession/enjoyment/income), §2036(a)(2) (designation power), and §2036(b) (controlled-corporation voting rights) explicitly. Document the analysis in a memo to file so the intent is visible if the IRS later challenges.
  2. Assess actuarial mortality against the retained term. Use current IRS mortality tables (Table 2010CM for §7520 valuations post-2022) or, for clients with material health concerns, an underwriting score from a life-settlement broker. If the probability of surviving the term is below 80%, the plan carries meaningful §2036 exposure and alternative structures should be considered.
  3. Structure GRATs and QPRTs to survive. Prefer 2-year to 5-year rolling GRATs for elderly or health-compromised clients. Choose QPRT terms based on age-specific actuarial tables — a 65-year-old grantor should think in 10-15 year terms; a 75-year-old grantor should think in 5-8 year terms. Document the term-selection reasoning in the QPRT trust file.
  4. For QPRTs, plan the post-term lease before the term ends. Draft the lease at least 12 months before the QPRT term expires. Set rent at documented fair-market value using at least two comparable arm's-length rentals. Establish standing transfer arrangements for the rent payments so the rent flow is durable and auditable.
  5. Vest discretionary trust powers in an independent trustee. Do not name the grantor as sole trustee of an irrevocable trust with discretionary distribution authority. Use an independent institutional trustee or a professional individual trustee with no beneficial interest in the trust. A trust protector with limited amendment authority can preserve family influence without triggering §2036(a)(2).
  6. Recapitalize before transferring controlled-corporation stock. If the plan involves transferring stock in a corporation where the decedent will retain any voting authority, execute a §368 recapitalization to separate voting from economic ownership. Transfer non-voting stock; retain voting stock. Confirm the recapitalization has independent business purpose to survive §2701 anti-freeze scrutiny.
  7. Document FLP nontax purpose contemporaneously. Every FLP formation should include a contemporaneous memorandum explaining the specific nontax reasons for the entity — asset management consolidation, creditor protection from named potential creditors, active operational purpose, generational transition planning, family governance formalization. Vague references to "family investment planning" will not survive Bongard scrutiny; specific facts about the family's actual circumstances will.
  8. Coordinate with state-level exposure. §2036 inclusions can produce state estate-tax cost in the 12 state-estate-tax jurisdictions and D.C. even when federal §2010(c) fully absorbs the taxable estate. Model the state consequence explicitly, particularly in Oregon, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Minnesota where the state exemption is materially below the federal $15M threshold.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §2036 do in 2026?

IRC §2036 is the retained-life-estate rule in Chapter 11 of the Internal Revenue Code. It pulls into the decedent's gross estate the full date-of-death fair market value of property that the decedent transferred during life but with respect to which the decedent retained either (a)(1) the possession or enjoyment of, or the right to income from, the property, or (a)(2) the right, alone or with any other person, to designate who shall possess or enjoy the property or its income. The retention must be for the decedent's life, for a period not ascertainable without reference to death, or for a period that does not in fact end before death. §2036(b), added in 1976, treats the retention of voting rights over stock of a controlled corporation as a §2036(a)(1) retained enjoyment. §2036(c) excepts bona fide sales for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth.

How does §2036(a)(1) differ from §2036(a)(2)?

§2036(a)(1) focuses on economic enjoyment: if the decedent transferred property but kept the right to possess it or receive the income, the full value of the property is pulled back. Classic examples include a deed of the family home reserving a life estate, a transfer of a bond portfolio while retaining the coupon stream, and — under §2036(b) — a transfer of stock in a controlled corporation while retaining the voting rights. §2036(a)(2) focuses on control over enjoyment: even if the decedent kept no economic benefit, the retained right to designate who among a class of beneficiaries will receive possession or income triggers inclusion. A grantor who transfers property to a trust and retains the sole discretion to sprinkle income among the grantor's children invokes §2036(a)(2), even though the grantor personally receives nothing. The two subsections operate independently — either is sufficient by itself to cause full inclusion.

What was Byrum v. United States and why did Congress reverse it in 1976?

In Byrum v. United States, 408 U.S. 125 (1972), the Supreme Court held 5-4 that a decedent who had transferred stock of a closely-held corporation to a trust but retained the right to vote the shares had not retained the enjoyment of the property under §2036(a)(1). The Court reasoned that the decedent's fiduciary duty as a controlling shareholder, plus the corporate directors' independent fiduciary duty to all shareholders, prevented the retained vote from constituting enjoyment. Congress reversed Byrum by adding §2036(b) in the Tax Reform Act of 1976. §2036(b) now provides that the retention of the right to vote (directly or indirectly) shares of stock of a controlled corporation is treated as retention of the enjoyment of the transferred property. A controlled corporation is one in which the decedent owned or had the right to vote 20% or more of the combined voting power at any time during the 3-year period ending on the date of death, applying the §318 stock-attribution rules.

Does a GRAT trigger §2036 inclusion if the grantor dies during the annuity term in 2026?

Yes. A Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT) under IRC §2702 pays the grantor a fixed annuity for a term of years, with the remainder passing to beneficiaries. During the annuity term, the grantor has retained the right to income from the property in the form of the annuity payment. If the grantor dies during the term, §2036(a)(1) pulls back into the gross estate the portion of the GRAT corpus needed to produce the annuity at the applicable §7520 rate — often the entire GRAT corpus when interest rates are low relative to the annuity amount. Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(c)(2) provides the actual inclusion formula, using the §7520 rate in effect on the date of death. This is the fundamental risk of the 2-year zeroed-out (Walton) GRAT strategy: short terms minimize mortality risk but also minimize compounding room.

Does a QPRT trigger §2036 inclusion if the grantor dies during the retained-residence term?

Yes. A Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT) under Treas. Reg. §25.2702-5(c) transfers a personal residence to a trust with the grantor retaining rent-free occupancy for a term of years, after which the residence passes to remainder beneficiaries. The grantor's retained possession during the term is a §2036(a)(1) retained enjoyment. If the grantor dies during the term, the full date-of-death fair market value of the residence is pulled back into the gross estate — undoing the entire QPRT plan. If the grantor survives the term but wants to remain in the residence, the grantor must pay fair-market rent to the remainder trust; failure to pay adequate rent post-term can support an implied-agreement §2036(a)(1) attack under Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(a). Practitioners typically choose QPRT terms based on IRS actuarial tables (Table 90CM under §7520) balanced against actuarial mortality assumptions.

What is the Estate of Powell §2036 attack on family limited partnerships?

Estate of Powell v. Commissioner, 148 T.C. 392 (2017), extended the §2036 doctrine to family limited partnerships (FLPs) in a way that dramatically increased §2036 exposure for FLP planning. The decedent, acting through a power of attorney seven days before death, transferred $10 million of cash and securities to an FLP in exchange for a 99% limited partnership interest, then assigned the limited partnership interest to a family charitable lead annuity trust. The Tax Court held that the decedent's ability, together with other partners, to dissolve the partnership under state law was a retained right under §2036(a)(2) to designate who would enjoy the property, causing the full transferred value to be pulled back into the gross estate. Powell also raised a novel §2043(a) double-inclusion theory that would have applied even under §2036(a)(1) analysis. Estate of Bongard, 124 T.C. 95 (2005), and Estate of Strangi, T.C. Memo 2003-145, are the earlier canonical FLP §2036 cases; Powell is the modern high-water mark.

What is the §2036(c) bona fide sale exception and when does it defeat a §2036 attack?

§2036(c) excepts from §2036 any transfer that was a bona fide sale for adequate and full consideration in money or money's worth. In the FLP context, the Tax Court in Estate of Bongard v. Commissioner, 124 T.C. 95 (2005), established a two-prong test to determine whether an intra-family transfer to an FLP is a bona fide sale: (1) there must be a legitimate and significant nontax reason for the transaction, and (2) the transferor must receive an interest in the partnership proportional to the fair market value of the property transferred. Post-Bongard case law has emphasized the first prong; the presence of a genuine business purpose — active asset management, consolidation of family investments, protection from creditors of family members, ongoing operational involvement — can defeat a §2036 attack. The absence of such a purpose (as in Strangi and Powell) leaves the FLP exposed to full §2036 inclusion.

Does §2036 apply to a transfer with a retained income interest that expires before death?

It depends on whether the retention was "for the decedent's life," "for any period not ascertainable without reference to death," or "for any period which does not in fact end before death." A transfer reserving income for a fixed 10-year term that expires 5 years before death is outside §2036 — the retention did in fact end before death. But a transfer reserving income for the decedent's lifetime, or for a period tied to some event that could last past death, remains within §2036 even if the actual retention ended before death. The §2035(a) three-year lookback then handles releases of §2036-hooked interests within three years of death: if a decedent released a §2036 retained interest 24 months before death, §2035(a) pulls the property back as if §2036 had been in force at death. A release outside the three-year window escapes both sections.

How does §2036 interact with the $15 million §2010(c) basic exclusion amount under the OBBBA?

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 2025) §70106 permanently set the §2010(c)(3) basic exclusion amount at $15,000,000 for estates of decedents dying and gifts made after December 31, 2025, indexed for inflation. §2036 operates independently of §2010(c). Property pulled into the gross estate under §2036 is included at date-of-death fair market value; the §2010(c) unified credit — worth $15,000,000 in 2026 — then offsets the resulting tentative tax on the aggregate taxable estate. For estates well below the $15M threshold, a §2036 inclusion may increase the taxable estate but produce no cash tax if the exemption still absorbs the total. For estates near or above the threshold, §2036 can transform what looked like a completed lifetime transfer into $15M+ of gross-estate inclusion at the 40% top rate under §2001 — often the difference between a full DSUE port to the surviving spouse and a materially diminished one under §2010(c)(4).

What is an implied agreement under §2036 and how does the IRS use it to attack retained residences and family homes?

Treas. Reg. §20.2036-1(a) provides that §2036 applies to a transfer under which the decedent retained the possession or enjoyment of the property under an express or implied agreement or understanding. An implied agreement exists when the facts and circumstances show that the decedent and the transferee understood, at the time of the transfer, that the decedent would continue to have the beneficial use of the property. The most common fact pattern: the decedent deeds the family home to children but continues to live in it rent-free, pays all utilities and property taxes, and treats the home as their own. The IRS regularly asserts an implied agreement in this posture, and the Tax Court has repeatedly upheld the assertion in cases including Estate of Maxwell v. Commissioner, 3 F.3d 591 (2d Cir. 1993), where a $290,000 home transferred to a son with a $10 monthly rent arrangement was pulled back into the gross estate. The practical rule: if the parent will continue to occupy the residence after transfer, the arrangement must be a genuine arm's-length lease at fair-market rent, documented and actually paid, or the transfer will not survive a §2036 challenge.
