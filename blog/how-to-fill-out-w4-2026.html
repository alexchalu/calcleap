How to Fill Out Form W-4 in 2026: The Complete 5-Step Guide After the OBBBA, Multiple-Jobs Fix, and Every Line Explained

Form W-4 is the single most-consequential piece of paper the American worker fills out — the difference between a $3,000 refund and a $4,000 tax bill runs entirely through five short steps. The 2020 redesign eliminated allowances and replaced them with dollar amounts, and dual-income households have been quietly under-withholding by $1,500–$4,000 per year ever since. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 permanently extended the TCJA rates, raised the Child Tax Credit to $2,200, and rewrote the 2026 withholding tables — meaning every W-4 filed before January 2026 is now computing withholding on stale assumptions. This is the complete 2026 line-by-line walkthrough, with the Publication 15-T mechanics your employer actually runs, four worked case studies, and every trap that costs typical households real money.

By CalcLeap Editorial Published July 3, 2026 Reviewed against IRS Form W-4 (2026 revision), IRS Publication 15-T (2026), IRC §3402, and Pub. L. 119-21 §70102 ~26 min read

The W-4 is the closest thing the federal tax code has to a steering wheel. Once you sign it, every paycheck for the rest of the year — and every paycheck after that, indefinitely, until you sign another one — has its federal income tax withholding computed from what you wrote. Get it right and you owe nothing extra on April 15 and receive a small refund or nothing at all, which is the correct outcome. Get it wrong and you either give the U.S. Treasury an interest-free $3,000 loan for the year, or you show up at filing time with a five-figure surprise bill and an underpayment penalty on top1. The form is four pages. The instructions are two of those pages. The actual worker input is a handful of dollar amounts. And yet a plurality of American households are quietly getting it wrong every year — under-withholding, over-withholding, or ignoring the update triggers that the IRC §3402(f)(2) framework treats as mandatory2.

Two things changed for 2026 that make the current W-4 baseline different from every W-4 filed since the 2020 redesign. First, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 (Pub. L. 119-21) permanently extended the seven-bracket TCJA rate schedule (10 / 12 / 22 / 24 / 32 / 35 / 37 percent) that had been scheduled to sunset on December 31, 2025 and revert to the pre-TCJA five-bracket structure with rates topping out at 39.6 percent3. Second, OBBBA §70102 raised the Child Tax Credit from $2,000 to $2,200 per qualifying child under 17 with a valid Social Security number and made the credit inflation-indexed starting 2026 — the first structural CTC change since the TCJA4. The IRS incorporated both changes into the 2026 Publication 15-T withholding tables that employers use to translate the W-4's dollar amounts into per-paycheck deductions5. If you filed a W-4 in late 2025 with an inflated Step 4(c) extra-withholding line to prepare for the anticipated sunset-induced rate hike, you are now over-withholding — measurably.

This guide walks the W-4 line by line for the 2026 revision, computes the actual math your employer runs against your entries, and shows the four traps that cost the most: the Step 2 multiple-jobs error at dual-income households, the Step 3 CTC-phaseout error at high earners, the Step 4(a) under-reporting error at gig workers with wage jobs, and the Exempt-claim error at teens and college students. What the W-4 actually does

Form W-4, formally the "Employee's Withholding Certificate," is the instrument by which an employee tells the employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck under IRC §3402(a). Withholding is not a tax — it is a prepayment of the employee's federal income tax liability, credited on the employee's Form 1040 at line 25a against the year's total tax owed. If withholding exceeds liability, the difference is refunded. If withholding falls short, the shortfall is due with the return, plus a possible §6654 underpayment penalty if the shortfall breaches the safe-harbor floor6.

The W-4 was redesigned effective January 1, 2020, replacing the old allowance-based form (in which each "allowance" represented roughly one personal exemption's worth of income shielded from withholding) with a dollar-denominated form under Treas. Reg. §31.3402(f)(2)-1. The old form had one input the whole system pivoted on — the number of allowances — and the employer's withholding table looked up a per-paycheck subtraction indexed to that count. The 2020 redesign made three changes: it removed allowances entirely (because the TCJA had suspended personal exemptions and made the old lookup meaningless); it added a dollar-denominated dependent-credit line (Step 3) that reduces withholding by exactly the credit's expected annual value spread across paychecks; and it added a multiple-jobs adjustment worksheet (Step 2) that attempts to solve the dual-income under-withholding problem that had been endemic under the allowance model7. The form has retained that structure through the 2026 revision.

The two-sentence W-4 mental model

The employer starts with the maximum realistic withholding (single filer, no adjustments, no credits) and then subtracts based on what you tell it — subtracts based on your filing status (Step 1), on your credits (Step 3), on your deductions above the standard (Step 4b) — and adds based on your extra withholding (Step 4c) and your other non-wage income (Step 4a). Step 2 is a separate adjustment that recognizes that two paychecks compute their withholding independently but the tax liability is joint, so extra withholding must be added somewhere to prevent under-withholding.

The five steps at a glance

The 2026 Form W-4 has five numbered steps on page 1, plus three worksheets on pages 3-4. Only Steps 1 and 5 are mandatory. Steps 2, 3, and 4 are all optional — but "optional" here means "the form treats them as $0 unless you enter something," which is a very different thing from "safe to skip."

Step 1: personal information and filing status

Step 1 collects the employee's name, address, Social Security number, and filing status. The filing-status choice — Single or Married Filing Separately (checkbox 1c-first-option); Married Filing Jointly or Qualifying Surviving Spouse (checkbox 1c-second-option); or Head of Household (checkbox 1c-third-option) — is the single largest lever on the W-4 short of Step 4(c). It determines which set of Publication 15-T withholding tables the employer uses and which standard deduction is baked into the calculation8.

For 2026, the standard deductions embedded in the withholding tables under Rev. Proc. 2025-32 are $16,100 for single and married-filing-separately, $32,200 for married filing jointly and qualifying surviving spouse, and $24,150 for head of household. These figures are the pass-through inputs to the "Automated Payroll Systems" percentage-method tables in Pub. 15-T that most employers use to compute per-paycheck withholding9. Under the 2020 form redesign, Married Filing Jointly reduces withholding relative to Single not by a flat percentage but by shifting the entire tax-bracket schedule to the MFJ brackets (which have wider 10 / 12 / 22 percent zones than the Single brackets). Head of Household similarly uses the HoH brackets and a larger standard deduction than Single.

The "check-the-Single-box-when-you're-married" trap

Employees who used to check "Single, zero allowances" on the pre-2020 W-4 to force higher withholding sometimes carry that habit into 2026 by checking Step 1(c) Single even though they file jointly. This over-withholds by roughly $2,500 to $6,500 a year at a typical MFJ household making $150K combined, because the withholding tables use single brackets that hit 22 percent much sooner than the MFJ brackets. If you want more withholding while filing jointly, use Step 4(c), not a false Step 1(c) filing status.

Step 2: the multiple-jobs step (the biggest trap on the form)

Step 2 exists because the withholding tables in Publication 15-T are calibrated to each job independently — each employer sees only its own paycheck and computes withholding as if it were the household's only source of wages. When there are two jobs in the household (a working spouse, a second job for the same employee, or a mid-year job change), each job's withholding leaves out the fact that the second job's income stacks on top of the first, pushing the household into a higher marginal bracket than either job's withholding table anticipates. The IRS estimates that under the pre-2020 allowance system, dual-income households under-withheld by an average of $1,800 per year. The 2020 redesign added Step 2 specifically to close that gap10.

Step 2 gives you three options, in the order the form recommends them:

  1. Step 2(a) — Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/W4App. This is the IRS's own web tool that walks you through combined household income, YTD withholding, expected credits and deductions, and outputs a recommended Step 4(c) extra-withholding dollar amount. It handles asymmetric earnings, mid-year starts, one-time bonuses, and non-wage income better than any of the paper worksheets. If you have any of those complications, use 2(a).
  2. Step 2(b) — Use the Multiple Jobs Worksheet on page 3. This paper worksheet uses lookup tables (Tables 1 and 2 on page 4 of the W-4) indexed by the two highest-paying jobs' annual gross pay and filing status, and returns a dollar amount to enter on Step 4(c) of the highest-paying job's W-4. It's more accurate than option (c) for asymmetric earnings but less accurate than the online estimator.
  3. Step 2(c) — Check the box. If (and only if) there are exactly two jobs total across the household, both jobs pay roughly the same, and both employees check the Step 2(c) box on both W-4s, the employer will use a modified withholding table that assumes the two-job stacking. The Step 2(c) instructions warn that the boxes on both W-4s must be checked and that pay should be similar.

The Step 2(c) accuracy problem. Step 2(c) is a shortcut that works well when the two jobs are within about 15 percent of each other's gross pay, and degrades quickly when they diverge. Under the Publication 15-T Step 2(c) tables, both employers effectively assume the household's total income is 2 × their own paycheck's annualized income, and back into a withholding rate accordingly. When one spouse earns $180,000 and the other earns $40,000, each employer's Step 2(c) table assumes the household earns 2 × its own paycheck, so the $180K employer withholds as if the household earns $360K (over-withholding), and the $40K employer withholds as if the household earns $80K (drastically under-withholding). The net across the two jobs still under-withholds the actual household by roughly $2,800 a year at those figures. Anytime the two jobs are asymmetric, use 2(a) or 2(b) instead.

The one-line Step 2 heuristic

If the higher-earning job pays at least 1.5× the lower-earning job, Step 2(c) will under-withhold you. Use the IRS estimator (option a) instead and copy the recommended Step 4(c) amount to the highest-paying job's W-4 only. Do not enter Step 4(c) on both W-4s or you will double-withhold.

Step 3: dependents and the post-OBBBA credit math

Step 3 tells the employer to reduce your withholding by the annual dollar value of the tax credits you expect to claim on your Form 1040. The two credits eligible for Step 3 are the Child Tax Credit under IRC §24 and the Credit for Other Dependents under IRC §24(h)(4). Under OBBBA §70102, effective for tax year 2026, the CTC is $2,200 per qualifying child under 17 with a valid Social Security number, up from $2,000, and the credit is indexed for inflation in $100 increments starting in 20274. The Credit for Other Dependents (ODC) remains $500 per qualifying non-child dependent (an elderly parent you support, a qualifying relative in college aged 17-24, a dependent with an ITIN rather than an SSN), also subject to OBBBA inflation-indexing.

The Step 3 arithmetic:

(# qualifying children × $2,200) + (# other dependents × $500) = Step 3 total

Example: a married-filing-jointly household with two children ages 4 and 7 (both with SSNs) and one 22-year-old college-student dependent enters (2 × $2,200) + (1 × $500) = $4,900 on the last line of Step 3. The employer will reduce total annual withholding by that amount, spread across paychecks — for a biweekly (26 paychecks) worker, $188.46 less withheld from each paycheck.

The high-income phaseout. Both CTC and ODC phase out at a 5 percent rate ($50 of credit per $1,000 of income) above modified AGI thresholds of $400,000 for married filing jointly and $200,000 for single, head of household, and married filing separately11. Practical effect: a MFJ household with two qualifying children ($4,400 total CTC) fully phases out at $488,000 in MAGI; a single filer with one child ($2,200 CTC) fully phases out at $244,000. Employees whose modified AGI is above the threshold should reduce or zero out their Step 3 entry to avoid claiming a credit they will not receive at filing. Because withholding is spread over the year and the phaseout is applied at filing, an over-claim on Step 3 shows up as a shortfall on April 15 that can trigger a §6654 penalty.

Step 4: other income, deductions, and extra withholding

Step 4 is three optional lines that give you fine-grained control over the withholding rate. Each has a specific fact pattern it fixes.

Step 4(a) — Other income (not from jobs)

Line 4(a) captures annual non-wage taxable income that you want covered by paycheck withholding rather than by making quarterly estimated payments on Form 1040-ES. The most common 4(a) entries in 2026:

  • Interest and ordinary dividends from taxable brokerage accounts, bank CDs, and I bonds redeemed within the year
  • Retirement distributions from traditional IRAs or 401(k)s where you did not elect withholding on Form W-4P
  • Gig-economy earnings from platform work (Uber, DoorDash, Airbnb, Etsy) that will produce a 1099-NEC or 1099-K but is not covered by any wage withholding — see our 1099-K vs 1099-NEC guide
  • Rental income net of Schedule E expenses
  • Taxable Social Security if you have started benefits mid-year

The employer treats the 4(a) amount as if it were additional annual wage income and computes the marginal withholding accordingly — this converts what would otherwise be a quarterly estimated-payment obligation into a smooth per-paycheck withholding stream. Practical effect: a $20,000 gig side-hustle at a 22-percent-bracket household adds roughly $20,000 × 0.22 = $4,400 of annual withholding, or $169 per biweekly paycheck. This is often more convenient than four quarterly Form 1040-ES payments, and the withholding treatment under IRC §6654(g)(1) still counts as evenly paid across the year for penalty purposes even if the W-4 change happens in October.

Step 4(b) — Deductions above the standard

Line 4(b) captures the amount by which your itemized deductions plus above-the-line adjustments (student loan interest, HSA contributions, self-employed health insurance, deductible IRA contributions) exceed the standard deduction. For 2026 the standard deduction under Rev. Proc. 2025-32 is $16,100 single / $32,200 MFJ / $24,150 HoH9. Only the excess flows through 4(b); if you take the standard deduction, 4(b) is $0.

Example: a MFJ household with $42,000 of itemized deductions (say, $18,000 SALT capped at $10,000 under OBBBA §70103 extension of the $10K cap, $8,000 home mortgage interest, $16,000 charitable, $8,000 medical above 7.5% AGI floor) enters $42,000 - $32,200 = $9,800 on Step 4(b). The employer treats this as an additional deduction against annual wage income, reducing withholding by roughly the household's marginal rate × $9,800.

Step 4(c) — Extra withholding per paycheck

Line 4(c) is a flat additional dollar amount withheld from every paycheck, on top of whatever Steps 1-4(b) already compute. It is expressed as a per-paycheck dollar amount, not an annual number — a common employee error is to enter the annual number and get 26× or 52× the intended withholding.

Step 4(c) is the most reliable adjustment on the form because it does not run through the withholding tables at all — it is simply added to the withholding computed from the other steps. If you have any of these fact patterns, use 4(c):

  • The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator or the Step 2(b) worksheet gave you a specific dollar amount to add
  • You owed more than $500 on your prior-year return and cannot pin down which lever caused it
  • You have a working spouse and Step 2(c) is inaccurate for your asymmetric earnings
  • You received a Lock-In Letter (Form 2800C) from the IRS requiring higher withholding
  • You expect a mid-year event (a bonus, a large capital gain, an inheritance) and want to pre-fund the tax

The "annual on 4(c)" mistake

Employees who mean to add $2,000 of annual extra withholding sometimes enter $2,000 on Step 4(c). If they are paid biweekly, the employer withholds an additional $2,000 × 26 = $52,000 across the year — a five-figure over-withholding. The correct entry is $2,000 ÷ 26 = $77 per paycheck (round up to $77 or down to $76; the IRS Estimator does the exact math). Read the fine print under Step 4(c): "Extra withholding. Enter any additional tax you want withheld each pay period."

How your employer actually computes withholding from your W-4

Once you sign the W-4, your entries flow into the mechanical formula in IRS Publication 15-T, "Federal Income Tax Withholding Methods." Most employers use the "Percentage Method Tables for Automated Payroll Systems" (Pub. 15-T Worksheet 1A) which is the algorithm that payroll software runs. Understanding the calculation is the fastest way to sanity-check what shows up on your pay stub. Here is Worksheet 1A end-to-end, worked for a hypothetical MFJ employee earning $85,000, two children, biweekly pay, no Step 2 or 4 entries:

Line 1a: Biweekly gross wages = $85,000 ÷ 26 = $3,269.23
Line 1b: (Optional) Nontaxable adjustments = $0
Line 1c: 1a − 1b = $3,269.23
Line 1d: Annualize: 1c × 26 = $85,000
Line 1e: Add Step 4(a) other income = $85,000
Line 1f: Subtract Step 4(b) deductions = $85,000
Line 1g: Subtract MFJ standard-deduction hardcoded offset = $85,000 − $32,200 = $52,800
Line 1h: Adjusted annual wage amount = $52,800
Line 2a: Look up 2a-2c in the MFJ percentage-method table
Line 2b: Tentative annual withholding at $52,800 MFJ (12% bracket): approximately $4,022
Line 3a: Subtract Step 3 credits ÷ 26 impact = $4,022 − $4,400 = -$378 (floor at $0)
Line 3b: Annual withholding = $0
Line 4a: Divide by 26 = $0 per biweekly paycheck
Line 4b: Add Step 4(c) extra withholding = $0 + $0 = $0 federal income tax withheld per paycheck

Practical interpretation: this household's Child Tax Credits ($4,400) exceed their pre-credit annual withholding ($4,022), so their federal income tax withholding is zero. They will still owe FICA (7.65% × $3,269.23 = $250.10 per paycheck), state tax, and any local tax — but their federal-income-tax box on Form W-2 will show $0. This is correct: their expected federal tax liability, after credits, is close to $0.

Now vary the same household by giving them a $60,000 working spouse who also does not adjust Step 2. Household total income is now $145,000, expected federal tax liability is about $18,000, but each spouse's employer is still computing withholding as if their paycheck were the household's only wage. Neither employer withholds enough to cover the household's actual liability, and at filing time they owe roughly $8,000-$10,000 more than either job's W-2 withheld. This is the Step 2 trap in one paragraph. Our paycheck calculator and income tax calculator both surface this problem before you sign the form, not after you file the return.

When to update your W-4 mid-year

The IRS identifies a specific set of life events after which you should file a new W-4 within 10 days under Treas. Reg. §31.3402(f)(2)-1(b). Filing on time is a compliance obligation, not a courtesy — a materially wrong W-4 that goes uncorrected after a triggering event can be treated as failure to file a required certificate under IRC §3402(f)(2)(B):

What OBBBA changed for 2026 W-4 withholding

OBBBA (Pub. L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) is the single largest tax package since the 2017 TCJA and reshapes several inputs that flow through the W-4 into 2026 withholding. Five changes matter for W-4 setup:

  1. Permanent extension of TCJA rate brackets (§70101). The seven-bracket rate schedule of 10 / 12 / 22 / 24 / 32 / 35 / 37 percent, previously scheduled to sunset December 31, 2025 and revert to pre-TCJA rates of 10 / 15 / 25 / 28 / 33 / 35 / 39.6 percent, is now permanent. Publication 15-T tables for 2026 use the TCJA schedule; pre-2026 W-4s calibrated for post-sunset rate increases now over-withhold.
  2. CTC increase to $2,200 (§70102). Per qualifying child under 17 with a valid SSN, indexed for inflation in $100 increments beginning 2027. Step 3 math is unchanged in structure but the per-child multiplier is now $2,200 instead of $2,000. Households with children who filed a 2020-through-2025 W-4 are now under-claiming their Step 3 credit by $200 per child if they used the old $2,000 figure.
  3. Standard-deduction inflation adjustment (§70103 extension). The TCJA's roughly doubled standard deduction is permanent; Rev. Proc. 2025-32 sets 2026 values at $16,100 single / $32,200 MFJ / $24,150 HoH — up from 2025's $15,000 / $30,000 / $22,500. Step 4(b) computations should use the higher 2026 figures.
  4. §199A qualified business income deduction preserved. The 20% QBI deduction under IRC §199A for pass-through income is permanent under OBBBA §70104, with modified phaseout thresholds. Employees with side-business gig income entering Step 4(a) should net down the entered amount by the QBI-deduction impact to avoid over-withholding on the gig portion.
  5. §162(m) executive-compensation deduction cap changes. OBBBA §70105 modifies the $1M annual executive-compensation deduction limit under §162(m), primarily affecting publicly traded companies. Employees with executive compensation subject to §162(m) do not need to change their W-4; the change is on the employer side.

Four worked case studies

Case 1 — The single filer with one job, no dependents (Marcus, Atlanta)

Marcus is a 28-year-old single filer earning $68,000 as a software engineer, no dependents, no side income, itemizes at less than the standard deduction (so takes the standard). His 2026 W-4 has:

  • Step 1(c): Single
  • Step 2: blank (only one job)
  • Step 3: $0 (no dependents)
  • Step 4(a): $0; Step 4(b): $0; Step 4(c): $0
  • Step 5: signed

Under Publication 15-T Worksheet 1A: biweekly gross = $2,615.38, annualized = $68,000, minus single standard deduction $16,100 = $51,900 adjusted annual wage. Single tax on $51,900 = $10,988 × 12% starting at $11,925 = roughly $6,608 annual withholding. Divided by 26 = $254 per biweekly paycheck. Marcus's 2026 tax liability at $68,000 single, standard deduction, no other credits, is approximately $6,608 — so his refund at filing is close to $0. Correct outcome. No W-4 changes needed unless he adds a side hustle, gets married, or has a child.

Case 2 — The dual-income MFJ household with children (Priya & Ravi, San Jose)

Priya earns $180,000, Ravi earns $80,000, MFJ, two children (ages 4 and 8, both with SSNs). Both filed post-2020 W-4s and both checked the Step 2(c) box in a well-meaning attempt to fix multiple-jobs withholding. Neither entered anything on Steps 3 or 4. This is a classic textbook Step 2(c) failure.

Actual 2026 household tax liability: household MAGI $260,000, standard deduction $32,200, taxable income $227,800. MFJ tax at $227,800 = $32,000 (approximate, across 10/12/22/24 brackets). Minus CTC 2 × $2,200 = $4,400 (fully allowed at $260K MAGI — under the $400K MFJ phaseout threshold). Net liability = $27,600.

Withholding actually applied by the two employers under Step 2(c): Priya's employer withholds approximately $28,000 (over-withholds slightly assuming the household is 2 × $180K = $360K); Ravi's employer withholds approximately $4,200 (drastically under-withholds assuming the household is 2 × $80K = $160K). Combined household withholding = $32,200. Refund at filing = $32,200 − $27,600 = $4,600.

Now suppose only Priya checks Step 2(c) and Ravi does not. Priya's employer withholds $28,000 as before. Ravi's employer withholds at Single rates as if his job is standalone = approximately $8,900. Combined = $36,900. Refund = $9,300. Still over-withholding, but much more so.

The correct fix: neither spouse checks Step 2(c). Priya uses the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/W4App, which recomputes on the combined $260K income, subtracts the $4,400 in CTCs (entered on Step 3 as $4,400), and outputs a Step 4(c) recommendation of approximately $50 per biweekly paycheck on Priya's job only. Ravi files a plain W-4 with no Step 2, Step 3 = $0 (Priya took the CTC), Step 4 = $0. Combined 2026 withholding lands within $500 of the actual $27,600 liability. This is the correct Step 2 setup for asymmetric dual-income MFJ households and is the single largest W-4 improvement most such households can make.

Case 3 — The gig worker with a wage job (Jasmine, Denver)

Jasmine earns $52,000 as a full-time marketing coordinator (W-2) and expects $22,000 of 1099-NEC and 1099-K income in 2026 from a graphic-design side business. Single, no dependents. She wants to avoid quarterly estimated payments and cover the side-hustle tax through wage withholding.

  • Step 1(c): Single
  • Step 2: blank (only one wage job)
  • Step 3: $0
  • Step 4(a): $22,000 (annual expected non-wage income)
  • Step 4(b): $0
  • Step 4(c): $0 initially — the 4(a) entry does the heavy lifting
  • Step 5: signed

Employer's Pub. 15-T Worksheet 1A computation: annualized wage $52,000 + $22,000 4(a) = $74,000, minus single standard deduction $16,100 = $57,900 adjusted annual wage. Single tax at $57,900 = approximately $7,900 annual withholding. Divided by 26 = $304 per biweekly paycheck (versus $199 per paycheck if she had left 4(a) blank).

What this does not cover: self-employment tax on the gig income. Jasmine still owes 15.3% SE tax on $22,000 × 0.9235 = $20,317 net SE earnings = $3,109 SE tax. That must be paid via quarterly Form 1040-ES to avoid an §6654 underpayment penalty, or she can add roughly $3,109 ÷ 26 = $120 per paycheck to Step 4(c) instead — but only if her side-business hasn't already elected to pay SE tax via other means. See our self-employment tax calculator for the full computation, and our estimated quarterly taxes guide for the safe-harbor mechanics.

Case 4 — The high earner in the CTC phaseout zone (David & Sarah, Boston)

David earns $310,000, Sarah earns $145,000, MFJ, one child (age 6, valid SSN). Household MAGI = $455,000, above the $400,000 MFJ CTC phaseout threshold.

CTC phaseout math: MAGI $455,000 minus $400,000 threshold = $55,000 above threshold, rounded up to the next $1,000 = $55,000, × 5% phaseout rate = $2,750 phaseout. Base CTC $2,200 minus $2,750 phaseout = $0 (floored). At MAGI $455K, David and Sarah receive no CTC at all.

If they enter $2,200 on Step 3 anyway, the withholding tables reduce annual withholding by $2,200 spread over both W-4s. At filing time, no CTC is allowed, so they owe an unexpected $2,200 plus interest on the shortfall (if it exceeds the safe-harbor floor). Correct setup: Step 3 = $0 on both W-4s. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator to fine-tune Step 4(c) on David's higher-paying W-4 to cover the household's actual joint tax liability, including the fact that the CTC is entirely phased out. This is a common high-earner error, particularly in the 2026 first year of MAGI-indexed CTC math where prior-year worksheets used $2,000 as the per-child figure.

Claiming Exempt and why most people cannot

Line 4(c) has a special use case: writing "Exempt" in the blank space just below Step 4(c) tells the employer to withhold no federal income tax at all. To qualify, both prongs of IRC §3402(n) must be met — you had no federal income tax liability the prior year, and you expect no federal income tax liability the current year. "No liability" means Form 1040 line 24 (total tax) was $0 after all credits — not just no refund, and not just low income.

Most workers cannot claim Exempt because most workers have some federal income tax liability. The specific fact patterns where Exempt is legitimately available:

  • A dependent college student with only part-time earned income below the standard deduction ($16,100 in 2026)
  • A retiree whose Social Security is fully non-taxable and whose IRA distributions plus other income fall below their standard deduction plus §63(f) additional standard deduction for age 65+
  • An expatriate qualifying for the §911 foreign-earned-income exclusion (fully covered by the exclusion)
  • A Native American whose tribal-source income is entirely exempt under treaty

The Exempt claim expires February 15 of the following year (a hardcoded expiration in the form's instructions). If you claim Exempt for 2026, you must file a new W-4 by February 15, 2027, or the employer must revert to withholding at Single-with-no-adjustments. Claiming Exempt when you do not qualify is a §6682 civil penalty of $500 per false statement plus a potential §7205(a) misdemeanor for a willful false claim, punishable by fine up to $1,000 and/or imprisonment up to one year12.

The teen-summer-job Exempt error

Teenagers with summer jobs sometimes claim Exempt on the theory that "I don't make enough to owe tax." This works if total 2026 earned income is under the dependent's applicable standard deduction ($16,100 for a single earner, or the greater of $1,350 and earned income + $450 up to $16,100 if the teen is claimed as a dependent). If the teen has any significant unearned income (dividends from a custodial brokerage account, interest from a Uniform Transfers to Minors Act custodial account, unearned income allocations from a family trust), the kiddie-tax rules under IRC §1(g) reintroduce liability at the parents' marginal rate and Exempt becomes wrong.

Six common mistakes that cost real money

  1. Checking Step 2(c) on both W-4s when jobs are asymmetric. Under-withholds by $1,500-$4,000 a year at typical dual-income households. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator instead.
  2. Entering the annual dollar on Step 4(c) instead of the per-paycheck dollar. Over-withholds by 26× (biweekly) or 52× (weekly) the intended amount, producing a five-figure paycheck reduction.
  3. Forgetting to update Step 3 when a child turns 17. The dependent transitions from CTC ($2,200) to ODC ($500), a $1,700 delta per child that shows up as an unexpected shortfall at filing.
  4. Claiming Step 3 CTC when household MAGI is above the phaseout. High-earner MFJ households above $400K and single filers above $200K should reduce or zero Step 3 to match the phase-out math.
  5. Ignoring Step 4(a) for gig income. A W-2 employee with $20,000 of side-hustle 1099 income who does not enter it on 4(a) will typically under-withhold by $3,000-$5,000 including SE tax coverage.
  6. Claiming Exempt when the §3402(n) two-prong test is not met. Civil penalty of $500 per false statement under IRC §6682 plus §7205 misdemeanor exposure for willful claims.

Your 2026 W-4 action checklist

  1. ✓ Run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/W4App using your latest pay stub, expected annual earnings, filing status, and dependent count. Save the recommended Step 4(c) amount.
  2. ✓ Verify Step 1(c) filing status is correct for how you actually file (Single, MFJ, or HoH — not "MFJ but check Single for higher withholding").
  3. ✓ For dual-income households: pick ONE Step 2 method. If earnings differ by more than about 15%, do NOT use Step 2(c); use 2(a) IRS Estimator or 2(b) worksheet, and enter Step 4(c) on the highest-paying job only.
  4. ✓ Step 3: multiply children under 17 (with SSN) × $2,200 (post-OBBBA, not old $2,000) and other dependents × $500. Verify MAGI is below the $400K MFJ / $200K other phaseout before claiming.
  5. ✓ Step 4(a): total up expected 2026 interest, dividends, gig 1099 income, taxable IRA distributions. Enter the annual figure so wage withholding covers the tax.
  6. ✓ Step 4(b): if itemizing, subtract the standard deduction ($16,100 single / $32,200 MFJ / $24,150 HoH per Rev. Proc. 2025-32) from your expected itemized total and enter the positive difference. Enter $0 if you take the standard.
  7. ✓ Step 4(c): enter the per-paycheck (not annual) extra withholding recommendation from the IRS Estimator. Double-check by multiplying by paychecks-per-year to make sure you meant that annual amount.
  8. ✓ Sign Step 5 and date. Give the form to HR or upload via your employer's payroll portal. Verify the change on the next pay stub — federal income tax withheld should match the Pub. 15-T Worksheet 1A output within $2.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to fill out a new W-4 for 2026?

Not automatically — your existing W-4 remains valid indefinitely. But three 2026-specific reasons make a refresh worth considering: OBBBA permanently extended TCJA rate brackets (so pre-2026 W-4s prepping for a sunset-driven rate hike now over-withhold), the CTC rose to $2,200 (so Step 3 is under-claimed if you used the old $2,000), and the Pub. 15-T tables incorporate a slightly higher standard deduction. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator is the fastest way to confirm.

What are the 5 steps on Form W-4 in 2026?

Step 1 is personal info and filing status. Step 2 handles multiple jobs. Step 3 is the CTC + ODC dependent-credit math. Step 4 is other non-wage income (4a), deductions above the standard (4b), and per-paycheck extra withholding (4c). Step 5 is the mandatory signature. Steps 2, 3, and 4 are optional but their omission has predictable consequences.

How does Step 2 multiple jobs work?

Three options in the order the form recommends: (a) IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov/W4App (most accurate); (b) Multiple Jobs Worksheet on page 3 (paper version of the same math); (c) Step 2(c) box on both W-4s (shortcut that only works when both jobs pay within ~15% of each other).

What is the dependent credit on the 2026 W-4?

$2,200 per qualifying child under 17 with a valid SSN (post-OBBBA §70102) plus $500 per Credit for Other Dependents recipient. Phases out at $400K MFJ / $200K other; enter $0 on Step 3 if you are above the full-phaseout point.

What goes on Step 4 of the W-4?

4(a) annual non-wage income you want covered by paycheck withholding; 4(b) annual itemized-plus-above-line deductions in excess of the standard; 4(c) per-paycheck (not annual) extra withholding amount.

When should I update my W-4 mid-year?

Within 10 days of marriage, divorce, birth or adoption, a dependent aging out, a spouse starting or stopping work, buying or selling a home, or receiving an IRS Lock-In Letter (Form 2800C). Also useful after large one-time events like bonuses, inheritances, or capital-gain realizations.

Can I claim Exempt on my 2026 W-4?

Only if you had no 2025 federal income tax liability and expect no 2026 federal income tax liability under IRC §3402(n). Most workers do not qualify. Exempt claims expire February 15 of the following year and must be re-filed to persist.

How do I fix a W-4 that under-withheld?

File a new W-4 with an increased Step 4(c) per-paycheck extra-withholding amount. Divide the projected shortfall by paychecks remaining in the year. Withholding counts as evenly paid across the year for §6654 penalty purposes, so late-year fixes still typically avoid the underpayment penalty.

What happens if I do not fill out a W-4?

The employer must withhold at the Single-with-no-adjustments default under Treas. Reg. §31.3402(f)(2)-1(a) — typically over-withholds, produces a large refund, never triggers an §6654 penalty.

How does the OBBBA change 2026 W-4 withholding?

Permanent TCJA rate brackets (§70101), $2,200 CTC per child (§70102), permanently higher standard deduction (§70103 extension). Employees who filed W-4s in 2020-2025 do not have to update, but the pre-2026 assumptions used to fine-tune Step 4(c) or Step 3 may now be off by $200-$500 per line.
