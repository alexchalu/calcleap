The IRC §2056(b)(7) statutory framework

IRC §2056(a) grants an unlimited marital deduction for property passing from the deceased spouse to the surviving spouse — the foundational rule that makes estate tax a two-death event rather than a one-death event. §2056(b)(1) narrows that grant with the terminable-interest rule: property passing to the surviving spouse for a limited period (a life estate, a term of years, or an interest that terminates on an event) does not qualify for the marital deduction, because the interest will "terminate" and thereafter someone other than the surviving spouse will receive the property. The terminable-interest rule prevents the surviving spouse's estate from being able to defer estate tax on property that ultimately passes to someone else.[4]

IRC §2056(b)(7) is the QTIP exception to the terminable-interest rule. It provides that a life estate — specifically, a "qualifying income interest for life" — in "qualified terminable interest property" qualifies for the marital deduction if the deceased spouse's executor makes an affirmative QTIP election on the estate's Form 706. The QTIP election is a deliberate choice by the executor to defer estate tax on the property until the surviving spouse's death, at which point the residual QTIP property is included in the surviving spouse's gross estate under IRC §2044. The QTIP mechanic thus preserves the deceased spouse's ability to control the ultimate remainder beneficiary of the QTIP property (typically the deceased spouse's children from a first marriage, or a class of descendants) while giving the surviving spouse income during her life and preserving the marital deduction at the deceased spouse's death.[5]

The four operative statutory phrases in §2056(b)(7) are:

  1. "Qualified terminable interest property" — property passing from the deceased spouse in which the surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life and to which the QTIP election has been made.
  2. "Qualifying income interest for life" — defined in §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii): the surviving spouse is entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals; no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse during the surviving spouse's life; the trust cannot direct payments to anyone other than the surviving spouse; and the surviving spouse's income entitlement is not subject to conditions that would allow the trustee to reduce or eliminate it.
  3. "Election" — the QTIP election is made on the deceased spouse's Form 706, Part IV (Property Passing to Surviving Spouse), by identifying the QTIP-electable property and affirmatively claiming the election. The election is irrevocable once the estate tax return is filed (Rev. Proc. 2001-38 provides a narrow relief mechanism for certain unnecessary QTIP elections).[6]
  4. "Passes from the decedent" — the property must pass from the decedent to the surviving spouse (or to a trust for the surviving spouse's benefit that satisfies the qualifying-income-interest test); property passing to a third party first and then to the surviving spouse does not qualify.

The qualifying-income-interest-for-life test in detail

The qualifying-income-interest-for-life test is the mechanical heart of the §2056(b)(7) analysis. It has three prongs: the income-entitlement prong (the surviving spouse must be entitled to all the income), the exclusivity-of-appointment prong (no person may have a power to appoint any part to anyone other than the surviving spouse during her life), and the payability prong (the income must be payable at least annually).[7]

The income-entitlement prong requires that the surviving spouse be "entitled to" — not merely "eligible for" or "permitted to receive" — all the income from the QTIP property. The distinction matters. A trust that gives the trustee discretion to distribute income to the surviving spouse fails the test because the surviving spouse is not entitled to the income; the trustee may withhold it. A trust that gives the trustee discretion to accumulate income and add it to principal similarly fails the test. A trust that entitles the surviving spouse to all the income and gives the trustee discretion only over principal satisfies the income-entitlement prong. A trust that entitles the surviving spouse to all the income and gives the trustee no discretion over anything satisfies the prong most clearly.

For an inherited IRA held in a trust, the income-entitlement prong operates at two levels. First, the trust must be drafted to entitle the surviving spouse to all the trust's accounting income. Second, the IRA distributions coming into the trust must be characterized in a manner that produces trust accounting income for the surviving spouse. If the trust instrument is drafted correctly — the surviving spouse is entitled to all the trust's accounting income — but state law characterizes 90 percent of the IRA distributions as principal under the classical 1997 UPIA §409 default, the surviving spouse's actual receipt from the IRA-attributable trust income is only 10 percent of the IRA distribution. The IRS held in Rev. Rul. 2006-26 that state law's characterization generally controls but only if it "reasonably apportions" between income and principal.[8]

The exclusivity-of-appointment prong prohibits any person — the trustee, the surviving spouse herself, a trust protector, or a third party — from having a power to appoint any part of the QTIP property to anyone other than the surviving spouse during the surviving spouse's life. This is a common drafting-error catch: a trust that gives the trustee discretion to make principal distributions to the deceased spouse's children during the surviving spouse's life fails the exclusivity prong, even if the trustee never actually exercises the discretion. The mere existence of the power defeats the QTIP qualification. The safest drafting is to prohibit all distributions to anyone other than the surviving spouse during her life; principal may be distributed after her death.

The payability prong requires that the income be payable at least annually. Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) elaborates: the income must be "current" income, not accumulated income, and must be distributed to the surviving spouse (or made available to her) at intervals not exceeding one year. A trust that pays income quarterly, monthly, or on the surviving spouse's demand satisfies the payability prong; a trust that pays income "when the trustee determines appropriate" or "at least once every five years" fails.

The exclusivity-of-appointment trap in blended-family drafting

Many blended-family QTIP trusts contain a well-intentioned but fatal drafting choice: they give the trustee discretion to make principal distributions to the surviving spouse or to the deceased spouse's children from a prior marriage, "in the trustee's discretion for the health, education, maintenance, or support of the beneficiary." The drafter's intent is to preserve flexibility for the trustee to help the children if a need arises. The federal-tax consequence is total loss of the QTIP election — the exclusivity-of-appointment prong is violated by the mere existence of the trustee's power to distribute to a non-spouse beneficiary during the surviving spouse's life, even if the trustee never exercises the power. The correct drafting is to prohibit all non-spouse distributions during the surviving spouse's life and to make the children remainder beneficiaries entitled to distributions only after her death. If flexibility for the children is desired during the surviving spouse's life, that flexibility must come from a different structure — a separate non-marital trust funded with the deceased spouse's exemption amount, or a lifetime family gift to the children — not from the QTIP trust itself.

Rev. Rul. 2000-2 and the retirement-benefits framework

Rev. Rul. 2000-2 addressed a specific mechanical question: how do the §2056(b)(7) qualifying-income-interest requirements apply to an IRA payable to a QTIP trust?[9] The ruling's factual pattern is the paradigm case: an IRA titled to the deceased spouse's revocable trust, which contained a QTIP marital-deduction subtrust for the surviving spouse. The revocable trust became irrevocable at the deceased spouse's death, the IRA distributions began flowing to the QTIP subtrust, and the executor of the deceased spouse's estate faced the question of whether and how to make the QTIP election with respect to the IRA.

The ruling reached three holdings that continue to control practice:

Holding 1: The IRA is itself a separate QTIP-eligible item. The QTIP election must be made separately with respect to the IRA on the deceased spouse's Form 706, in addition to any QTIP election made with respect to the trust or trust corpus. This means an executor making a QTIP election on Schedule M of Form 706 must identify both the trust and the IRA as separate QTIP-elected items. Failing to identify the IRA as a separate QTIP-elected item can result in the IRA being treated as non-QTIP property — with the estate tax immediately due on the IRA balance (rather than deferred until the surviving spouse's death).

Holding 2: The trust must satisfy the qualifying-income-interest test with respect to the IRA distributions. The trust must be drafted so that the surviving spouse is entitled to all the trust's income attributable to the IRA distributions, and the trust cannot give the trustee discretion to accumulate the IRA distributions or to distribute them to anyone other than the surviving spouse. The trust also cannot restrict the surviving spouse's ability to compel current distribution of the IRA-attributable income; a trust that pays the surviving spouse only on the trustee's determination or only at specified intervals longer than one year fails.

Holding 3: The trustee must distribute the greater of the IRA's income (determined for trust-accounting purposes) or the trust's actual receipt from the IRA. This is the operational rule Rev. Rul. 2000-2 imposed to prevent a mismatch between the trust's income characterization and the surviving spouse's actual receipt. If the trust's characterization rule under state law would produce a small annual income figure for the surviving spouse (say, 10 percent under the 1997 UPIA §409 default), but the trust actually receives a large IRA distribution (say, the year-10 required residual acceleration), the trustee must distribute the greater amount to the surviving spouse. This "greater of" rule effectively forces the trust to be a conduit for the year-10 acceleration if the state-law characterization would otherwise short-change the surviving spouse.

Rev. Rul. 2000-2 remains valid law in 2026 as the controlling authority on the IRA-plus-QTIP mechanic. Its practical operation has changed because the SECURE Act 10-year rule replaced the pre-SECURE stretch-IRA mechanic for non-EDB designated beneficiaries. Under the pre-SECURE stretch, the trust could pay out the IRA over the surviving spouse's remaining life expectancy — often 20-30 years — giving the QTIP mechanic ample time to smooth out year-over-year variation. Under the SECURE 10-year rule, the trust must fully distribute the IRA within 10 years, meaning the trustee's income-distribution obligations under Rev. Rul. 2000-2 must be satisfied ten times, not thirty.[10]

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 and the reasonable-apportionment framework

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 supplemented Rev. Rul. 2000-2 with a framework for evaluating the state-law characterization of IRA distributions between income and principal.[11] The ruling's core holding is that state law's characterization generally controls for QTIP qualification purposes, but only if the state-law rule "reasonably apportions" the IRA distribution between income and principal.

The reasonable-apportionment analysis is a facts-and-circumstances inquiry. The IRS identified three specific state-law characterization approaches and rated each against the reasonable-apportionment standard:

Approach 1 — the classical 1997 UPIA §409 default (10 percent income, 90 percent principal). The IRS held this approach satisfies the reasonable-apportionment standard because it apportions the IRA distribution between income and principal in a specific numerical fashion tied to the trust-accounting-income determination methodology used by the state's statute. The 10 percent characterization is small in absolute terms but reflects a defensible allocation between the account's internal-buildup component (principal) and the account's current-period return component (income).

Approach 2 — the §104 power-to-adjust or §106 unitrust conversion (variable annual amount, 3-5 percent of value). The IRS held these approaches also satisfy the reasonable-apportionment standard because the trustee's factor analysis under §104 or the state's unitrust rate under §106 produces an amount within the same 3-5 percent range that the classical accounting doctrine would produce over long-run averages. The trustee's exercise of §104 or the state's §106 mechanic thus produces a "reasonable apportionment" for QTIP purposes.

Approach 3 — a state rule that characterizes 100 percent of the IRA distribution as principal (leaving the surviving spouse with no income). The IRS held this approach fails the reasonable-apportionment standard. A state rule that leaves the surviving spouse with no income from the IRA effectively eliminates the qualifying-income-interest for the QTIP portion attributable to the IRA — the surviving spouse gets nothing, so the marital-deduction rationale fails. Any state rule producing this result would disqualify the QTIP election for the IRA portion.

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 also confirmed the operational relationship between the state-law characterization and the Rev. Rul. 2000-2 "greater of" rule. The trustee must distribute to the surviving spouse the greater of (i) the state-law-characterized income amount and (ii) the trust's actual IRA receipt for the year. A trust in a §104 state that produces a $50,000 §104 reallocation from a $100,000 IRA receipt must distribute the full $100,000 (not the $50,000) to the surviving spouse if the state-law amount is less than the actual receipt. This "greater of" rule prevents the trustee from using state-law characterization to short-change the surviving spouse.

The Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) state-unitrust safe harbor

Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f)(1) provides the trustee's operational off-ramp from the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 annual reasonable-apportionment analysis: the state-unitrust safe harbor. Under the safe harbor, a state-law unitrust conversion satisfying four specific conditions produces an income stream that qualifies for QTIP purposes without a per-year reasonable-apportionment inquiry.[12]

The safe harbor's practical consequence is substantial. Absent the safe harbor, a trustee converting a QTIP trust to unitrust would still need to perform an annual Rev. Rul. 2006-26 reasonable-apportionment analysis to confirm the unitrust distribution qualifies for QTIP purposes each year — a burden roughly equivalent to the §104 per-year factor-analysis burden. With the safe harbor, the trustee performs the reasonable-apportionment analysis once at conversion time and confirms the state-law procedural compliance each year on the trust's fiduciary income-tax return. The recurring burden is dramatically lower.

The four conditions are conjunctive — all four must be met, or the safe harbor does not apply. A trustee choosing a 6 percent unitrust rate under a state statute permitting rates up to 7 percent would fall outside the rate condition and lose the safe harbor. A trustee using a valuation methodology of "the trust's book value as recorded five years ago" would fall outside the valuation condition. A trustee converting under a state law that does not expressly authorize unitrust conversion (rare but possible in the few remaining UPIA 1962-only states) would fall outside the state-authorization condition. A trustee who fails to give the state-required advance notice would fall outside the state-compliance condition. Any one failure moves the trust out of the safe harbor and back into the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 annual reasonable-apportionment analysis for QTIP purposes.

The reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3) for GST purposes

IRC §2652(a)(3) permits the executor of the deceased spouse's estate to elect to treat the QTIP property as if the QTIP election had not been made — solely for generation-skipping transfer (GST) tax purposes. The election is called the reverse-QTIP election, and its purpose is GST exemption utilization.[13]

The default GST transferor rule under IRC §2652(a) treats the surviving spouse as the transferor of QTIP property at her death (because the property is included in her gross estate under §2044, and the §2044 inclusion triggers §2652(a)(1)'s "if a decedent" test with respect to the surviving spouse). This means the deceased spouse's GST exemption is not allocated to the QTIP property and — because no GST-taxable event occurs until the surviving spouse's death — the deceased spouse's GST exemption is effectively wasted on the QTIP portion. If the ultimate remainder beneficiaries are grandchildren or great-grandchildren (a common blended-family structure), the surviving spouse's GST exemption is the only exemption available to shield the property from the 40 percent GST tax at her death.

The reverse-QTIP election restores the deceased spouse as the GST transferor for the elected property. The election permits the deceased spouse's GST exemption to be allocated to the QTIP property on the deceased spouse's Form 706 (via Schedule R), and that exemption then shields the property from GST tax at the surviving spouse's death. The economic result is that both spouses' GST exemptions are available to shield QTIP property from GST tax — a doubling of the effective GST exemption from $15 million (surviving spouse's only) to $30 million (both spouses'), which materially matters for high-net-worth estates with grandchildren as intended ultimate beneficiaries.

The election has three specific mechanical rules. First, it must be made on the deceased spouse's Form 706, Schedule R, at the same time as the QTIP election itself; it cannot be added later or made on the surviving spouse's Form 706. Second, it applies only to the property specifically identified in the election — a partial reverse-QTIP election is permitted for a subset of the QTIP property. Third, it operates only for GST purposes; the estate-tax deferral of the QTIP election itself is unaffected.

The reverse-QTIP election is a "free" GST doubling for blended-family estates with grandchildren beneficiaries

The reverse-QTIP election has essentially no downside for the deceased spouse's estate — it doesn't accelerate any tax, it doesn't reduce the marital deduction, it doesn't affect the surviving spouse's income entitlement. Its only cost is the deceased spouse's GST exemption allocation, which would otherwise be wasted on non-generation-skipping bequests to the surviving spouse or to non-skip-persons. For a blended-family estate where the ultimate remainder beneficiaries of the QTIP property include grandchildren or great-grandchildren, the reverse-QTIP election is almost always the right choice — and its omission from the deceased spouse's Form 706 is often the highest-value estate-tax planning error identified in the surviving spouse's estate-tax return preparation many years later. If you are preparing a Form 706 for a first-marriage decedent whose QTIP trust ultimate remainder beneficiaries include grandchildren, verify the reverse-QTIP election is on Schedule R before filing.

The $15 million OBBBA exemption and its interaction with QTIP planning

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed by the President on July 4, 2025, permanently increased the federal estate and GST exemption to $15 million per person for decedents dying after December 31, 2025.[14] The exemption is indexed for inflation and stands at $15 million for 2026 per Rev. Proc. 2025-32. The OBBBA replaced the sunset that would have reverted the exemption to approximately $7 million on January 1, 2026 under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act's original terms.

The $15 million exemption fundamentally changes the QTIP planning calculus for most estates. Under the pre-OBBBA regime — either the $13.61 million 2024 exemption or the projected 2026 sunset of $7 million — many surviving spouses would face material federal estate tax at their deaths without QTIP planning, and the QTIP election's estate-tax deferral was correspondingly valuable. Under the $15 million exemption, most surviving spouses will not face federal estate tax at their deaths regardless of QTIP planning, and the federal estate-tax deferral value of the QTIP election is correspondingly reduced.

Three considerations continue to make the QTIP election valuable at the $15 million exemption:

State estate tax. Twelve states plus the District of Columbia impose state estate taxes at exemption levels well below the federal $15 million: Massachusetts $2 million, Oregon $1 million (though rising to $2 million in 2027 under SB 1063), Washington $2.193 million, Minnesota $3 million, Rhode Island $1.775 million, Connecticut $13.61 million (matching federal), Hawaii $5.49 million, Illinois $4 million, Maine $6.8 million, Maryland $5 million, New York $6.94 million, and Vermont $5 million.[15] A surviving spouse dying in New York with a $10 million estate is well above the state's $6.94 million exemption but well below the federal $15 million exemption; a QTIP election that defers the state-level tax until the surviving spouse's death — combined with appropriate state QTIP election coordination — can be materially valuable.

Blended-family remainder-beneficiary control. The QTIP election is often chosen not for tax deferral but for control over the ultimate remainder beneficiaries. A first-marriage decedent who wants his children from a first marriage — not his second-marriage surviving spouse — to receive the residual IRA balance after the surviving spouse's death must use a QTIP trust to achieve that result. An outright transfer or spousal rollover would give the surviving spouse full control over the ultimate remainder beneficiaries, allowing her to redirect the IRA to her own children from a prior marriage or to her own descendants exclusively. The QTIP trust's dispositive language is the deceased spouse's guarantee of remainder-beneficiary control, and it operates regardless of estate-tax deferral value.

Reverse-QTIP GST exemption utilization. As discussed above, the reverse-QTIP election doubles the effective GST exemption available to shield the property from GST tax at the surviving spouse's death. For an estate with grandchildren or great-grandchildren as ultimate remainder beneficiaries, this doubling matters even at the $15 million estate-tax exemption because the GST exemption is a separate calculation and its doubling produces a separately-valuable shield against a 40 percent GST tax at the surviving spouse's death.

The SECURE Act 10-year rule interaction

The SECURE Act's 10-year rule for non-EDB designated beneficiaries creates three specific QTIP-planning challenges for trusts holding inherited IRAs. All three arise from the compression of the IRA's drawdown from the pre-SECURE stretch (often 20-30 years) into a 10-year window that must be fully complete by December 31 of the year containing the 10th anniversary of the account owner's death.[16]

Challenge 1 — annual income-distribution timing. The trust must actually distribute the IRA income to the surviving spouse each year within the 10-year window. Under Rev. Rul. 2000-2 and Rev. Rul. 2006-26, the surviving spouse's income entitlement is measured on the trust's actual receipt each year. The trustee cannot retain the IRA distributions in the trust for post-10-year accumulation, cannot cap the annual distribution at a fixed dollar amount independent of the IRA's actual receipt, and cannot defer distribution until the surviving spouse's death. The surviving spouse must receive the income each year, and the income must be characterized by state-law rules that satisfy the reasonable-apportionment standard.

Challenge 2 — the year-10 residual acceleration. The final year's residual distribution — the full remaining balance of the IRA that must be distributed by December 31 of year 10 — is typically 25-40 percent of the original balance depending on market performance and prior distributions. This large one-year distribution must be characterized under state-law rules and, under Rev. Rul. 2000-2's "greater of" rule, distributed to the surviving spouse if it exceeds the state-law-characterized income amount. A trust using a §104 power-to-adjust reallocation that produces a $50,000 annual income figure but faces a $500,000 year-10 residual distribution must distribute the full $500,000 to the surviving spouse under the "greater of" rule; retaining the residual as principal fails the QTIP test for year 10.

Challenge 3 — the IRC §2519 gift-treatment risk. If the surviving spouse and the trustee agree during her lifetime to distribute part of the year-10 residual to remainder beneficiaries (typically to reduce the surviving spouse's own gross estate at her eventual death), the surviving spouse is treated under IRC §2519 as having made a gift of the entire remaining QTIP property to the remainder beneficiaries — even if the actual distribution is small. This is the third challenge that arose only after the 10-year rule created large year-10 distributions the surviving spouse might not need for her own support: the temptation to consent to a remainder-beneficiary distribution and the corresponding §2519 gift-tax trap.[17]

The year-10 §2519 trap

The most dangerous single-move estate-tax mistake in a QTIP-elected inherited-IRA trust is the year-10 distribution to a remainder beneficiary with the surviving spouse's consent. The surviving spouse may reason that she does not need the year-10 residual for her own support, that the remainder beneficiaries (typically the deceased spouse's children from a first marriage) are the ultimate intended recipients anyway, and that distributing the residual to them accelerates the eventual distribution. All three reasons are defensible on the substance. The problem is IRC §2519: her consent to the distribution is treated as a gift of the entire remaining QTIP property — potentially $2-3 million or more — to the remainder beneficiaries at the moment of consent, consuming her own $15 million gift-tax exemption and potentially triggering a 40 percent federal gift tax on any excess. The correct approach is either (i) the surviving spouse takes the year-10 residual herself, retains it or invests it in her own name, and passes it to the remainder beneficiaries only at her eventual death (when it will be in her gross estate but sheltered by her $15 million estate-tax exemption), or (ii) the trust is drafted to require the year-10 residual to pass to the remainder beneficiaries without her consent (a structure that requires careful qualifying-income-interest drafting during years 1-9 to avoid a §2056(b)(7) qualification defect).

Worked case study: $6 million inherited IRA in a QTIP trust

A concrete case study illustrates the mechanic. Consider Michael, who died on January 15, 2026 at age 76 (past his required beginning date). Michael's revocable trust contained a QTIP marital-deduction subtrust with a $6,000,000 inherited IRA titled to it. Michael's surviving spouse is Sarah (age 73). The QTIP subtrust names Sarah as the income beneficiary and Michael's two children from his first marriage — Christopher (age 48) and Andrea (age 45) — as remainder beneficiaries. The trust is in New York and Sarah is a New York resident.

The 10-year window ends December 31, 2036. Under Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4(f), Sarah (as the trust beneficiary, applying the see-through trust rules) must take annual RMDs in years 1-9 and the full residual balance by December 31 of year 10. Assume the IRA grows at 6 percent per year net of distributions. Michael's executor is planning the deceased-spouse Form 706 filings and needs to decide four things: (1) whether to make the QTIP election, (2) whether to make the reverse-QTIP election, (3) whether the trust satisfies the qualifying-income-interest-for-life test as drafted, and (4) what state-law characterization mechanic the trustee should elect to satisfy Rev. Rul. 2006-26.

The case study surfaces three planning conclusions. First, the "greater of" rule in Rev. Rul. 2000-2 dominates in every year of this fact pattern. The 4 percent New York unitrust distribution (based on the three-year smoothed average) is materially less than the annual RMD in every year 1-9 and dramatically less than the year-10 residual acceleration. Sarah receives the annual RMD in years 1-9 (approximately $250,000 to $517,000, growing as the account depletes and the life-expectancy-table denominator declines) and the full $5,795,000 residual in year 10. Her total ten-year receipt is approximately $9,634,000 gross (the IRA is fully distributed to her).

Second, the reverse-QTIP election should be made on Michael's Form 706. Michael and Sarah's ultimate estate plan contemplates the residual passing to their combined grandchildren after Sarah's death. The reverse-QTIP election allows Michael's $15 million GST exemption to be allocated to the QTIP-elected IRA at Michael's death, shielding the residual balance from GST tax at Sarah's eventual death when the grandchildren-remainder-beneficiary distributions occur. Without the reverse-QTIP election, only Sarah's own $15 million GST exemption would be available at her death, and it would be spread across her entire estate (both QTIP property and her own separately-owned property).

Third, the trust as drafted must be reviewed for QTIP qualification. The trust must (i) entitle Sarah to all trust income each year (payable annually or more frequently), (ii) prohibit any distribution to Christopher, Andrea, or any other non-Sarah beneficiary during Sarah's lifetime, (iii) permit Sarah to compel current distribution of accumulated income if any, and (iv) not condition Sarah's income on any trustee discretion. If the trust as drafted contains a discretionary-principal clause permitting distributions to Christopher and Andrea during Sarah's life, the trust must be reformed (via non-judicial settlement agreement or court proceeding) before the QTIP election is made — or the QTIP election will fail and the entire $6 million IRA will be subject to estate tax at Michael's death.

At New York's 2026 state estate tax rate (top marginal rate approximately 16 percent above the $6.94 million exemption), Michael's non-QTIP estate would face state estate tax of approximately $150,000-$300,000 on a $6 million IRA alone (depending on other estate assets). The QTIP election defers this to Sarah's death, when the balance is generally lower (Sarah has received substantial distributions during the 10-year window). The QTIP election also defers the federal estate tax to Sarah's death, though the federal exposure at Sarah's death is minimal at the $15 million exemption. The combined federal-plus-state deferral value in this case study is real — approximately $200,000-$400,000 of estate tax deferred from Michael's 2026 death to Sarah's eventual death, plus the doubled GST exemption from the reverse-QTIP election.

Seven recurring QTIP-with-inherited-IRA mistakes

The estate-tax return audit record and the fiduciary-litigation record surface seven recurring mistakes on QTIP elections for trust-held inherited IRAs. Each is avoidable with attention at Form 706 preparation time or trust reformation time before the QTIP election is finalized.

Mistake 1 — failing to make a separate QTIP election for the IRA. The Rev. Rul. 2000-2 holding requires the IRA to be identified as a separate QTIP-elected item on the deceased spouse's Form 706 Schedule M. An executor who identifies the trust as QTIP-elected but does not separately identify the IRA may find, on audit, that the IRA is not QTIP-elected and the estate tax is due on the IRA balance at the deceased spouse's death. The fix is to identify both the trust and the IRA in the QTIP election, following the Rev. Rul. 2000-2 format.

Mistake 2 — drafting the trust with trustee discretion to accumulate IRA distributions. A trust that gives the trustee discretion over whether to distribute the IRA-attributable income to the surviving spouse fails the qualifying-income-interest test. The surviving spouse must be entitled to the income; the trustee cannot have discretion to withhold it. The correct drafting entitles the surviving spouse to all the trust's income and gives the trustee discretion only over principal (subject to the IRC §2519 restriction on principal distributions to non-spouse beneficiaries during the surviving spouse's life).

Mistake 3 — converting to unitrust at a rate outside 3-5 percent. A trustee electing a state-unitrust rate outside the 3-5 percent Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor loses the safe harbor and must revert to the Rev. Rul. 2006-26 annual reasonable-apportionment analysis. All ten surveyed non-UFIPA state statutes fall within the safe harbor at default rates (4 percent), so the mistake is rare — but a Texas or Ohio trustee choosing a rate above 5 percent under the state's discretionary range would fall outside the safe harbor.

Mistake 4 — failing to make the reverse-QTIP election. The reverse-QTIP election under IRC §2652(a)(3) is a separate line on Form 706 Schedule R. An executor who makes the QTIP election but omits the reverse-QTIP election forfeits the deceased spouse's GST exemption on the QTIP property, wasting up to $15 million of GST-exempt capacity for the ultimate remainder-beneficiary transfer. The mistake compounds because the reverse-QTIP election cannot be added after Form 706 is filed.

Mistake 5 — no plan for the year-10 acceleration. A trust that has not planned for the year-10 SECURE Act residual acceleration will find, in year 10, that a large residual balance must be distributed and that the trust's ordinary income-distribution mechanism (§104 factor analysis, §106 unitrust smoothing) does not capture the residual as income for the surviving spouse. The Rev. Rul. 2000-2 "greater of" rule requires the residual to be distributed to the surviving spouse if it exceeds the state-law-characterized income; failing to plan produces a scramble at year 10.

Mistake 6 — surviving spouse's consent to a year-10 principal distribution. As discussed in the §2519 callout above, the surviving spouse's consent to a distribution of QTIP principal to a remainder beneficiary during her lifetime triggers the constructive-gift treatment of the entire remaining QTIP property. The mistake is often made by well-intentioned family members trying to accelerate the remainder-beneficiary inheritance; the tax consequence can exceed $1 million on a $3-5 million QTIP trust.

Mistake 7 — state QTIP mismatch. Several states permit or require a state QTIP election that differs from the federal QTIP election. New York permits a state-only QTIP election that may differ from the federal QTIP amount; Illinois similarly permits a state-only QTIP election. A federal QTIP election that does not coordinate with the state QTIP election can produce a federal-state mismatch that leaves state estate tax due at the deceased spouse's death (rather than deferred to the surviving spouse's death). The DSUEA portability workaround may address the mismatch in some situations, but it is state-specific and requires attention at the state Form 706 equivalent.

Pre-election action checklist

The 8-item pre-QTIP-election checklist for a trust-held inherited IRA

Complete each item before filing the deceased spouse's Form 706. Retain the completed checklist and the executor's contemporaneous notes for the statute of limitations plus a reasonable margin.

  1. Confirm the trust satisfies the qualifying-income-interest test. Read the trust instrument for the four §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) requirements: surviving spouse entitled to all income, no non-spouse-appointment power during her life, income payable annually or more frequently, no accumulation discretion. If any requirement is not met, reform the trust before filing Form 706 (via non-judicial settlement agreement, court reformation, or trust protector action if the instrument authorizes).
  2. Confirm the IRA is properly titled to the trust. The IRA custodian's beneficiary designation must name the trust (not the surviving spouse individually), and the trust must be the see-through trust satisfying Treas. Reg. §1.401(a)(9)-4(f) requirements. Verify beneficiary designation with the custodian in writing.
  3. Prepare the Rev. Rul. 2000-2-compliant Form 706 Schedule M identification. Identify both the trust and the IRA as separately QTIP-elected items on Schedule M, tracking the Rev. Rul. 2000-2 format. Document the fair market values of both items at the alternate valuation date if applicable.
  4. Evaluate and make the reverse-QTIP election. If the ultimate remainder beneficiaries include grandchildren or great-grandchildren, prepare the reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R and allocate the deceased spouse's GST exemption. The election is essentially free and doubles the effective GST exemption; omission is a common high-value error.
  5. Select the state-law characterization mechanic. Identify the trust's state of situs and the state's §106-style unitrust conversion statute. Confirm the trustee's ability to invoke the Treas. Reg. §20.2056(b)-5(f) safe harbor at a 3-5 percent rate. Alternatively, plan for annual §104 power-to-adjust reallocations with the required nine-factor contemporaneous memorandum.
  6. Model the year-10 acceleration. Project the year-10 residual IRA balance under reasonable investment assumptions. Confirm the trustee's plan for handling the year-10 residual — supplemental §104 adjustment, distribution to the surviving spouse per the "greater of" rule, or another approach — is documented at Form 706 preparation time.
  7. Coordinate with state estate tax filings. Identify the state estate tax exposure at both the deceased spouse's death and the surviving spouse's projected death. Make any required state-only QTIP election on the state Form 706 equivalent. Confirm the state-tax deferral value of the QTIP election exceeds any state-tax cost.
  8. Document the executor's reasoning. Prepare a contemporaneous memorandum explaining the QTIP election decision, the reverse-QTIP election decision, the state-law characterization choice, and the year-10 residual plan. The memorandum becomes the record on audit if the IRS challenges any of the elections.

Frequently asked questions about IRC §2056(b)(7) QTIP and inherited IRAs

What is the IRC §2056(b)(7) QTIP marital deduction and why does it matter for inherited IRAs?

IRC §2056(b)(7) grants the deceased spouse's estate an unlimited marital deduction for property passing to a trust in which the surviving spouse holds a qualifying income interest for life, provided the executor makes an affirmative QTIP election on the estate's Form 706. The election defers estate tax on the QTIP property until the surviving spouse's death, at which point the residual QTIP value is included in her gross estate under IRC §2044. For an inherited IRA titled to a trust, the QTIP election is the primary tool for deferring estate tax on the IRA balance while ensuring the surviving spouse receives the IRA's income during her life and the deceased spouse's chosen remainder beneficiaries receive whatever remains after her death.

What is the qualifying-income-interest-for-life test?

The test requires that the surviving spouse be entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals, and that no person have a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse during the surviving spouse's life. See IRC §2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). For a trust holding an inherited IRA, this operates on two levels: the trust must satisfy the test with respect to trust corpus, and the IRA distributions must be characterized as trust accounting income in a manner that produces meaningful income for the surviving spouse under state-law characterization rules.

How does Rev. Rul. 2000-2 apply?

Rev. Rul. 2000-2 held that an IRA payable to a QTIP trust can qualify for the marital deduction if both the trust satisfies the §2056(b)(7) qualifying-income-interest-for-life test and the executor makes a QTIP election with respect to both the IRA and the trust separately. The ruling also imposed the "greater of" rule: the trustee must distribute to the surviving spouse annually the greater of the IRA's income (determined for trust-accounting purposes) or the trust's actual receipt from the IRA.

What does Rev. Rul. 2006-26 require?

Rev. Rul. 2006-26 held that state law's characterization of IRA distributions as income versus principal generally controls for QTIP qualification, but only if the state-law rule "reasonably apportions" between income and principal. The classical 1997 UPIA §409 90/10 default satisfies the reasonable-apportionment standard, as do the §104 power-to-adjust and §106 unitrust conversion mechanics. A rule that characterizes 100 percent of the IRA distribution as principal (leaving the surviving spouse with no income) fails the standard.

What is the reverse-QTIP election?

IRC §2652(a)(3) permits the executor to elect to treat the QTIP property as if the QTIP election had not been made — solely for GST purposes. The election restores the deceased spouse as the GST transferor for the elected property, allowing the deceased spouse's GST exemption to be allocated to the QTIP property and preserved for the eventual grandchildren-beneficiary transfer. It must be made on the deceased spouse's Form 706 Schedule R at the same time as the QTIP election.

How does the $15M OBBBA exemption affect QTIP planning?

The OBBBA (signed July 4, 2025) permanently increased the exemption to $15 million per person for decedents dying after December 31, 2025. This reduces the federal-estate-tax deferral value of the QTIP election for most estates. The QTIP election continues to matter for (i) state estate taxes with lower exemptions (New York $6.94M, Massachusetts $2M, Oregon $1M, etc.), (ii) blended-family remainder-beneficiary control, and (iii) reverse-QTIP GST exemption utilization at the deceased spouse's death.

How does the SECURE Act 10-year rule change QTIP planning?

Three challenges: (i) annual income-distribution timing — the trustee must actually distribute IRA income to the surviving spouse each year within the 10-year window, cannot accumulate; (ii) year-10 residual acceleration — the final-year residual (25-40% of the balance) must be characterized as income and distributed to the surviving spouse under the Rev. Rul. 2000-2 "greater of" rule; (iii) IRC §2519 gift-treatment risk — the surviving spouse's consent to a year-10 principal distribution to a remainder beneficiary is a constructive gift of the entire remaining QTIP property.

What is IRC §2519 and why is it dangerous?

IRC §2519 treats a disposition by the surviving spouse of any part of her qualifying-income-interest in QTIP property as a transfer by her of all the underlying property. The surviving spouse's consent to a distribution of QTIP principal to a remainder beneficiary during her lifetime — even a small distribution — is a constructive gift of the entire remaining QTIP property. Rev. Rul. 98-8 and PLR 200030030 confirm this. The gift-tax consequence can exceed $1 million on a $3-5 million QTIP trust.

What are the most common QTIP-with-inherited-IRA mistakes?

Seven: (1) failing to make a separate QTIP election for the IRA on Schedule M; (2) drafting the trust with trustee discretion to accumulate IRA distributions; (3) converting to unitrust at a rate outside 3-5%; (4) failing to make the reverse-QTIP election on Schedule R; (5) no plan for the year-10 acceleration; (6) the surviving spouse's consent to a year-10 principal distribution triggering §2519; (7) state QTIP mismatch where a state-only QTIP election is required and the federal election does not coordinate.

Should I use a QTIP trust for a first-marriage inherited IRA in 2026?

For a first-marriage situation with both spouses' children as ultimate beneficiaries, the QTIP trust is generally not necessary — the surviving spouse can take the IRA as a spousal rollover under IRC §408(d)(3)(C) and pass the balance to the children on her death. For a blended-family situation with a first-marriage decedent and children from a first marriage, the QTIP trust is often the right answer because it lets the deceased spouse control who receives the balance after the surviving spouse's death. The QTIP-versus-rollover decision turns on remainder-beneficiary control, not on estate-tax deferral, especially at the $15 million exemption. Discuss with an estate-planning attorney who understands both the SECURE Act 10-year rule and IRC §2056(b)(7).
