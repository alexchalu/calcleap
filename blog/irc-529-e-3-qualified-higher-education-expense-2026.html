IRC §529(c)(3)(B) Qualified Higher Education Expenses in 2026: The §529(e)(3) Framework, the OBBBA §70443 K-12 $20,000 Expansion, the Room-and-Board Ceiling, the Required-vs-Optional-Fee Line, and Every Anti-Double-Benefit Coordination

Yesterday's IRC §127 employer-provided educational assistance piece walked the employer-side channel for tax-free education support, and closed by naming §25A/§529 anti-double-benefit coordination as the friction point between the employer channel and the saver-side §529 channel. This piece walks the saver-side qualified-expense definition end to end. Every tax-free §529 dollar has to survive §529(c)(3)(B) — the includability rule that measures the distribution against the beneficiary's qualified higher education expenses for the year — and every "qualified higher education expense" itself has to survive the §529(e)(3) tests. Coverage: the §529(e)(3)(A)(i) tuition and required-fees line and how it excludes optional student-life fees, the §529(e)(3)(A)(ii) books/supplies/equipment "required for enrollment" test, the §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) computer/peripheral/software/internet inclusion made permanent by PATH Act 2015 §302, the §529(e)(3)(A)(iv) special-needs services, the §529(e)(3)(B) room-and-board ceiling anchored to the institution's published cost of attendance, the §529(c)(7) K-12 tuition expansion (TCJA 2017 $10,000 tuition-only → OBBBA §70443 doubled to $20,000 effective 2026 and broadened to tutoring/test prep/therapy/curriculum), the §529(c)(8) apprenticeship channel, the §529(c)(9) education-loan repayment ($10,000 lifetime cap), and the §25A/§221/§117 scholarship anti-double-benefit rules that determine what portion of any distribution is actually qualified. Distributions from a §529 qualified tuition program are tax-free for federal income-tax purposes only to the extent the distribution equals or is less than the beneficiary's qualified higher education expenses for the year. That is the rule of §529(c)(3)(A). §529(c)(3)(B) — the mirror-image includability rule this piece walks — says what happens when the distribution exceeds those expenses: a proportionate share of the earnings portion (determined under §72's basis-and-earnings ratio applied at the §529 account level) is includable in the distributee's gross income, and — absent a §529(c)(6) exception — subject to a 10% additional tax.[1] The two clauses lean on a single load-bearing definition: what counts as a "qualified higher education expense." That definition lives at IRC §529(e)(3), supplemented by the K-12 category at §529(c)(7), the apprenticeship category at §529(c)(8), and the education-loan-repayment category at §529(c)(9). Every §529 planning conversation eventually reduces to the same question: is this expense qualified or not?

The question is more procedurally complex than it looks. A single semester at a state university may involve $8,000 of tuition (qualified), $600 of required activity and technology fees (qualified), $175 of an optional parking pass (not qualified), $1,200 of textbooks and lab manuals required by the course syllabus (qualified), $340 of an off-syllabus reading enrichment book the student bought because they wanted to (not qualified), $1,050 of a laptop the student uses primarily for coursework (qualified under the PATH Act 2015 permanence of §529(e)(3)(A)(iii)), $95/month of home internet service (qualified in proportion to the student's primary use), $1,050 of the fall-semester on-campus meal plan through the institution's dining services (qualified up to the institution's cost-of-attendance ceiling), $180/month of the student's off-campus grocery spending after they move off-campus in year 2 (qualified only up to the institution's published cost-of-attendance room-and-board allowance for off-campus students), $2,500 spent on the Lifetime Learning Credit's qualifying tuition and required fees that the parent already claimed on Form 8863 (must be subtracted from qualified §529 expenses under §25A(g)(2)), and $3,000 of a merit scholarship reported on Form 1098-T box 5 (must be subtracted from qualified §529 expenses under §529(c)(3)(B)(v)). The §529 plan administrator does not perform any of this coordination. The beneficiary or account owner performs it — and if the math comes out wrong, the earnings portion of the excess becomes taxable ordinary income plus a 10% additional tax on the beneficiary's next return.[2]

Yesterday's §127 piece flagged this coordination as the friction point between the employer-side education channel and the saver-side §529 channel. This piece is the primer on the saver-side rules. It walks the §529(e)(3) framework category by category, unpacks the two dollar limits that materially changed in 2026 (the OBBBA §70443 K-12 doubling and the sibling extension of the §529(c)(9) loan repayment), maps the anti-double-benefit landscape with §25A/§221/§117 in a single decision table, and closes with three worked case studies that walk realistic dollar flows through the qualification analysis. This is the piece a practitioner sends to a family the first time they take a §529 distribution — the reference that turns the plan administrator's Form 1099-Q into a defensible tax position.

The §529(c)(3)(B) includability rule and how it depends on §529(e)(3)

The mechanics of §529(c)(3)(B) are compact but consequential. Where distributions from a §529 account in a taxable year exceed the beneficiary's aggregate qualified higher education expenses (adjusted for scholarships, credits, and other tax-free education assistance), the excess distribution's earnings portion is includable in the distributee's gross income. The earnings ratio is determined under §72 at the account level: for a §529 account with cumulative basis of $80,000 and current-year balance of $220,000, the earnings ratio is ($220,000 − $80,000) / $220,000 = 63.6%. If a $10,000 distribution exceeds qualified expenses by $2,500, the taxable earnings portion of that excess is $2,500 × 63.6% = $1,590. That $1,590 is added to the distributee's ordinary income and — under §529(c)(6) — a 10% additional tax of $159 also applies unless a §529(c)(6)(B) exception (scholarship, VA education benefit, tax-free educational assistance, death, disability, or attendance at a U.S. service academy) shields it.[3]

The distributee — the person on whose taxpayer identification number the Form 1099-Q is issued — is either the account owner or the designated beneficiary, depending on who initiated the distribution. Most §529 plan administrators default to issuing the 1099-Q to whoever wrote the check-request or authorized the ACH pull. If the distribution is used for qualified expenses of the beneficiary, best practice is to issue it directly to the beneficiary or to the eligible educational institution — this puts the income-inclusion risk (if any) on the beneficiary's return, where the marginal bracket is typically lower than the parent's.[4]

The remainder of this piece defines "qualified higher education expenses" — the number that goes in the denominator of the §529(c)(3)(B) test. Every category that follows is a category that can appear in that number.

IRC §529(c)(3)(B) — Distributions in excess of qualified higher education expenses

If aggregate distributions during a taxable year exceed the qualified higher education expenses of the designated beneficiary, then the includable earnings portion is computed under the §72 basis-and-earnings ratio applied to the excess. Adjustments for scholarships, credits, and other tax-free educational assistance are made under §529(c)(3)(B)(iv) and (v). Only the qualified higher education expenses defined in §529(e)(3) (plus §529(c)(7) K-12 tuition, §529(c)(8) apprenticeship fees, and §529(c)(9) education-loan repayments) reduce the excess-distribution calculation.

§529(e)(3)(A)(i) tuition and the required-fees vs optional-fees line

§529(e)(3)(A)(i) is the primary qualified-expense category and covers "tuition, fees, books, supplies, and equipment required for enrollment or attendance of a designated beneficiary at an eligible educational institution." The tuition prong is straightforward — the amount the institution charges as tuition, whether at the in-state resident rate, the out-of-state resident rate, or the international/private-institution posted rate, is qualified. The fees prong is where the analysis narrows.[5]

"Required for enrollment or attendance" excludes any charge the student can elect to skip. Modern universities layer dozens of fee lines on top of tuition on the semester bill. Only the ones the institution requires all enrolled students to pay are qualified. The rest — even when they appear on the same bill — are not.

The books, supplies, and equipment sub-prong

The §529(e)(3)(A)(i) sub-prong for books, supplies, and equipment layers a "required for enrollment or attendance" test on top. The best documentation is the course syllabus that names the specific book or equipment as required. A course that lists a textbook as "recommended" or "supplementary" does not push that book into the qualified-expense category; a course that lists it as "required" does. Institutions increasingly publish syllabus-required-materials lists on the same page as the course registration, and these lists — printed and retained — are the strongest documentation for §529(e)(3)(A)(i)(ii) qualification.[5]

Supplies are also qualified when required. Lab notebooks, a specific-model scientific calculator required for engineering coursework (a TI-89 or TI-Nspire for a calculus sequence), specialty pencils and drawing paper for an architecture drafting course, professional-grade paints for a fine-arts program, a lab coat for a biology sequence, safety glasses for a chemistry sequence — all qualify. Supplies not required by the syllabus (a nicer notebook the student prefers, a second set of drawing materials for personal practice) do not qualify.

Equipment "required for enrollment or attendance" is the narrowest sub-prong. Musical instruments required for a music-program major, industrial-machining equipment required for a machining-program apprenticeship, medical-equipment kits required for a nursing or medical-school program, a specific-model camera required for a photography or film program — these are qualified. A general-purpose musical instrument the student wanted to purchase for personal enrichment is not qualified. The specific-course or specific-program requirement is the deciding factor.

§529(e)(3)(A)(iii) computer, peripheral, software, and internet expenses

This is the modern-usage category that materially expanded §529's practical utility. Before 2009, computers were not §529-qualified expenses. ARRA 2009 §1005 added a temporary provision (through the 2010 tax year) that treated computers and peripheral equipment as §529(e)(3) qualified expenses. ATRA 2012 §724 reintroduced the provision for 2011-2013. PATH Act 2015 §302 codified §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) permanently, effective for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2014.[6] The statutory text of §529(e)(3)(A)(iii):

IRC §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) — Computer and peripheral equipment, software, and internet access

Expenses for the purchase of computer or peripheral equipment (as defined in §168(i)(2)(B)), computer software (as defined in §197(e)(3)(B)), or internet access and related services, if such equipment, software, or services are to be used primarily by the beneficiary during any of the years the beneficiary is enrolled at an eligible educational institution.

Three tests apply. First, the property or service must fall into one of the three enumerated types: computer/peripheral equipment (§168(i)(2)(B) — a computer plus peripherals such as a monitor, printer, external drive, docking station), computer software (§197(e)(3)(B) — off-the-shelf software), or internet access (broadband home service, mobile hotspot, on-campus wired connection). Second, the "primarily by the beneficiary" test — a facts-and-circumstances standard. A computer used by the student for coursework but shared with two younger siblings for their own homework will not survive the primary-use test in most cases; a computer the student uses in a dorm room or their own bedroom is more defensible. Third, the "during any of the years the beneficiary is enrolled" test — the equipment must be acquired during a year the beneficiary is enrolled, not before enrollment or after graduation.[6]

The primary-use documentation problem

The §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) primary-use test does not have a bright-line rule. IRS Publication 970 declines to define "primarily" numerically. Practitioners generally use a facts-and-circumstances test with a 51%+ threshold — if the student's use of the equipment is more than half of total household use, the equipment is primary-use. Documentation practices vary: some families retain a logbook of student vs household use for the first month of ownership as evidence; others rely on the physical location of the equipment (a laptop in the student's dorm room, not the family living room). In the event of an IRS examination, the burden of proof is on the taxpayer; retain contemporaneous evidence.

§529(e)(3)(B) room-and-board qualification and the cost-of-attendance ceiling

§529(e)(3)(B) is the second-largest qualified-expense category by dollar volume for most families and is subject to two tests that materially narrow it.[7]

Test 1: enrollment status. Under §529(e)(3)(B)(i), room and board qualifies only for a student enrolled at least half-time. Half-time is defined by the institution — most four-year colleges use 6 credit-hours per semester as the half-time threshold; graduate programs and community colleges vary. If a student drops below half-time in any given term (a medical leave of absence, a personal leave, an academic-standing-related reduction to under 6 hours), room and board for that term is disqualified regardless of any prior qualification. Enrollment certification from the institution's registrar's office documents the term-by-term status.

Test 2: cost-of-attendance ceiling. §529(e)(3)(B)(ii) caps qualifying room-and-board expenses at the "amount the eligible educational institution includes in its cost of attendance under section 472 of the Higher Education Act of 1965 (20 U.S.C. §1087ll) for the period of enrollment." The cost of attendance (COA) is published by each institution's financial-aid office and is broken into components: tuition and fees, books and supplies, room and board, transportation, and personal expenses. The room-and-board component is the ceiling for §529 qualification. If the institution's COA lists room and board at $14,500 per year for on-campus students, the beneficiary can claim up to $14,500 as qualified §529 room-and-board expenses even if actual off-campus rent and food total $16,200.[8]

The "less than half-time" gotcha

The §529(e)(3)(B)(i) half-time test is a periodic test — not a full-year test. A student who is full-time in fall semester but drops to 3 credit-hours in spring semester loses room-and-board qualification for spring semester only. Fall semester's qualified expenses are preserved. If the student then returns to at-least-half-time enrollment in a summer term, room and board for the summer term is again qualified. Practitioners drafting §529 distribution schedules should verify enrollment status term-by-term against the registrar's records.

The COA breakdown is institution-specific and updates annually

Each institution publishes its COA breakdown for the current academic year at the start of the year — typically August for the fall semester start. The room-and-board component varies materially: on-campus vs off-campus vs at-home-with-parent allowances often differ by $3,000-$8,000. Off-campus allowances for high-cost-of-living cities (Boston, New York, San Francisco, Los Angeles) often exceed $18,000 per academic year; institutions in lower-cost markets may publish $10,000-$12,000 allowances. Always source the current-year COA from the institution's financial-aid office website; prior-year numbers are not the ceiling.

§529(e)(3)(A)(iv) special-needs services

The fourth §529(e)(3)(A) sub-prong covers "expenses for special needs services in the case of a special needs beneficiary which are incurred in connection with such enrollment or attendance." The provision was added by EGTRRA 2001 §402(a)(3) and has remained textually stable since.[9] "Special needs beneficiary" is not defined at §529 — the Treasury has punted on final regulations — but the practitioner reading uses the §529A framework where available (a beneficiary with a disability under the ADA, or a beneficiary receiving Supplemental Security Income or Social Security Disability Insurance benefits). Qualifying special-needs services can include:

  • Tutoring, note-taking assistance, and specialized instructional aides
  • Adaptive equipment: voice-to-text software, screen readers, alternative keyboards, hearing-impaired classroom equipment
  • Sign-language interpreters and other communication-access services
  • Transportation services for a student unable to use standard institutional transportation
  • Reader services for a visually-impaired student

The connection-to-enrollment requirement is real. General medical care unrelated to the beneficiary's educational participation is NOT qualified. But services that enable the beneficiary to participate in the educational program — which would not be required for a non-disability student — are qualified.

The §529(e)(3)(A)(iv) special-needs channel also interacts with the §529A ABLE-account rollover discussed in the recent §529A piece. A special-needs beneficiary may use both: §529 for education-related expenses and §529A for other qualified disability expenses (housing, transportation, health-and-wellness services, employment support). The choice depends on the specific expense.

§529(c)(7) K-12 tuition — the TCJA baseline and the OBBBA §70443 doubling

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 §11032 added §529(c)(7), permitting up to $10,000 per year per beneficiary of §529 distributions for tuition at public, private, or religious elementary or secondary schools. TCJA §11032 was tuition-only — the category did not reach K-12 books, uniforms, transportation, or ancillary educational expenses. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) §70443 substantially expanded this in two dimensions, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.[10]

The OBBBA §70443 doubling and expansion is one of the largest §529 policy shifts since the 2001 EGTRRA revisions. It converts §529 from a college-only vehicle (with a modest K-12 tuition sidecar under TCJA) into a K-through-college education-finance vehicle with meaningful K-12 utility. For families with children in private K-12 institutions in high-tuition markets (Boston, New York, San Francisco), the $20,000/year cap now covers a substantial fraction of typical private-school tuition ($30,000-$55,000/year for elite day schools). For families using tutoring, test-preparation services, and educational-therapy providers, the expanded scope removes a friction that previously limited §529's utility to the tuition-line-item only.[11]

The related-party tutoring exclusion is deliberate

OBBBA §70443's tutoring category excludes payments to a tutor or class provider who is a §152(d)(2) related person to the student — parents, grandparents, siblings, aunts and uncles, in-laws, and other listed family members. This prevents a family from paying itself for tutoring using §529 dollars. The exclusion mirrors the §127 employer-plan §127(b)(3) 5% related-owner cap and the §125 cafeteria-plan related-party antiabuse structure. Bona fide arm's-length tutoring through Sylvan Learning, Kumon, a Wyzant-marketplace tutor, or an unaffiliated private tutor is fully qualified. Only same-family provider arrangements are excluded.

State-tax treatment of OBBBA §70443 categories

The OBBBA §70443 expansion is a federal-tax change. States that follow federal §529 treatment automatically extend qualification to the new categories. States that decouple from §529(c)(7) K-12 treatment (Illinois, New York, Michigan, and several others have historically not permitted state-tax-free treatment for K-12 §529 distributions even at the federal $10,000 tuition-only baseline) may not extend qualification to the OBBBA-expanded categories. As of September 2026, state responses to OBBBA §70443 are still evolving; practitioners should verify the specific state's position at the time of distribution.[12]

§529(c)(8) apprenticeship-program expenses

SECURE Act §302 (2019) added §529(c)(8), permitting §529 distributions for "fees, books, supplies, and equipment required for the participation of a designated beneficiary in an apprenticeship program registered and certified with the Secretary of Labor under §1 of the National Apprenticeship Act (29 U.S.C. §50)."[13] The provision opened §529 to a non-college educational pathway that had been outside the scope of §529(e)(3) — which is defined by reference to "eligible educational institution" under 20 U.S.C. §1088, primarily post-secondary degree-granting institutions.

The DOL Registered Apprenticeship database (accessible via apprenticeship.gov) lists over 27,000 active registered programs as of 2026, spanning construction trades (electrical, plumbing, HVAC, carpentry, welding), healthcare (medical assistant, licensed practical nurse, dental assistant), IT (software developer apprenticeships added under the American Apprenticeship Initiative), advanced manufacturing (CNC machinist, industrial maintenance technician), and service sectors (hospitality management, culinary arts). The apprenticeship must be listed in the DOL Registered Apprenticeship database — informal on-the-job training arrangements do not qualify.[14]

Practical §529(c)(8) qualified expenses:

  • Required program-participation fees charged by the DOL-registered sponsor
  • Textbooks and reference materials for the required classroom-instruction component (typically ~144-200 hours per year of related instruction)
  • Tools required by the specific trade (electrician's meter and tester kit, plumber's specialized wrenches, welder's specialty hood and safety gear)
  • Personal protective equipment (safety glasses, steel-toe boots, hard hat, welding gloves) where required by the program
  • Certification or license examination fees required by the program (Journeyman electrician exam, EPA 608 refrigeration certification, OSHA 10 or OSHA 30 training)

Tuition per se is typically not charged in registered apprenticeship programs — apprentices earn hourly wages and receive employer-paid related instruction. The §529(c)(8) qualified-expense category is therefore focused on the tools-and-materials sidecar, not on a tuition line item.

§529(c)(9) qualified education loan repayments

SECURE Act §302 (2019) also added §529(c)(9), permitting tax-free §529 distributions for principal or interest payments on any qualified education loan (as defined by §221(d)) of the designated beneficiary or a sibling of the designated beneficiary. The lifetime cap is $10,000 per individual — $10,000 for the beneficiary and $10,000 for each sibling — regardless of whether the payments are made in one year or across multiple years.[15]

The sibling extension is administratively meaningful. An over-funded §529 account with the older sibling as designated beneficiary can pay down a younger sibling's student loans up to $10,000 without a §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change. The §529(c)(9) sibling extension effectively creates a $10,000 per-sibling loan-payoff bucket without triggering the §529(c)(3)(E) 15-year clock restart discussed in the recent §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary-change piece. For a family with three children, that is $30,000 of tax-free student-loan repayment capacity ($10,000 beneficiary + $10,000 × 2 siblings) from a single §529 account.[15]

The §221 interaction: no double dip

Interest paid with tax-free §529 distributions cannot also be deducted under §221. §221(e)(2) excludes from the §221 student-loan-interest deduction any interest paid with "tax-free distributions from qualified tuition programs." If a $2,500 interest payment on the beneficiary's loans is made partly from §529 distributions ($1,500) and partly from the beneficiary's own after-tax earnings ($1,000), only the $1,000 portion is potentially deductible under §221; the $1,500 §529-paid portion is not.[16]

The anti-double-benefit coordination: §25A, §117, §127, and §221

The §529(e)(3) qualified higher education expenses figure is a gross number. §529(c)(3)(B)(iv) and (v) require reducing that gross number by amounts that received tax-free treatment through other channels — because the same dollar cannot be tax-free twice. This is the load-bearing coordination step that determines the actual §529-qualified base for a given year.[17]

The AOTC-first sequencing rule

For most families with an undergraduate student, the American Opportunity Credit under §25A(i) is the highest-value education tax benefit: 100% of the first $2,000 of qualified expenses plus 25% of the next $2,000, up to a $2,500 maximum credit per eligible student per year, refundable up to 40%. The AOTC is available for the first four post-secondary years and requires the student to be enrolled at least half-time in a program leading to a degree or credential. It phases out at $80,000-$90,000 modified AGI (single) or $160,000-$180,000 (joint) for 2026.[18]

Practitioner sequencing for a family with §529 assets and AOTC eligibility: (a) claim the AOTC by allocating $4,000 of qualified tuition and required fees to Form 8863, (b) use §529 distributions to cover the balance of qualified expenses beyond the $4,000 AOTC-claimed amount, (c) verify that non-AOTC expenses cover at least the §529 distribution amount, (d) document the allocation on the beneficiary's or account owner's own worksheet since the plan administrator does not do this. Reversing the sequence — using §529 to cover the $4,000 first and then trying to claim AOTC — leaves the AOTC unclaimed because the underlying $4,000 was tax-free §529.[18]

Lifetime Learning Credit and graduate-student §529 planning

The Lifetime Learning Credit under §25A(c) is a 20% credit on up to $10,000 of qualified tuition and required fees, capped at $2,000 per return per year. Unlike AOTC, LLC is available for graduate-degree years, part-time enrollment, and job-related coursework at eligible institutions. For graduate students using §529 assets, LLC-first sequencing typically means allocating $10,000 of tuition to LLC claim (yielding $2,000 credit) and using §529 for the balance. The $10,000 subtracted from §529-qualified expenses is significant — for a graduate student with $22,000 of annual tuition, only $12,000 remains as §529-qualified. Careful sequencing across the LLC's $10,000 credit base and §529 distributions materially reduces the risk of a non-qualified excess distribution.

Three worked case studies

Case 1: Maya (undergraduate, on-campus year 1, off-campus year 2, computer purchase)

Maya, age 18, enrolls at a state flagship university as a full-time undergraduate for fall 2026. Her §529 account (opened at birth by her parents) has a $95,000 balance with $30,000 basis, giving an earnings ratio of 68.4%. Fall 2026 semester bill: $8,000 tuition (in-state), $600 required activity/tech fee, $1,200 required textbooks and lab manuals per syllabus, $200 optional parking permit (not qualified), $5,200 on-campus dorm and required base meal plan through the institution's dining services. Maya also purchases a $1,150 laptop for coursework used primarily in her dorm room, a $185 productivity software subscription, and pays her share of a home broadband service used during winter break at $95/month × 1 month = $95 (allocated as primary-use during the break month).

Qualified §529 expenses for fall 2026: tuition $8,000 + required fees $600 + textbooks $1,200 + on-campus room and board $5,200 + laptop $1,150 + software $185 + internet $95 = $16,430. Not qualified: $200 parking permit. Maya's parents receive Form 1098-T showing $8,000 tuition + $600 required fees = $8,600 in Box 1. Maya's parents claim the AOTC by allocating $4,000 of the tuition to Form 8863; the $4,000 AOTC-claimed amount reduces §529-qualified expenses to $16,430 − $4,000 = $12,430. Fall §529 distribution taken from Maya's account: $12,430 (matched to the qualified base). No taxable excess; Form 1099-Q issued to Maya (as recipient) reports gross $12,430, earnings portion $8,502 (= $12,430 × 68.4%), which is not includable because it fully offsets qualified expenses.

Spring 2027 shift to off-campus: Maya moves off-campus for spring semester into her own rented apartment near campus. Institution's published cost-of-attendance for spring 2027 lists off-campus room-and-board at $6,800. Maya's actual rent is $850/month × 5 months = $4,250, plus food $400/month × 5 months = $2,000, total actual $6,250 (under the $6,800 COA ceiling; the actual $6,250 is qualified). Semester bill: $8,000 tuition + $600 required fees + $1,100 required books. Total qualified spring §529: $8,000 + $600 + $1,100 + $6,250 = $15,950. Less $4,000 AOTC (spring semester's continuing $2,500 AOTC allocation) → §529 base of $11,950. Spring §529 distribution: $11,950 matched. Full-year §529 draw: $24,380; balance remaining after growth: ~$78,000 for Maya's continuing years.

Case 2: Diego (graduate student, partial scholarship, LLC-first coordination)

Diego, age 25, enrolls in a master's program at a private university. Annual tuition: $32,000. Required fees: $1,800. Books: $850. Off-campus rent + food actual: $18,500; institution's COA off-campus room-and-board allowance: $17,200 (binding ceiling). Diego receives a $12,000 tax-free merit scholarship (Form 1098-T Box 5) that the institution restricts to tuition. Diego works part-time and has $22,000 of earned income. His §529 account (transferred from his younger brother's account via §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change in 2024) has a $110,000 balance with $45,000 basis, earnings ratio 59.1%.

Qualified §529 expenses computation for the year: gross qualified = tuition $32,000 + required fees $1,800 + books $850 + off-campus room-and-board $17,200 (capped at COA) = $51,850. Less: §117 tax-free scholarship $12,000 (restricted to tuition) → $39,850. Less: LLC first sequencing — Diego claims the Lifetime Learning Credit on the first $10,000 of tuition/required fees, yielding $2,000 credit; the $10,000 must be subtracted from §529-qualified base → $29,850. Final §529-qualified base: $29,850.

§529 distribution executed: Diego draws $29,850 for the year, matched to the qualified base. Form 1099-Q issued to Diego reports gross $29,850, earnings portion $17,641 (= $29,850 × 59.1%), all offset by qualified expenses. No taxable excess. LLC $2,000 credit claimed on Diego's Form 8863. §221 student-loan interest deduction unavailable this year (no student-loan interest paid). Diego coordinates his §529 timing by taking the distribution in the same calendar year as the qualified expenses; a distribution taken in January 2027 for December 2026 expenses would fail the same-year matching rule and be a non-qualified distribution.[19]

Case 3: Priya (§529(c)(7) K-12 OBBBA expansion for special-needs elementary student)

Priya, age 8, has a diagnosed learning disability. Her grandparents' §529 for her benefit has a $65,000 balance with $25,000 basis (earnings ratio 61.5%). For tax year 2026, the family incurs the following K-12 expenses: $12,000 tuition at a private religious elementary school, $2,500 for after-school tutoring at a Wyzant-marketplace tutor (arm's-length, not a §152(d)(2) related party), $1,800 for educational-therapy sessions with a licensed speech-language pathologist for reading intervention, $650 for online educational materials and curriculum-supplementation subscriptions, $420 for standardized-test-preparation coursework for an upcoming ISEE examination for private secondary-school admissions.

Pre-OBBBA (TCJA baseline) qualified §529(c)(7) expenses: only the $10,000 tuition portion up to the $10,000 K-12 tuition-only cap. All other categories (tutoring, therapy, materials, test prep) were NOT qualified under the TCJA-baseline rules. Available §529-qualified: $10,000.

Post-OBBBA §70443 qualified §529(c)(7) expenses (effective tax year 2026): gross K-12 qualified = tuition $12,000 + tutoring $2,500 + educational therapy $1,800 + online materials $650 + test prep $420 = $17,370. Under the OBBBA-doubled $20,000 K-12 cap, the full $17,370 is qualified. §529 distribution executed: $17,370, matched to qualified base. Form 1099-Q reports gross $17,370, earnings portion $10,683, all offset. No taxable excess. The family also claims the child-and-dependent care credit and any state-level education credits where available; the §529 K-12 distribution does not affect those other credits.

The OBBBA §70443 expansion converts this fact pattern from a "$10,000 qualified plus $7,370 hypothetically non-qualified" to "$17,370 fully qualified within a $20,000 cap" — a $7,370 shift in the family's §529 qualified base for one year. Compounded across a nine-year elementary-through-middle-school runway (grades 4-12), the OBBBA expansion produces meaningful additional §529 utilization capacity for special-needs families.

The OBBBA §70443 expansion is state-dependent

State-level treatment of the OBBBA-expanded §529(c)(7) categories is still evolving as of September 2026. States that follow federal §529 treatment (Pennsylvania, Ohio, Georgia, and many others) extend the tax-free treatment to the new categories automatically. States that decoupled from §529(c)(7) K-12 treatment historically (Illinois, New York, Michigan, California) may not extend the state-tax-free treatment to any K-12 category — including the OBBBA-expanded ones. A family in a decoupled state can still use the §529(c)(7) OBBBA expansion for federal tax-free treatment but may owe state income tax on the earnings portion of K-12 distributions. Always verify state treatment before executing large K-12 §529 draws.[12]

Seven recurring §529(c)(3)(B) mistakes

  1. Treating every fee on the institution's bill as qualified. Only fees required as a condition of enrollment or attendance are qualified. Parking, optional health-insurance premiums, meal-plan upgrades beyond the required base plan, recreational-facility premium tiers, and pre-enrollment or post-graduation charges are not qualified — even when they appear on the same tuition bill. The institution's cost-of-attendance breakdown separates required from optional; use it.[5]
  2. Ignoring the room-and-board cost-of-attendance ceiling. §529(e)(3)(B)(ii) caps qualifying room-and-board at the institution's published COA number. Off-campus students with actual rent above the COA can only claim up to the COA. Practitioners routinely see families claim full off-campus rent, which fails the §529(e)(3)(B) ceiling and creates non-qualified excess.[8]
  3. Failing the §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) primary-use test on computers. A family laptop shared among siblings does not survive the primary-use test. Retain contemporaneous documentation showing the equipment is primarily the student's — physical location, sole-user practice, or usage-log evidence.[6]
  4. Skipping the AOTC-first sequencing. The AOTC ($2,500 credit on $4,000 of tuition/required fees) is a bigger dollar-for-dollar benefit than tax-free §529 distributions on the same dollars. Sequence: claim AOTC first, then use §529 for the residual qualified expenses. Reversing the sequence forfeits the credit.[18]
  5. Missing the same-year matching requirement. §529 distributions taken in year N for expenses paid in year N+1 (or vice versa) are treated as non-qualified. The IRS enforces same-calendar-year matching. Time distributions to align with the expense payment date; don't front-load or back-load.[4]
  6. Overlooking the §529(c)(9) $10,000 sibling extension. An over-funded §529 for one child can pay down each other child's student loans up to $10,000 without a §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change. Families miss this because plan administrators do not surface the sibling category — the recipient must know to ask.[15]
  7. Assuming OBBBA §70443 K-12 expansion is state-tax-neutral. The federal expansion is real, but state responses are still evolving. Some states may not extend state-tax-free treatment to the OBBBA-expanded K-12 categories. Always verify state treatment before large K-12 draws.[12]

Eight-item §529 qualified-distribution action checklist

  1. Retain the institution's Form 1098-T reporting tuition and required fees paid (Box 1) and scholarships/grants received (Box 5). This is the primary source document for the §529(e)(3)(A)(i) and §117 numbers.
  2. Pull the current-year cost-of-attendance breakdown from the institution's financial-aid office website. Use the room-and-board component as the §529(e)(3)(B)(ii) ceiling for off-campus students and as documentation for on-campus students.
  3. Itemize book, supply, and equipment purchases with syllabus documentation where available. Retain receipts and photograph the syllabus's required-materials list.
  4. Document the §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) primary-use test for computer, peripheral, software, and internet purchases. Contemporaneous evidence (location of equipment, sole-user practice) is stronger than reconstructed evidence later.
  5. Verify enrollment status term-by-term against the institution's registrar records for §529(e)(3)(B)(i) half-time compliance. A term with below-half-time enrollment disqualifies room-and-board for that term.
  6. Run the anti-double-benefit coordination worksheet. Subtract tax-free scholarships (§117), employer §127 assistance, AOTC-claimed $4,000, LLC-claimed $10,000, and any other tax-free educational assistance from gross §529(e)(3) qualified expenses before matching the distribution.
  7. Time §529 distributions to the same calendar year as the expenses. Draw for fall-semester expenses in fall of the same tax year; draw for spring-semester expenses in the calendar year when they are paid. The IRS enforces same-year matching under §529(c)(3)(A).
  8. Retain the Form 1099-Q from the §529 plan administrator and reconcile it against your qualified-expense worksheet. Any excess distribution is a §529(c)(6) non-qualified event and should be reported on Schedule 1 of the beneficiary's or account owner's return.

Frequently asked questions

What does IRC §529(c)(3)(B) require for a §529 distribution to be tax-free?

§529(c)(3)(B) is the includability rule mirror of §529(c)(3)(A). Where the distribution equals or is less than the beneficiary's qualified higher education expenses, no earnings is includable. Where the distribution exceeds those expenses, a proportionate earnings portion is includable in the distributee's gross income under §72's basis-and-earnings ratio, and — unless a §529(c)(6) exception applies — the earnings portion also carries a 10% additional tax. Qualified expenses are defined at §529(e)(3) plus §529(c)(7) K-12, §529(c)(8) apprenticeship, and §529(c)(9) education-loan repayment (with a $10,000 lifetime cap).

What counts as tuition and required fees under §529(e)(3)(A)(i)?

§529(e)(3)(A)(i) covers tuition, fees, books, supplies, and equipment required for enrollment or attendance. "Required" is the load-bearing word. Charges the institution requires every enrolled student to pay (tuition, required activity fees, required tech fees) are qualified. Optional charges (parking, optional health insurance, meal-plan upgrades, recreational-facility premium tiers) are not — even when they appear on the same bill. The institution's cost-of-attendance breakdown is the safest documentation.

How does §529(e)(3)(B) limit room-and-board qualification?

Two tests: at-least-half-time enrollment (§529(e)(3)(B)(i)) and cap at the institution's cost-of-attendance room-and-board component (§529(e)(3)(B)(ii)). Off-campus rent and food are qualified up to the COA ceiling; on-campus is qualified up to the actual institutional charge. Below-half-time enrollment in any term disqualifies room-and-board for that term.

Are computers, printers, software, and internet service qualified expenses?

Yes. §529(e)(3)(A)(iii) (added by PATH Act 2015 §302, permanent) covers computer and peripheral equipment, computer software (off-the-shelf; not entertainment), and internet access, provided the property or service is used primarily by the beneficiary during a year the beneficiary is enrolled. The primary-use test is facts-and-circumstances; documentation matters.

What did OBBBA §70443 change about K-12 tuition qualification?

OBBBA §70443 (signed July 4, 2025, effective tax year 2026) doubled the §529(c)(7) K-12 annual cap from $10,000 to $20,000 per beneficiary and expanded eligible K-12 expenses from tuition-only to include curriculum and instructional materials, online educational materials, tutoring by unrelated providers, standardized-test-preparation fees, dual-enrollment fees, and educational therapies for students with disabilities. Family-member tutoring is excluded via a §152(d)(2) related-party test.

How does the §25A anti-double-benefit rule reduce qualified §529 expenses?

§25A(g)(2) requires that qualified higher education expenses paid with tax-free §529 earnings cannot also be counted toward the American Opportunity Credit or Lifetime Learning Credit. Subtract from §529-qualified expenses: tax-free §117 scholarships, employer §127 educational assistance (up to $5,250), $4,000 used for maximum AOTC claim, $10,000 used for maximum LLC claim, and any other tax-free educational assistance. Sequence AOTC/LLC-first for maximum household benefit.

How does §529(c)(9) education-loan repayment work?

SECURE Act §302 added §529(c)(9), permitting tax-free §529 distributions for principal or interest on any §221(d) qualified education loan of the designated beneficiary or a sibling — with a $10,000 lifetime cap per individual. The sibling extension provides a $10,000-per-sibling loan-payoff bucket without a §529(c)(3)(C)(ii) beneficiary change. Interest paid with §529 dollars is not §221-deductible.

What is the §529(c)(8) apprenticeship-program qualification?

SECURE Act §302 added §529(c)(8), covering fees, books, supplies, and equipment required for participation in a DOL Registered Apprenticeship program under the National Apprenticeship Act. Over 27,000 registered programs qualify as of 2026 across construction, healthcare, IT, and manufacturing. Informal on-the-job training does not qualify — the program must be listed in the DOL Registered Apprenticeship database.

Can §529 funds pay for study-abroad programs?

Yes, provided the foreign institution is on the Department of Education's FAFSA-eligible-institution list. If the study-abroad program is administered through a U.S. institution, the standard §529(e)(3) rules apply. If the student directly enrolls at a foreign institution, verify FAFSA eligibility via studentaid.gov. Travel expenses to reach the study-abroad site are NOT qualified expenses.

What documentation should accompany a §529(c)(3)(B) qualified-expense distribution?

Form 1099-Q from the §529 plan administrator, Form 1098-T from the institution, itemized receipts for books/supplies/equipment/computers/software/internet with primary-use documentation, the institution's cost-of-attendance breakdown for room-and-board substantiation, term-by-term enrollment certification for the half-time test, scholarship and §25A/§127 credit documentation for anti-double-benefit coordination, and a same-tax-year alignment worksheet showing distribution and expense timing align.

IRC §529(c)(3)(B) qualified-expense distributions require careful coordination with the §25A American Opportunity and Lifetime Learning Credits, §117 tax-free scholarships, §127 employer-provided educational assistance, and §221 student-loan-interest deduction — the §529 plan administrator does not perform this coordination, and errors flow through Form 1099-Q to the distributee's return as taxable earnings plus a 10% §529(c)(6) additional tax. The OBBBA §70443 K-12 expansion is federal-tax-law; state-level treatment of the expanded categories is still evolving as of September 2026 and varies materially across §529-decoupled states. Room-and-board qualification is capped at the institution's cost-of-attendance ceiling under §529(e)(3)(B)(ii), not at the family's actual expenses. Computer, peripheral, software, and internet purchases require primary-use documentation under §529(e)(3)(A)(iii). Consult a state-licensed tax advisor or CPA experienced in §529 mechanics before executing any material §529 distribution, and reconcile the plan administrator's Form 1099-Q against your qualified-expense worksheet on the beneficiary's or account owner's return.
